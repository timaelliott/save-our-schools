Portland Public Schools · Rightsizing 2026

The record vs. the guiding principles

PPS says its school-closure plan is built on four guiding principles. This page sets what district leaders have said since early 2025 against the district’s own data, documents and unanswered Board questions. Every quote has a video clip at the exact moment; every figure links to the PPS source.

In May 2026 the Board adopted four guiding principles for rightsizing (Resolution 7307). In October PPS released two scenarios closing 11 or 14 schools. The findings below are grouped by the principle each one tests.

Quotes are verbatim from PPS’s YouTube auto-captions; obvious caption errors are fixed in [brackets]. Press ▶ Play clip to watch the moment in place. Demographic and distance figures are PPS’s, from each school’s family guide.

Guiding principle 1

Well-Resourced Schools

“Sustainable, well-resourced schools that provide consistent access to strong academic programming across PPS.” PPS, Nov 4, 2026

  1. The headline equity number counts students in bigger buildings, not resources.
  2. The size thresholds behind the closures are called a stopgap by the staff who set them.
  3. The savings story changes with the audience, and the net number still does not exist.
  4. Families leaving the district: a reason to stop a move in 2025, accepted in 2026, and not modeled.

#The headline equity number counts students in bigger buildings, not resources.

PPS says every focal student group “gains” under both scenarios. The measure is the share of students in schools above the size threshold, so it rises by design when small schools close. PPS has not said what any student would gain.

  • A “sustainable” school is one above the enrollment threshold, 360 students for K-5. PPS rightsizing presentation, Oct 6, 2026, p.10
  • PPS’s own written answer: “there is no data supporting that smaller schools have higher test scores. Test scores are better predicted by socio-economic status than school size.” PPS Board Q&A, Oct 1, 2026, p.27
  • “Class size capping is not a part of the rightsizing conversation.” PPS Board Q&A, Oct 1, 2026, p.33
  • Board questions “What specific resources will students gain in the receiving school?” and “Will class sizes get larger, smaller, or remain the same?” were still marked “In process” on Oct 30. PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.37 PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.38

#The size thresholds behind the closures are called a stopgap by the staff who set them.

Every closure starts from a size floor: 360 students for K-5, 500 for middle schools. Senior staff describe those numbers as temporary and possibly wrong.

  • The 360 and 500 floors come from the state’s Quality Education Model prototype school; the K-8 floor reuses the middle-school number. PPS rightsizing memo, Aug 4, 2026, p.2
  • The Board-commissioned SEGC equity audit proposed floors of about 450, 600 and 650. SEGC Equity Audit report, Aug 2026, p.146

#The savings story changes with the audience, and the net number still does not exist.

Leaders have said closures are “not a cost-saving strategy,” then cited $14 million in savings, while a Board member’s request for net savings stayed unanswered.

  • Ten days later, Director Engelsman’s question “What is the actual net savings to closing Sellwood? What are the net savings to closing any school?” was still “In process.” PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.35
  • Staff concede closed buildings keep costing money (utilities, security, insurance, repairs) and that “financial estimates will need to account for ongoing and one-time costs when calculating net savings.” PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.39
  • The SEGC audit warns that districts “often overestimate the savings produced by school closures and underestimate the costs.” SEGC Equity Audit report, Aug 2026, p.147

#Families leaving the district: a reason to stop a move in 2025, accepted in 2026, and not modeled.

Every family that leaves takes state funding with it. PPS has no grade 1–12 data on how many families a closure drives out, and the last Southeast boundary process saw faster enrollment loss.

  • SEGC audit: SEGC elementary enrollment fell “on average, by 20%” from 2020–21 to 2024–25, “compared to 14% among elementary schools outside SEGC.” SEGC Equity Audit report, Aug 2026, p.6
Guiding principle 2

Safe and Healthy Learning Environments

“Aligning school facilities and learning environments to support student safety, wellness, and long-term sustainability.” PPS, Nov 4, 2026

  1. Earthquake money went to schools now proposed to close. High-risk receiving schools have no retrofit plan.
  2. Longer trips, and no transportation plan.

#Earthquake money went to schools now proposed to close. High-risk receiving schools have no retrofit plan.

Board members said seismic risk should guide which buildings stay open, and warned about schools serving students of color. The scenarios close recently retrofitted schools and send students to high-risk buildings with no retrofit planned.

  • Closure-list schools with seismic money: a full campus retrofit at Rose City Park (closes in Scenario A) and a targeted retrofit at Beach (closes in both). PPS seismic presentation, Mar 2026, p.9
  • Woodmere (closes in both): roof seismic and masonry work, construction summer 2026. PPS seismic presentation, Mar 2026, p.22
  • Marysville (closes in both): a seismic upgrade budgeted at about $6.75 million for design and construction. 2020 bond seismic status report, Apr 2025, p.1
  • Receiving schools with high seismic-risk scores and no retrofit planned include Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. (score 9.0; 51.1% poverty, 63.0% underserved racial groups) PPS seismic risk assessment, Sept 2025, p.213 PPS family guide: Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., p.4, Brentwood MS (9.0; 57.5% poverty) PPS seismic risk assessment, Sept 2025, p.214 PPS family guide: Brentwood, p.4, Ockley Green (9.1) PPS seismic risk assessment, Sept 2025, p.229 and Rieke (9.6) PPS seismic risk assessment, Sept 2025, p.233.

#Longer trips, and no transportation plan.

The schools proposed to close are the ones children can walk to (see Educational Options). PPS has published no bus routes, ride times or transportation costs.

  • Harriet Tubman MS (50.5% students from underserved racial groups): students living within a mile fall from 23.7% to 12.5% under both scenarios. PPS family guide: Harriet Tubman MS, p.4
  • Transportation and ride times “will require further implementation planning.” Scenario release memo, Oct 5, 2026, p.2
  • A Board question on “transportation costs, travel-time burdens, and safety considerations” was still “In process” on Oct 30. PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.38
  • The SEGC audit found “the burden of travel falling hardest on families with the least means.” SEGC Equity Audit report, Aug 2026, p.18
Guiding principle 3

Educational Options

“Maintaining a strong network of neighborhood schools (while providing a balance of educational options) that are sustainable and well-resourced.” PPS, Nov 4, 2026

  1. The schools proposed to close are the district’s most walkable.
  2. Focus options kept, neighborhood schools closed, and no reason given for Sunnyside.
  3. “We have to be open” became a mandate to close, with the number set before the criteria.

#The schools proposed to close are the district’s most walkable.

Families ranked “Strong Neighborhood Schools” first among the four principles. The closure list is concentrated in the schools where most students live within a mile.

#Focus options kept, neighborhood schools closed, and no reason given for Sunnyside.

PPS’s memo said no focus-option school would close. Sunnyside’s focus option ends, Buckman (an arts focus school) closes, and staff could not say why Sunnyside was picked.

  • Scenario memo: “No alternative or focus option school closure is proposed.” Scenario release memo, Oct 5, 2026, p.2
  • Staff’s own summary of the surveys: “Surveys say that the proposed scenarios over-emphasize choice programs.” PPS rightsizing presentation, Nov 4, 2026, p.13

#“We have to be open” became a mandate to close, with the number set before the criteria.

In 2025 the Superintendent said the process might close no schools. By April 2026 the Board required closures, and the count rose after Board members called the first range too low.

  • Resolution 7296 (Apr 14, 2026) says rightsizing “will include school and program closures, consolidations, or boundary changes.” A motion to change “will” to “may” failed 2–5. Board minutes, Apr 14, 2026, p.4
  • On Sept 1, 2026 the Superintendent said she first offered 5 to 12 closures and Board members told her it was too low; the scenarios proposed 11 to 14. Board meeting, Sept 1, 2026, 2:11:46 District-wide scenarios, Oct 4, 2026, p.2
Guiding principle 4

PPS Equity Lens

“Using the PPS Equity Lens to guide decision-making and center students most impacted by inequities.” PPS, Nov 4, 2026

  1. Closures fall hardest on students with disabilities.
  2. Title I schools: staff name the pattern, then repeat it.
  3. The schools PPS promised to stabilize after the last boundary process are closing.
  4. The required equity analysis was not available while the public weighed in, and many affected families were not reached.
  5. Praised for results one hour, on the closure list the next.
  6. One size floor for every school, and every school in, except high schools.

#Closures fall hardest on students with disabilities.

Students receiving special education are one of PPS’s focal groups. The schools proposed to close have a noticeably higher share of them than the rest of the district.

  • A Board question on how PPS will protect “student belonging, cultural identity, and school-community relationships, especially for racially minoritized students” was still “In process” on Oct 30. PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.38

#Title I schools: staff name the pattern, then repeat it.

Six Title I schools close in both scenarios, and receiving schools lose Title I status. A Board member’s question about why was still unanswered on Oct 30.

  • Title I schools closing in both scenarios: Beach, Buckman, James John, Marysville, Peninsula and Woodmere. In Scenario B that is 6 of 11 closures (55%), against 35 of 82 schools district-wide (43%). PPS enrollment & utilization table, Apr 2026, p.1
  • Woodmere: 59.0% poverty, 25.1% multilingual, 30.4% special education. PPS family guide: Woodmere, p.4 Marysville: 58.3% poverty. PPS family guide: Marysville, p.4 James John: 54.5% poverty, 63.8% underserved racial groups. PPS family guide: James John, p.4
  • Brentwood MS goes from 57.5% poverty and Title I to 32.1% (A) or 30.6% (B) and no Title I. PPS family guide: Brentwood, p.4 Markham loses Title I under Scenario A. PPS family guide: Markham, p.4
  • “Why do we have a list that has close to 50% Title I schools closing?” (Director Engelsman): “In process.” PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.42
  • The KPI “Number of Title I and CSI/TSI schools impacted” was listed in the framework but never reported. PPS decision framework, Sept 15, 2026, p.20

#The schools PPS promised to stabilize after the last boundary process are closing.

The 2021 Southeast Guiding Coalition (SEGC) process moved boundaries and programs across Southeast Portland. Leaders said lessons were learned and hard-hit communities would be protected. Marysville, Lewis and Creston, all SEGC-affected, now close.

  • Marysville closes in both scenarios. PPS enrollment & utilization table, Apr 2026, p.1 It received students displaced by SEGC. SEGC school-by-school analysis, Aug 2026, p.39
  • Lewis, whose projection SEGC cut from 358 to 281, closes in Scenario A. SEGC school-by-school analysis, Aug 2026, p.66 Creston, also part of SEGC SEGC Equity Audit report, Aug 2026, p.87, closes in both. District-wide scenarios, Oct 4, 2026, p.2
  • Staff on the Wells-area routing: “the quantitative model optimizes for building utilization rather than non-measurable social networks.” PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.36
  • The audit recommended tracking cumulative burden on communities. That measure is not among the KPIs. PPS rightsizing presentation, Aug 4, 2026, p.12

#The required equity analysis was not available while the public weighed in, and many affected families were not reached.

The Board required an equity impact analysis for each scenario. Staff said it would arrive with the November recommendation, after the Oct 6–28 engagement window.

  • Resolution 7307 requires a “standardized equity impact analysis” for each scenario. Resolution 7307 (guiding principles), May 26, 2026, p.2 The September framework promised “An equity analysis review attached to every scenario.” PPS decision framework, Sept 15, 2026, p.7
  • The resolution names Black and Native students as focal groups; the KPIs merge them into “historically underserved by race.” Resolution 7307 (guiding principles), May 26, 2026, p.2 PPS Board Q&A, Oct 1, 2026, p.33
  • Survey respondents were 64% White and 74% PPS parents or guardians. PPS rightsizing presentation, Nov 4, 2026, p.9

#Praised for results one hour, on the closure list the next.

At the Nov 4 meeting, PPS highlighted strong state test results for focal students at several schools that are proposed to close.

  • Sabin, Marysville and Maplewood close in both scenarios. District-wide scenarios, Oct 4, 2026, p.2

#One size floor for every school, and every school in, except high schools.

Leaders warned that treating schools the same can be inequitable, and that exempting some schools is inequitable. The plan applies one 360-student floor to every elementary school, and high schools are off the table.

  • The 360 floor comes from the state’s prototype school and applies citywide. PPS rightsizing memo, Aug 4, 2026, p.2

For balance

What PPS says in response

District leaders argue that doing nothing is worse for the same students.

The status quo also hurts focal students

Without changes, staff project that the share of K-5 Title I schools meeting the size threshold falls “from 18% to 9%” and Title I middle schools fall to 0% by 2031-32 Board meeting, Sept 15, 2026, 3:00:14. The $65 million budget gap means cuts somewhere.

  • Title I money stays in the district: it is redirected “to other PPS schools that do meet the highest concentrations of poverty.” PPS Board Q&A, Oct 30, 2026, p.42
  • Staff say they are revising DLI, focus-option and transportation assumptions before the recommendation. PPS rightsizing presentation, Nov 4, 2026, p.17
  • On poverty and race overall, the closure list roughly mirrors the district: average poverty is 32.2% (A) and 35.1% (B) at closing schools against about 32–33% elsewhere. The sharper gaps are in special education, walkability and Title I status at receiving schools.

About this page

All sources are public PPS records: Board meeting videos, BoardBook agenda documents and Board Q&A, and the school family guides PPS published in October 2026. Because PPS edits and removes posted documents, every source is also archived with its retrieval date in the save-our-schools research archive, which has the full transcripts, data tables and method notes.

Averages across schools are simple averages of the percentages printed in each school’s family guide (“No changes” column). Speakers are named from the video; meeting captions do not label speakers.
