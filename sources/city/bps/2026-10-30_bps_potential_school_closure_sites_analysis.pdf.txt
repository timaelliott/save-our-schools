This memo provides basic information and context for the 14 school sites being considered for
closure by Portland Public Schools (PPS). The first portion of the memo gives high level
information and considerations followed by profiles for each of the school sites. The sites are
distributed evenly throughout the PPS district, and therefore throughout the city. There are
schools in three of the four City Council districts, with no schools slated for closure in District 1,
which is by-and-large in a different school district. Five sites are in District 2, six in District 3,
and three in District 4. The analysis includes:

      •   current zoning;
      •   a description of the surrounding area, roughly defined as within a half mile of the
          school;
      •   transit access and bicycle and pedestrian infrastructure;
      •   a description of potential interim or longer-term uses; and
      •   potential zoning changes that would be needed to facilitate interim or long-term reuse.

Because the sites are dispersed throughout the city, they are in a variety of neighborhoods,
with different contexts, levels of transit access, and potential for interim or longer-term reuse.

School Enrollment Report from the Oregon Department of Education shows a 7,797 reduction
in students statewide from the prior year. School districts in other parts of the State including
Tigard-Tualatin, Salem-Keizer, Newberg, Corvallis, Bethel and Beaverton have closed and
consolidated schools or are actively considering doing so. The decline continues a trend
started before the COVID 19 pandemic that has only accelerated since. The following table

Historic Structures
All the school structures are potentially eligible for historic protections and national register of
historic places listing, with the newest building, Stephenson Elementary built in 1965. Irvington
Elementary is already a contributing structure in a historic district. In 2009 PPS conducted a


City of Portland Bureau of Planning and Sustainability: School sites analysis – Preliminary draft                               Page 3 of 35
Historic Building Assessment that evaluated the potential historic significance of the buildings
in their portfolio, including an evaluation of historic integrity and association with educational
and architectural development of Portland and its neighborhoods.

Updating this evaluation for the 14 potential schools to be closed and, where appropriate
pursuing national register of historic places listing, could provide access to greater regulatory
flexibility and financial tools like historic tax credits and grants to facilitate reuse. Historic
listing would also provide some assurances to Portlanders that the elementary schools they
attended could be retained even if they are used for uses other than a school. This also can
provide some assurances that the some notable features of the built environment can be
retained and enlivened with housing and new uses that continue to support surrounding
neighborhoods.


Interim Uses, Commercial, and Housing
It may be desirable for PPS or some other government entity to retain ownership of the closed
school sites. Over the long-term demographic trends may shift, birthrates could go up or
families could migrate to the city. There may be new demand for schools and having the sites,
or even just the school buildings available for use is a prudent hedge against future needs.
Interim uses that retain the schools’ structures fulfill this desire and can be lower cost. These
interim uses include other educational uses, daycares, community centers, senior centers, art
studios, exhibit spaces, and museums. While these uses present a low barrier to conversion,
they do not typically generate a lot of revenue and may require subsidies or outright
ownership and management by a government entity.

Commercial uses present an opportunity to generate more revenue, while preserving the
structure. Some of these uses can leverage existing facilities like commercial kitchens for
restaurants, sports and recreational facilities (gymnasiums, locker rooms etc.) for commercial
gyms, or auditoriums for performance venues. Others may require more extensive conversion
work with hotels, office suites, business incubators, or workshops presenting a range of
potential benefits and challenges depending on the school building and use. Commercial uses
may not make sense on every site and feasibility, and market studies should be conducted to
determine which sites present the most opportunity. The costs of conversion and the ability to
easily convert back to school use, if necessary, should also be weighed.

Some of the school structures may be appropriate for conversion to housing. This option has
the potential to retain the existing structure while contributing to housing supply for the city.
There are known models for this approach in other cities in the country and in contrast to

City of Portland Bureau of Planning and Sustainability: School sites analysis – Preliminary draft   Page 4 of 35
commercial uses, which usually require purpose-built space, housing conversion has a more
formulaic approach. An architect with experience managing such a conversion can evaluate a
structure and flag potential issues and costs. Conversion to housing does remove potential to
use the structure for educational purposes in the future.

As stated above, the school sites are relatively large and, in many cases, represent the only
large site development potential in the neighborhood. Building new housing on a portion of
the site allows the school structure to remain, in a strong market could provide cross-subsidies
for less lucrative interim uses in the school structure, keeps the school structure for potential
school use in the future, and contributes to the city’s housing supply. A balance between
development and maintaining the open space asset that many of these sites provide would
need to be struck. In most cases a rezoning would be needed to facilitate development at an
appropriate density.


Seismic
Given the age of the school structures and the Oregon Building Code requirements for seismic
upgrades the status of seismic improvements of each of the structures is a factor in their
viability for reuse. Records indicate that several of the school structures have had seismic
upgrades, some more comprehensive than others. It is recommended that PPS prioritize reuse
of those school structures with seismic upgrades, while identifying those structures in need of
upgrade and creating a plan for financing the upgrades.


2035 Comprehensive Plan Urban Design Framework
Portland's 2035 Comprehensive Plan Urban Design Framework identifies a network of centers,
civic and neighborhood corridors, and broader neighborhood pattern areas that provide
context for future growth and development. The sites have the following relationship to
Comprehensive Plan designations.

•   Pattern Areas: Most sites are within the Inner Neighborhoods. Maplewood and
    Stephenson elementary schools are within the Western Neighborhoods, while Woodmere
    Elementary is primarily within the Eastern Neighborhoods, near the Inner Neighborhoods
    boundary.

•   Designated Centers: Two sites are within or partially within designated centers. James John
    Elementary is within the St. Johns Town Center, and Creston Elementary is partially within
    the Powell/Creston Neighborhood Center. Sellwood Middle School is approximately one
    block from the Sellwood/Moreland Neighborhood Center.

Inner Eastside Zoning Project
The Inner Eastside Zoning Project study area overlaps a portion of the Inner Neighborhoods.
The project is in its early stages and is exploring potential zoning changes to allow a greater
diversity of housing types and neighborhood businesses. The Buckman, Creston, Irvington, and
Rose City Park elementary schools are within the study area. Other sites, including Sabin and
Marysville elementary schools, are located near the study area boundary. The project creates
an opportunity to explore zoning options for these sites.


Zoning and Land Use
Of the 14 school sites being considered for closure, 13 are primarily zoned for single-dwelling
residential uses. Nine sites are zoned Residential 5,000 (R5), two are zoned Residential 2,500
(R2.5), and one each is zoned Residential 7,000 (R7) and Residential 10,000 (R10). James John
Elementary School is the exception, with Multi-Dwelling Residential (RM2) and Commercial
Mixed Use 2 (CM2), medium density zoning. Several sites also have additional zoning
considerations, Beach Elementary School is within the Albina Community Plan District, James
John Elementary School is within the St. Johns Plan District, and Irvington Elementary School
includes an area zoned Open Space (OS) and is a contributing resource in the Irvington Historic
District. Sabin Elementary School also has an aircraft landing overlay zone.

Mobility and Access
Transit access also varies across the sites, several are located within approximately ¼ to ½ mile
of frequent-service transit, including Beach, Buckman, Creston, Irvington, James John, Lewis,
Marysville, Peninsula, Rose City Park, Sabin, and Woodmere elementary schools and Sellwood
Middle school. Transit access is more limited at Maplewood and Stephenson elementary
schools in Southwest Portland, located within the Western Neighborhoods pattern area.

Based on a 1-mile walkshed analysis conducted by Metro, Maplewood Elementary (88%) and
Stephenson Elementary (74%) have the highest percentages of missing sidewalks, followed by
Woodmere Elementary (37%) and Lewis Elementary (33%). In contrast, Irvington Elementary
(2%) and Sabin Elementary (5%) have the lowest percentages of missing sidewalks. Barrier
streets are defined as streets with more than 2 lanes, a speed limit greater than 35 miles per
hour or peak PM traffic volume greater than 2,500. Buckman Elementary (23%) and Beach
Elementary (20%) have the highest percentages of barrier streets in their 1-mile walksheds,
while Lewis Elementary (0%), Sabin Elementary (2%), and James John Elementary (3%) have the
lowest.
