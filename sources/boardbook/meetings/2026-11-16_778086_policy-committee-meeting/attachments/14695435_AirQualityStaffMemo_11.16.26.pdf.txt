Subject: Staff Response to the Community Proposed Indoor Air Quality (IAQ) Policy

Dear Board Members,

 The District appreciates the thought and energy put into the proposed policy changes, however, the language from the
 Community Proposed Indoor Air Quality additions should not be attached to the existing Climate Crisis Response Policy.
 There are concerns that a blanket commitment to higher ventilation rates above ASHRAE 62.1 and the Oregon
 Mechanical Specialty Code would put the District in misalignment with the Climate Crisis Response Policy’s energy and
 emissions targets. By consolidating this under one policy, we risk an expansion of the policy’s scope that would dilute
 the existing language, (seeing a pivot from global emissions mitigation and climate action to localized public health) and
 implementation of the existing CCRP’s climate goals.

 If the Board decides that an Air Quality policy should exist, it should exist as a stand-alone policy, with the potential
 for a broader focus on public and environmental health. We recommend a collaborative staff-and-community-driven
 reporting structure, a timeline of goal implementation independent from the CCRPs timelines, and a clearly identified
 IAQ management team of existing staff members tasked with responding to the goals under the direction of a
 to-be-hired IAQ Coordinator position. The motivations behind this are to ultimately protect the current scope of the
 Climate Crisis Response Policy and to make the financial and operational impact of this proposed policy more
 apparent.

Core Policy Misalignment: Why IAQ & CCRP Should Remain Separate
  The overarching objective of the CCRP is to mitigate global emissions and reduce our reliance on fossil fuels. Given
  IAQs focuses on micro-environmental public health parameters, targets like these would be better suited in a different
  policy with a focus on compliance with existing occupational health mandates. The combining of standard indoor
  occupational health mandates with global decarbonization policy dilutes the core focus of the CCRP and risks making it
  harder to reach net-zero by 2040.

  In its current state, the CCRP explicitly states that district investments should prioritize those that achieve the “greatest
  emissions reduction.” By elevating IAQ monitoring within the CCRP, the priority of the Climate Justice, Energy &
  Sustainability team would pivot from policy fulfillment to ongoing facilities operational maintenance, further hindering
  the pursuance of environmental and sustainability education gains as well as structural and multi-year carbon
  reduction projects. Additionally, the Climate Justice, Energy & Sustainability team has concerns about the lack of
  involvement of the Climate Crisis Response Committee in this policy decision.

  The Climate Crisis Response Committee is a group of nine individuals (two students and seven adults), who are tasked
  with advising the PPS Board of Education on implementation of the CCRP. The committee has not been involved in this
  decision-making process or had the chance to formally provide input to the board on what they think about this
   proposal. This proposed policy change would drastically alter the scope of what the CCRC oversees, without asking the
   individual committee members for their input on this decision. The CCRC’s goal is to ensure the strength and relevance
   of the CCRP, and by not consulting them, they are not being utilized as the committee charter intends. This is another
   major driver in the desire for policy separation, establishing Air Quality as a standalone policy with a topical focus on
   public and environmental health.

   Lastly, it is important to highlight that IAQ standards and HVAC operations are traditionally regulated under facilities
   management plans, standard operating procedures, occupational health and safety protocols (OSHA/ASHRAE), or
   public health guidelines. Many of these frameworks already inform the mission of the Environmental Health & Safety
   team in their pursuance of clean air in our schools. There is a hesitancy to lock in this level of detail in the Climate Crisis
   Response Policy, knowing that operational target metrics have the potential to rapidly evolve. As it stands, the CCRP
   has taken a more broad approach with its regulatory rigidity, so the Climate Justice, Energy & Sustainability team can
   adapt as the science and industry adapts. We believe any Air Quality targets should take the same approach with policy
   and take a more granular approach in the creation of operating procedures.

3rd-Party Technical Evaluation: Industry Expert Findings
   Apex Companies is an environmental consulting firm that has been in business for 38 years and has a staff of almost
   2,000 individuals, known for their technical excellence and commitment to quality. They were tasked with providing a
   report on the impact of changing the Ventilation Rates at Atkinson Elementary (chosen because it reflects an average
   Elementary School in terms of size within PPS). Schools in the District are built to industry standards using best
   practices, specifically compliance with legally adopted building codes. The emphasis on air changes per hour (ACH) in
   the proposed policy alone is not a widely-accepted industry benchmark for air ventilation systems.
   The use of ASHRAE 62.1-2022 within the Oregon Mechanical Specialty Code represents the legally adopted,
   peer-reviewed, and balanced engineering standards for ventilation design. ASHRAE standards are developed
   through a multi-year consensus process involving engineers, public health experts, and regulators. It provides
   acceptable indoor air quality while maintaining reasonable construction costs, energy consumption, and
   environmental impact. Designing schools in compliance with OMSC and ASHRAE 62.1 therefore represents
   appropriate professional engineering practice and regulatory compliance.

Financial & Resource Constraints: Impact & Resource Allocation
   Meeting aggressive ventilation goals like targeting 6 to 12 effective air changes per hour (ACH) requires running HVAC
   systems continuously and drawing in significantly larger volumes of unconditioned outdoor air. Increasing ventilation
   rates above codes and standards risks the longevity of our building systems and has the potential to overwork and
   ultimately increase the failure rate of building systems. Pursuing a target of 6 - 12 ACH would lead to ventilation rates
   at 50% to 200% higher than code minimums. Before a policy commitment of this caliber is made by this Committee, a
   larger conversation must happen, outside of the Climate Crisis Response Policy, to determine how existing Standard
   Operating Procedures and compliance with the CCRP can be balanced to meet additional goals.

   The APEX report on ventilation rates to target 4 - 6 ACH suggests that we could see an annual increase of HVAC energy
   costs of $15,000-$30,000 at Atkinson Elementary. Pursuing rates of 6 - 12 ACH would see an even higher cost impact in
   our utility bill portfolio. Designing schools for higher ventilation rates requires larger mechanical equipment. Since
   HVAC systems typically represent 10% to 20% of total school construction cost, this translates to 1% to 5% increase in
   total building construction cost. In addition to the cost impact of pushing our air ventilation systems beyond existing
   standards and codes, we would see an increase to our HVAC’s energy usage by 10-40% at each site, which will make
  our emissions reduction targets harder to meet. Before adopting ventilations standards that are more robust than
  current ASHRAE 62.1 and OMSC recommendations, a larger conversation needs to happen around the conflict
  between pushing our building systems to work harder and the emissions that would accompany this policy decision.

  The financial impacts of the proposed language are significant for the District. The proposed language would require
  the District to purchase sensors for each classroom at a (low end) cost of $1,500 per classroom. This cost does not
  include the hardwiring needed for each classroom from IT and Maintenance and the required maintenance and
  monitoring. With research from our HVAC partners, the estimated cost (low end) to increase air changes per hour
  (ACH) from 3 to 6 would be approximately $500,000 a year for the entire District. That is the basic operating costs of
  increased energy usage. This cost does not include the initial investment required by the District and the need for an
  additional FTE to monitor this program.

In conclusion, we recommend the following next steps:

 ●​ Pause the Policy Adoption: Do not include the IAQ additions into the CCRP to allow a more thorough board review
    of 3rd party findings.
 ●​ Maintain CCRP Policy Boundaries: Keep the Climate Crisis Response Policy focused on emissions reduction, net-zero
    goals, and structural climate action.
 ●​ If establishing a Standalone Policy: Draft a separate, dedicated IAQ policy supported by an IAQ Coordinator and
    tailored operational procedures and non-conflicting goals of CCRP.
