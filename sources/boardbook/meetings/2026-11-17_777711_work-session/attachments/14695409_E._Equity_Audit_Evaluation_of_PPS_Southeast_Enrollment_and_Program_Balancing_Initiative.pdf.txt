                   Detailed Executive Summary of Findings
Portland Public Schools, hereafter referred to as (PPS), implemented the Enrollment and
Program Balancing initiative as part of a broader district effort to address enrollment imbalances,
program access, and related equity concerns across Southeast Portland schools. As part of this
effort, PPS partnered with and commissioned Dr. Terrance L. Green and his team to conduct an
equity audit evaluation of the enrollment and program balancing initiative process and its
implementation. The partnership officially began on January 20, 2026, and the final report was
submitted on June 10, 2026.

Many PPS stakeholders and participants commonly referred to this effort as SEGC, which stands
for Southeast Guiding Coalition. Since SEGC was frequently used by participants during
interviews and conversations to describe the broader process, we use SEGC in many places
throughout this report to reflect participants’ language. Unless we are specifically referring to
members of the Southeast Guiding Coalition itself, references to SEGC in this report should be
understood as referring to the broader Enrollment and Program Balancing initiative and its
related processes.

The purpose of this evaluation is to provide PPS with empirical data to better understand how
SEGC-related changes were experienced by students,2 families, principals, educators, and staff,
in schools that experienced boundary changes, program relocations, enrollment shifts, and other
related transitions. The report also examines patterns in student outcomes in these impacted
schools before and after SEGC implementation, while recognizing that these outcomes are
shaped by multiple factors and should not be interpreted as solely caused by SEGC.

Findings from this evaluation are intended to identify areas of equity and inequity across the
SEGC process, implementation, and outcomes, including how decisions were made,
communicated, implemented, supported, and experienced over time. Through this report, we aim
to provide useful, evidence-based, and humanizing data that can inform PPS’s future decisions
about enrollment balancing, boundary changes, program relocation, school consolidation and
closures, and other districtwide changes in ways that are more equitable, transparent, and
responsive to school communities.

    Domain 1: Equitable Access to Robust Middle Schools and all SEGC-impacted schools4

    Guiding Questions:
       1. To what extent did SEGC boundary changes improve enrollment balance across
          schools, especially middle schools, by race, income, and language status?

       2. To what extent were opportunity gaps in achievement, attendance, and discipline
          reduced among students affected by enrollment shifts, especially in middle schools?

       3. How do students and families from historically underserved communities describe the
          quality of learning environments they experienced after SEGC-related shifts in middle
          schools?

       4. How do administrators, teachers, and staff describe the quality of learning

         middle school level. Non-SEGC schools went the other direction. The difference
         between the highest-and lowest-enrolled SEGC middle schools narrowed from
         approximately 320 students in 2020–21 to about 220 in 2024–25, including Kellogg
         Middle School, which opened in 2021–22 and had the highest enrollment among
         middle schools in 2024–25. By contrast, the range of enrollment numbers at non-SEGC
         middle schools increased by about 45 students. SEGC elementary school enrollment

         school planning and stability. When enrollment is lower than projected, schools may

    RQ3: How do students and families from historically underserved communities describe the
    quality of learning environments they experienced after SEGC-related shifts in middle
    schools?

       ● Within the middle school perception gap, the largest differences are in school
         environment and learning. Families with only elementary students rate high-quality
         learning environments at 61% versus 45% for families with only middle school
         students or with both middle school and elementary students. Regarding absence of
         disrespect or bias, the split is 66% versus 48% (54% for families with both middle
         school and elementary school students). On belonging, 78% versus 64–66% for
         families with middle school students. Additionally, 73% of families with only
         elementary students agree that their student is learning a lot vs. 57–59% of families
         with only middle school students or a mix of middle and elementary students. These
         gaps suggest that middle school families perceive qualitative differences in their
         children's school and learning environments.

    RQ4: How do administrators, teachers, and staff describe the quality of learning environments
    after SEGC-related shifts?

       ● Staffing reductions and enlarged class demands made learning environments
         more challenging and harder to sustain.
         Across multiple schools, staff described large classes, fewer adults, more students with
         complex needs, and less ability to differentiate instruction. In several schools,
         participants linked reduced staffing and larger classes to weaker support for project-
         based learning and middle-school grades programming. Participants also said
         classroom quality “varies widely,” with some high-rigor rooms and others not.
         Educators described classes with students with many IEPs, behavior needs, and safety
         concerns, while participants at other schools explicitly tied class size to declining
         ability to offer a comprehensive middle school or relationship-rich elementary
         environment. In one school, staff described teachers with multiple preps and class loads
         in the high 30s or low 40s. In the survey, about half of staff agree that students
         experience high-quality learning environments (54%) and that collaboration supports
         teaching (56%). However, only 16% agree that class sizes are distributed manageably,
         and 64% say class sizes are too large.

       ● Spanish DLI consolidation from SEGC changes was described as both more
         coherent and more costly.
         In one school, staff said the shift to a full-school Spanish DLI model felt “much more
         intentional” and more equitable than the prior split structure, while also describing loss
         of neighborhood access and walkability for students who were part of the neighborhood

Key Findings:

To what extent did access to DLI, electives, or advanced programs expand for historically
underserved students after program relocation per SEGC changes?

   ● Total SEGC DLI enrollment reached 86% of projected targets by 2024–25. Total
     DLI enrollment in the SEGC region declined by 9% between 2020–21 and 2024–25. It
     stands about 280 students short of the 1,959-student target the district set in 2021. The
     aggregate decline masks variation across programs. Enrollment in Spanish DLI, the
     largest program, held steady. Vietnamese DLI more than tripled from a small base (17
     to 57 students). Mandarin and Russian lost about 20% enrollment and Japanese DLI
     enrollment declined by 5%. Districtwide, total DLI enrollment held steady because
     non-SEGC DLI programs gained more than 100 students during the same period.
   ● Spanish DLI programs are growing toward or exceeding enrollment targets,
     reaching 104.5% of the 2025 projection in aggregate (811 vs 776). Three of four
     Spanish DLI sites exceeded their targets, and one stood at 76% of the projection as of
     2024–25. Latino/a/x students discontinue DLI at only 1-2% per year, lower than most
     other racial groups except multiracial students (who discontinue at less than 1%).
     White and Asian discontinuation rates are also low (each under 3% per year). Black
     students discontinue at 7–17% per year, substantially higher than any other group,
     though small sample sizes (58-93 students per year) introduce uncertainty in single-
     year estimates. From 2022–23 to 2024–25, the share of Latino/a/x students enrolled in
     SEGC DLI programs rose by 4 percentage points (31% to 35%), slightly outpacing the
     3-point increase in non-SEGC DLI programs (41% to 44%). However, non-SEGC
     programs continued to enroll a higher share of Latino/a/x students throughout the
     period, by about 9 percentage points.
   ● Heritage language match in DLI programs varies by program and by SEGC
     status. In 2024–25, the percentage of students whose home language matched the DLI
     program’s language ranged from under 10% (Japanese, both SEGC and non-SEGC)

To what extent does the relocation of programs and boundary changes displace students from
their newly assigned schools?

   ● DLI-enrolled students whose program moved to another school largely stayed in
     PPS, whether or not they followed their DLI program to the new school. The
     percentage of DLI-enrolled students leaving the district in SEGC schools is not notably
     different from that in DLI programs outside SEGC. On average, DLI students leave
     PPS at roughly half the rate of non-DLI students. At SEGC schools, the exit rate for
     DLI-enrolled students is about 6% compared to 11% for students not enrolled in DLI in
     2023–24. The gap is similar (about 7% vs. 10%) at non-SEGC schools.

To what extent is student displacement happening in DLI programs as a result of SEGC
program relocation? If so, for whom?

   ● Students enrolled in DLI programs that moved generally followed their program
     to the school it relocated to when they stayed in PPS. Among students left their
     programs upon relocation, they more often identified as white and more often speak a
     language other than English at home. For programs that stayed in place, on average
     across years, students who speak English at home again had higher retention rates than
     students speaking other languages at home.

     program moved. Middle school enrollment declined with relocation but now
     serves a higher share of Chinese speakers. One strand of elementary Mandarin DLI

Domain 3: Leaders, Educator, and Staff Experiences

Guiding Research Questions:
   1. To what extent did the SEGC process affect administrator, educator and staff morale
      and retention, especially among personnel of color?

   2. To what extent did administrators, educators and staff feel equipped to support students
      and families during and after SEGC-related changes?

   3. How do administrators, educators, and staff, particularly those of color, experience the
      SEGC changes in terms of emotional well-being and professional support?

   4. How did administrators, educators, and staff describe the experiences of students and
      families from historically underserved communities after SEGC-related shifts?

Key Findings:

RQ1: To what extent did the SEGC process affect administrator, educator and staff morale
and retention, especially among personnel of color?

   ● Staff morale was most negatively affected where SEGC-related change overlapped
     with repeated staffing loss, turnover, or role instability.
     Across schools, staff described morale not as a single dip tied to SEGC changes alone,
     but as a sustained response to annual cuts, un-assignment of staff, role reassignment, or
     ongoing instability. For example, at one school, staff described the merged years post
     SEGC as so taxing that one participant said they “wanted to quit, like, every day.” At
     another school, school-based staff described morale as “poor” and one participant
     reported taking a five-week mental health leave. Participants also described annual
     reductions of “three to four FTE per year,” sometimes even seven or eight at a time,
     while some participants at several schools described living in a “doing more with less”
     environment that eroded job security and left burnout feeling “imminent.”

   ● Strong internal culture and leadership buffered morale but did not eliminate
     strain.
     Several SEGC-impacted schools held two realities at once, that is, they were internally
     strong and externally strained. Participants at one school described the school as having
     a “bright future” with strong relationships and calm classrooms, even while staff talked
     about what they called “trauma bonding” around looming district cuts. Participants at
     another school described the school as both “a good place to be” and a place where
     “burnout felt ‘imminent for everybody.’” Participants at two schools described long-
     term leadership and strong internal culture as major stabilizers, while participants at
     one school described a shift from low staff confidence to a more “people-centered”
     climate, and participants at another school repeatedly contrasted positive day-to-day
     morale with deep anxiety about district-level cuts. In other words, morale was buffered
     where leadership and peer culture were strong, but that buffering often looked more
     like preventing collapse than producing ease.

● Administrator and principal turnover preceded the major changes. In 2021–22,
  55% of principals at SEGC schools left, compared to 26% at non-SEGC schools.
  Districtwide, schools with the highest staff retention were more likely to have kept the
  same principal across all six years (7 of 19 districtwide) and the bottom quartile of
  retention almost always had principal turnover (15 of 19 schools districtwide). Ten
  SEGC schools appear in the bottom quartile of staff retention in PPS, and the district
  pattern holds among them: 8 of the 10 had principal turnover. Principal turnover is
  notably associated with lower staff retention, fewer long-tenured staff, and higher rates
  of new hires.

● Evidence about personnel of color suggests distinct burdens and, in some places,
  distinct forms of affirmation. However, the cross-school data is not strong enough
  for broad generalization because of the small sample size of personnel of color.
  One school provides the clearest evidence that SEGC and school context intersected
  with staff racial and linguistic identities. One bilingual educator described the current
  school as a place where they no longer had to “explain who I am” or overthink their
  language, while another recounted being told by a colleague, “don’t speak in Spanish in
  front of me,” while working at another PPS campus. A participant at another school
  explicitly described seeing “an exodus of administrators of color, of staff of color”
  because systems to support them were not in place, before and after SEGC changes.
  Participants also highlighted the emotional and symbolic harm of district layoffs that
  heavily impacted colleagues of color in the central office. These examples matter, but
  because our school-by-school data does not explicitly state that staff race was not
  consistently identified, the data support a suggestive but not comprehensive cross-
  school claim.

● The SEGC implementation year produced a dramatic one-year staffing
  disruption. After the 2022–23 school year, 32% of staff at SEGC schools left their
  school, compared to 19% at non-SEGC schools. By 2024–25, the gap had closed to 2
  points (20% vs. 18%). The disruption was acute but temporary in the aggregate.
  Assistant principals were hardest hit as more than 50% (7 of 13) left their school vs.

● The three largest racial groups experienced SEGC changes differently. Black staff
  at SEGC schools (n=12–16 per year) show persistently elevated turnover and the

       highest involuntary termination rate, nearly 5 times the rate for white staff (though

RQ2: To what extent did administrators, educators and staff feel equipped to support students
and families during and after SEGC-related changes?

   ● Staff frequently reported that district communication and planning were
     insufficient for the scale of change they were expected to manage from SEGC.
     At one school, staff described supporting families while “flying blind” and said there
     was no deliberate, intentional merger planning. At another school, one participant
     answered the question about feeling equipped with “Not at all, I would say as close to
     zero,” after inheriting a severely unsafe elementary environment with little groundwork
     laid. Participants at another school recalled answering family questions with unreadable
     maps and incomplete information, while staff at a nearby school said much of the
     transition planning was ultimately left to individual schools. Participants also described
     feeling under-informed and left to absorb parent frustration after the central SEGC lead
     disappeared.

   ● Schools felt most equipped when local leadership, not district systems, built the
     support structures to navigate SEGC changes.
     At one SEGC-impacted school, staff generally felt able to support students and families
     because of the school’s strong trauma-informed routines, relational culture, and internal
     support teams, even while broader district conditions felt precarious. In other schools,
     staff described extensive school-led outreach, family game nights, social work, and
     translation work. Additionally, according to participants, they described essentially
     inventing the middle school transition themselves because there was no clear district
     “guidebook” on how to navigate SEGC changes. Participants noted that much of what
     they could offer families was empathy and school-level encouragement rather than a
     district-supported solution. Across these schools, the practical capacity to support
     families came more from school-level initiative than from a robust, coherent district
     transition infrastructure.

   ● Being “equipped” to navigate SEGC changes was especially fragile where support
     depended on unstable staffing or discretionary choices.
     Participants described choosing between climate supports and reading and math
     intervention because there were not enough adults to do both. This was underscored as
     a participant indicated that intervention classes were “what’s making the difference
     overall” but the school was “having to scramble to keep them.” Other participants
     described district “tier three” supports as help that was “not helpful,” because what the
     school needed was a more cohesive building, not just curricular add-ons. Post SEGC
     changes, other participants described trying to implement an inclusive neighborhood
     model without enough paraeducator and special education support, and in other
                                                                                                16


       schools, participants repeatedly said they could not offer families much beyond
       transfers or encouragement because real options were limited.

   ● Staff felt unequipped during the process and remain feeling unequipped today.
     During implementation, only 6-10% of survey respondents reported adequate
     information, support, or input. Four years later, only 5% reported they have written
     guidance for responding to family questions about their school model, and only 4%
     know who to contact for SEGC-related information. Limited access to information and
     support among staff echoed across family survey respondents: only 22% agreed they
     understood the changes happening at their school during the SEGC process.

Nuance, Tensions, and Variations within the qualitative findings
  ● During our analysis, we identified important variations around the findings to the
     research question. Some schools suggested that strong school-level systems can create
     a sense of practical efficacy even when district support is weak (and these schools were
     impacted with few and lots of changes as a result of SEGC changes). By contrast, our
     analysis suggests that in other schools when the district’s planning and follow-through
     are especially thin, schools can spend years in reactive support mode. Another tension
     is that staff often felt equipped in a moral sense in that they knew what students and
     families needed, but not in a structural sense because they lacked staffing, time,
     information, or district backing to deliver it.

RQ3: How do administrators, educators, and staff, particularly those of color, experience the
SEGC changes in terms of emotional well-being and professional support?

   ● Emotional well-being was strained by cumulative, not single-event (SEGC-
     changes), disruption.
     Participants across schools described stress, grief, anger, dread, sleeplessness, mental-
     health leave, and emotional exhaustion as layered effects of SEGC changes along with
     repeated changes. Some participants said they “wanted to quit, like, every day,” while
     other participants recalled “a lot of tears during the [SEGC] transition.” Participants
     also described feeling on a “tightrope,” and other participants reported not sleeping and
     taking several weeks of mental-health leave, and other educators described that SEGC
     changes brought a constant crisis of “putting out fires.” Some participants framed their
     SEGC experience as a “multi-year erosion” rather than a one-time shock.

   ● Professional support during SEGC was often strongest inside the school and
     weakest at the district level.
     This was a recurring theme across several of our research questions. Some participants
     described their current principal as highly supportive and “people-centered,” and staff
     in another school said their principal’s long tenure and internal culture helped the
     school make it through enormous change. Again, participants repeatedly contrasted
     excellent internal support with low trust in district decisions, and while other
     participants said day-to-day morale stayed relatively high because of strong local
     culture even while district conditions drove anxiety. Staff also described stable internal
     leadership and cohesion as a major protective factor. The emerging pattern is that
                                                                                                  17


       school-based leaders buffered emotional harm, but that buffering often had to
       compensate for the absence of reliable district support.

   ● Among survey respondents, 25% considered leaving PPS due to SEGC changes.
     Personnel data validate this: 15% left the district in 2022–23, compared with 10% at
     other PPS schools. Trust collapsed. Only 3% of staff survey respondents mostly or
     completely trust the district to handle rightsizing fairly. More than 50% responded that
     they do not trust the district at all (mean 1.7/5).

   ● SEGC-period well-being ratings are sharply negative. On average, 8% of
     respondents agreed with items related to wellbeing during the SEGC process, including
     perceptions about worsening morale, not feeling emotionally supported at work, and
     unmanageable workloads. Administrators had neutral or negative perceptions of
     wellbeing during the SEGC process, often perceiving a need for more support and
     better communication. Staff have more positive perceptions of their wellbeing today,
     with BIPOC staff reporting somewhat more positive experiences than white staff both
     during SEGC changes and now, though the small sample (n=32) limits confidence.

   ● The limited available evidence suggests that staff of color and bilingual staff
     sometimes experienced both sharper harm and sharper affirmation.
     While the data are too limited to support broad claims across all SEGC-impacted
     schools, several accounts suggest that staff of color, bilingual staff, and staff working in
     DLI settings sometimes experienced questions of belonging, professional value,
     burnout, and support in distinct ways during SEGC-related change.

       Race, language, and role shaped how some staff experienced SEGC-related change.
       While the evidence is limited and should not be overstated, participant accounts
       suggest that SEGC-related changes did not land evenly across all staff. Some staff
       described their experiences through the lens of racial identity, cultural identity,
       language status, and professional role.

       School context mattered. Some staff described environments where racial, cultural, and
       multilingual identities were affirmed as assets, while others described contexts where
       those identities or roles felt undervalued. This suggests that leadership, school culture,
       and implementation support shaped whether staff experienced SEGC-related change as
       harmful, affirming, or both.

       Some staff experienced sharper harm when their identities or roles were not fully
       valued or supported. Participants described how broader system conditions, including
       rapid change, limited support, and inequitable organizational cultures, could intensify
       feelings of burnout, marginalization, or professional devaluation for staff of color and
       linguistically positioned staff.

Nuance, Tensions, and Variations within the qualitative findings
  ● One school in particular complicates any simple “SEGC harmed staff well-being” story
     because it is simultaneously one of the sites of greatest grief and one of the clearest
                                                                                              18


       sites of professional affirmation for bilingual/Latino/a/x staff. Another school that was
       less impacted also complicates the pattern because staff describe the school as calm and
       relationally strong even while naming broader uncertainty. So, across the data, the
       emotional story is not uniform despair. We’d argue that it could probably be more
       accurately described as cumulative strain that was mediated by several factors, such as

RQ4: How did administrators, educators, and staff describe the experiences of students and
families from historically underserved communities after SEGC-related shifts?

   ● Staff across multiple schools perceived that low-income communities and
     communities of color bore disproportionate disruption as a result of SEGC-
     related changes.
     Staff at one school described the school as becoming “smaller and whiter,” and said
     lower-income students felt more isolated as the school lost racially and
     socioeconomically diverse boundary areas. A participant remarked that SEGC once
     again meant “mostly BIPOC students” being bused out of a multicultural working-class
     neighborhood into a more affluent one. Similarly, staff said affluent families had more
     power to avoid the changes and that what parents were often not saying aloud was that
     they did not want to send their children to school with “our students of color.” Staff
     similarly had a perception of a district pattern in which “communities of color… and
     lowest socio-economic” communities were most affected by the SEGC changes.

   ● Some participants described gains in cultural affirmation or developmental fit for
     particular underserved communities.
     Participants in a few schools described their school as a “sanctuary” for Latino/a/x
     families and a more intentional, affirming full-school Spanish DLI model.
     Additionally, participants said families at another campus were “very excited and
     happy” to have elementary students back in a neighborhood K–5 instead of sharing
     space with middle schoolers, at one site. Other staff described DLI consolidation as
     strengthening opportunities for Latino/a/x students, and other participants described the
     new comprehensive middle school model as giving students a more developmentally
     appropriate and richer middle school experience. Some schools also became more
     coherent or affirming for specific student groups, especially students of color and
     students who are emerging bilingual students.

   ● Transportation, language access, chronic absenteeism, and specialized support
     capacity were recurring mechanisms through which inequity was experienced.
     Participants at some schools described long bus rides, difficult access to pick up
     locations, and the burden of travel falling hardest on families with the least means (this
     also came up in some of our family and SEGC member conversations). Staff at one
     school emphasized high chronic absenteeism, newcomer needs, and the difficulty of
     educating students who simply could not get to school consistently. However, at one
     school, participants described the school as essential for immigrant, refugee,
     multilingual, and low-income families, even as program contraction and future
     uncertainty threatened those conditions. Some staff described lower-income students as
                                                                                               19


       bearing disproportionate burdens of feeder isolation and less ability to navigate transfer
       loopholes. Other staff described intervention support as critical for historically
       underserved students but increasingly fragile under staffing loss.

Nuance, Tensions, and Variations within the qualitative findings
  ● A major tension in this finding is that the same school can appear both as a site of loss
     and a site of affirmation, culturally and linguistically. One school is the clearest
     example of this. On one hand, staff describe the loss of a diverse, walkable
     neighborhood K–8 and the concentration of burden on other communities, but they also
     describe the current DLI model as more affirming and intentional for Latino/a/x

   2. How can future district efforts, especially those related to enrollment and program

Key Findings

RQ1: In what ways did SEGC members feel their input was heard, valued, and reflected in
final decisions?

   ● Most SEGC members that we talked with felt they had very limited influence over
     final decisions.
     While SEGC members invested substantial time, expertise, and energy, most came
     away believing their input did not meaningfully shape district outcomes.

   ● SEGC Members experienced a sharp disconnect between the promise of
     participation and the reality of advisory-only power.
     Many SEGC members entered the process expecting to help shape major decisions, but
     over time concluded that their recommendations were symbolic, constrained, or easily
     overridden.

   ● Perceptions of predetermined outcomes deeply undermined trust in the process.
     SEGC Members repeatedly described key decisions as effectively pre-set (from their
     perceptions), especially around which schools could be included in the process,
     program relocations, school configurations, and the narrowing of what was “on the
     table,” which made the process feel less authentic and less equitable.

   ● Control over data and scenario modeling shaped whether members felt valued.
     Delayed, uneven (meaning some people had access and others did not), or limited
     access to requested data made it difficult for members to test alternatives and
     contributed to the sense that district-preferred outcomes were being advanced
     regardless of coalition input.

   ● Feeling heard among other SEGC members did not translate into feeling heard by
     district leadership.
     SEGC members often described the coalition itself as respectful, collaborative, and
     mutually supportive (meaning mostly the non-district members of the coalition), but
     sharply distinguished that internal experience from their sense that PPS district
     leadership and the board did not meaningfully act on SEGC’s collective input.
                                                                                             21



   ● SEGC members reported low trust and limited confidence in the procedural
     fairness of the rightsizing process. Among 22 SEGC members who responded to the
     family survey, only 23% agree that decision rules were clear and applied consistently,
     23% agree criteria stayed stable, and 23% agree PPS considered family input. All three
     items have a median of 2.0 (disagree). The typical member endorsed fewer than one of
     these three procedural integrity statements. Only 5% of SEGC members trust the
     district on rightsizing (mean 1.8, median 1.5), the lowest trust rating among all groups
     in either the staff or family survey, including groups defined by race/ethnicity, gender,
     school, and other characteristics (e.g., staff role in school, staff years of experience,
     programs students are enrolled in).

   ● SEGC members who participated in the process are among the harshest critics of
     its equity outcomes. On the survey item “benefits and burdens were distributed
     equitably,” SEGC members report a median of 1.0 (strongly disagree), meaning more
     than half selected the lowest possible rating. The people who participated directly in
     designing the distribution of changes believe it was inequitable. This finding converges
     with the quantitative evidence: the schools selected for SEGC changes served student
     populations with 2022–23 free and reduced-price lunch rates averaging 54%, compared
     to 25% at Southeast (SE) Portland schools excluded from the process.

   ● Despite deep dissatisfaction with the process, SEGC members report comparable
     student experiences to other families. SEGC members were positive about many
     aspects of their students’ learning experiences (they responded positively to 70% of
     questions in this domain, on average). This matches the overall family survey results
     (67%). It suggests their dissatisfaction is not driven by enduring personal harm but
     instead reflects informed judgment about a process they observed from the inside and
     concluded failed on its own terms.

Key Findings:

RQ2: How can future district efforts, especially those related to enrollment and program
changes, better build trust and support meaningful engagement with families and
communities?

   ● Future district rebalancing (e.g., school closures and/or consolidations) efforts
     need clearer goals, firmer scope, and more honest discussion of tradeoffs from the
     start.
     Participants felt trust was damaged when the purpose of the process was unclear, the
     scope narrowed midstream, and major constraints were introduced without transparent
     rationale.

   ● Rebuilding trust depends on direct, concrete, and internally consistent
     communication.
                                                                                       22


   SEGC members who participated argued that district communication was often too
   abstract, overly wordy, and at times inconsistent with the data SEGC members had
   been using, which undermined credibility.

● Community Engagement must be accessible, early, and genuinely influential, not
  reactive to pre-developed plans.
  Participants emphasized that families and committee members need understandable
  data, earlier listening opportunities, multilingual and lower-barrier engagement
  formats, and real evidence that their input shaped proposals.

● The district must apply its racial equity and social justice lens through actual
  decisions, not just symbolic language.
  Participating SEGC members felt trust eroded when equity rhetoric was repeated but
  the process excluded certain schools that were more affluent and whiter, protected
  some communities, and placed greater burdens on already under-resourced schools and

● Rebuilding trust requires follow-through, accountability, and visible support for
  affected schools and communities.
  SEGC members who participated in our data collection stressed that the district should
  only make promises it can keep, publicly track commitments, support schools before,
  during, and after major changes, and begin future efforts by acknowledging past harms
  and creating clear guardrails for a different process.

● The district should begin future school change and enrollment balancing efforts
  by publicly acknowledging past harms, broken trust, and specific process failures,
  especially with SEGC changes.
  Participants’ recommendations suggest that rebuilding trust will require more than
  improved communication or better community engagement processes. Future

  harms, including ignored dissent of some SEGC members, broken promises,
  unbalanced outcomes, and perceived inequities in who was included and protected

Domain 5: Factors that Shaped School Experiences with SEGC Changes

Guiding Research Questions:

   1. How did SEGC-related changes play out across the schools, and what factors shaped
      how schools experienced, absorbed, and responded to those changes?


Key Findings:

How did SEGC-related changes play out across the schools, and what factors shaped how
schools experienced, absorbed, and responded to those changes?

   ● SEGC was experienced as cumulative and layered rather than as a single
     transition.
     Across schools, participants described SEGC as one strand in a larger web of stacked
     change, including school mergers, program relocations, feeder loss, boundary shifts,
     grade reconfiguration, construction, pandemic disruption, labor actions, budget cuts,
     and leadership turnover at both the central office and school-level. One participant
     describes this most clearly as a “multi-year erosion,” while others call the process a
     “dense web of transitions.” For one school, SEGC arrived after their fire recovery, K–8
     to K–5 change, and SES program shifts, and another describes being asked to absorb
     major reconfiguration without enough planning, communication, staffing, or emotional
     repair.

   ● Change was easier to absorb where leadership and internal culture were stable.
     Participants from several schools all describe strong internal leadership or school
     culture as a protective factor that helps to mitigate the impacts of SEGC changes. A
     school’s more recent story also suggests improvement where relationships with
     leadership became more coherent and supportive. It is important to note that these
     schools were not “unaffected.” Rather, their leaders and adult cultures absorbed strain
     more effectively. Schools with stable and supportive principals and stronger staff
     culture buffered SEGC strain more effectively, while unstable schools were more
     destabilized.

   ● Program relocation and identity change generated more relational disruption
     than boundary change alone, but even single boundary changes could produce
     deep forms of loss.
     Two schools offered the strongest cases of school identity disruption. In one school,
     participants described a merger that “tore apart” a close community, the other described
     grief, neighborhood loss, and a remade school identity. Another school’s consolidation
     similarly produced both stronger coherence and new tradeoffs. But other schools
     showed that boundary and feeder pattern related changes alone could still trigger deep
     loss by shrinking enrollment, reducing racial and socioeconomic diversity, or
                                                                                            24


       undermining the possibility of a “robust” comprehensive school. So SEGC change type
       mattered (e.g., reconfiguration, program relocation, boundary change etc.), but school
       context shaped intensity.

   ● Schools repeatedly portrayed district support as late, partial, or mismatched to
     the SEGC realities they were facing.
     Whether the issue was missing planning documents, late data, weak transition
     infrastructure, promises not honored (e.g., more FTEs, additional rebalancing, etc.), or
     supports that arrived after the moment had passed, staff often described the district as
     giving them too little, too late, or the wrong kind of help. The result was that schools
     improvised, schools held family distress, and SEGC’s burden was effectively
     downloaded onto people inside buildings.

Nuance, Tensions, and Variations within the qualitative findings
  ● A major tension is that SEGC changes, according to participants, could produce both
     gains and harms in the same site. For example, one school gained facilities, electives,
     and a more developmentally appropriate middle school structure even while becoming
     less racially and socioeconomically diverse. Another school became more culturally
     affirming for many Latino/a/x families even while losing neighborhood access,
     diversity, and parts of its earlier identity. Yet, another school shows how a school with
     limited direct SEGC change could still live under its shadow through downstream
     instability and future consolidation fears. So “how SEGC played out” cannot be
     reduced to one story, it might be best understood as challenging change filtered through
     local, school capacity, history, and politics.

PPS’ Effort to Balance Enrollment in Southeast Schools9
Portland Public Schools’ (PPS) Enrollment and Program Balancing initiative was a Board-
authorized effort created to address how the district’s physical footprint, school configurations,
boundaries, and program locations shaped students’ access to high-quality learning
environments. The work was framed as part of PPS’s reImagined10 strategic vision that was
adopted in June 2019, and then formally launched through Board Resolution 6059 in February
2020. That resolution recognized that PPS faced facility use and system design challenges that
affected student success. The Enrollment and Program Balancing effort was intended to produce
short-and long-term recommendations, informed by community input, with a clear emphasis on
improving access and opportunity for historically underserved students.

For Southeast schools, PPS implemented this work in phases. Phase 1 focused on improving
access to comprehensive middle schools by establishing the attendance area and programs for
Kellogg Middle School, which was intended to be a community-guided process that informed the
recommendation that was ultimately adopted by the Board in January 2021. Phase 2 was then
initiated through Board Resolution 6315 in May 2021, which directed the district to bring
forward recommendations on three concrete outcomes. The outcomes included (a) setting
attendance area and special program assignments for Harrison Park Middle School, (b)
developing a plan to relocate the kindergarten through fifth grade students and programs
currently served at Harrison Park, and (c) increasing enrollment at Lane Middle School.

To support Phase 2 in Southeast, PPS convened the Southeast Guiding Coalition (SEGC) as a
volunteer advisory coalition responsible for reviewing options, considering community feedback,
and advising district leadership rather than making final decisions. Throughout this process, the
coalition reviewed multiple proposals and weighed tradeoffs connected to boundary changes,
feeder pattern adjustments, and program relocations intended to stabilize enrollment, strengthen
access to robust middle school programming, and improve program coherence. While the
coalition did not reach full consensus, it forwarded the plan that received the most votes to
district leadership in spring 2022, and PPS moved toward adoption and implementation planning
for changes beginning in the 2023–24 school year, with boundary changes phased in for new
students and major program relocations implemented more comprehensively at once, alongside
identified transition supports.

future district decision-making.

made changes to boundaries, programs, and enrollment patterns, but whether those changes were
communicated, implemented, and supported in ways that were experienced as equitable, socially
just, transparent, and humanizing. Equity, especially racial equity, in districtwide change requires
more than technical decision-making. It requires attention to who benefits, who bears the burden
of change, whose voices shape the process, what supports are provided during implementation,
and whether impacted schools are strengthened rather than destabilized over time.11 12

those who have been historically marginalized in schools systems because of their race,

Our Approach to Equity Audit Evaluations
This SEGC Equity Audit Evaluation is grounded in Dr. Terrance L. Green’s more than decade-
long body of research on community-based equity audits. Dr. Green introduced community-
based equity audits as a practical approach for educational leaders to examine inequities across
schools and communities while working toward more equitable outcomes.13

Building on existing equity audit research, our approach is designed to further racial and
educational justice. As such, we understand equity audits not simply as technical reviews of data,
policy, or practice, but as opportunities to examine how systems are experienced by the students,
families, educators, staff, and communities most affected by district decisions. For this reason,
our approach is customized to each district we partner with, grounded in research,
comprehensive, collaborative, and transparent.

Additionally, our approach is also strengths-based, rooted in culturally responsive evaluation,
informed by Paulo Freire’s notion of dialogue,14 and attentive to race, racism, and their
intersections with social class, language, gender, gender identity, perceived ability, sexual
orientation, religion, and other markers of difference that have been used to marginalize children,
youth, families, and communities in schools. We also anchor our work in culturally responsive
evaluation15 and emphasize the importance of culture, context, and stakeholder experience in
evaluation design and practice, while intersectionality16 reminds us to attend to how multiple
systems of power shape people’s lived experiences and identities.

In conducting equity audits, our goal is not to “catch” districts doing something wrong or to
assign blame. Instead, we aim to serve as an external, critical, and collaborative partner. Our role
is to provide empirical data, elevate the experiences of those most directly affected, identify
systemic patterns of equity and inequity, and offer actionable recommendations that can help
districts better serve children, youth, families, educators, staff, and communities. This stance is
consistent with professional evaluation principles that emphasize systematic inquiry,
competence, integrity, respect for people, and attention to the common good and equity.

District-Informed Intensity Scoring and Sampling Process
To support a transparent and credible qualitative sampling process, PPS staff with direct
institutional knowledge of the SEGC work completed an intensity scoring exercise for all SEGC-
impacted schools.18 Several PPS district leaders and staff categorized schools into high-,
medium-, and low-intensity based on their overall level of SEGC-related change and
implementation demands. Our evaluation team19 then held two working meetings with PPS on
January 9, 2026, and January 13, 2026, to review the scoring, discuss the rationale for ratings,
surface questions and differences in interpretation, and confirm final school categories.

These calibration conversations strengthened the clarity and consistency of the intensity
categories and helped to support that the sampling frame reflected both district data and lived
implementation knowledge. This process was important because SEGC did not affect all schools
in the same way. Some schools experienced multiple overlapping changes, including program
relocation, boundary shifts, grade reconfiguration, feeder pattern changes, and/or changes in
school identity. Other schools experienced more limited changes, such as boundary adjustments
or downstream enrollment effects.

The final qualitative sample prioritized high-intensity schools while also including a smaller
number of medium- and low-intensity schools. This allowed the evaluation team to examine
schools that were identified for having experienced the greatest implementation demands while
also making meaningful comparisons across different levels of SEGC impact. From this process,
our team identified schools to visit for qualitative data collection.

Addition of Domain 5: Factors That Shaped School Experiences with SEGC Changes
During our data collection and analysis, our evaluation team added a fifth analytic domain,
which was Factors That Shaped School Experiences with SEGC Changes. This domain was
added because the findings suggested that SEGC-related changes did not unfold in the same way
across all schools in the sample. While the broader analysis identified common patterns across
the study, the school-level data showed that schools experienced, absorbed, and responded to
SEGC differently (based on a number of factors that emerged, which we discuss later in the
report).

We added this fifth domain because it moves the analysis beyond describing what changed to
examining the conditions that shaped how those changes were felt, experienced, and
implemented within school communities. These conditions included leadership capacity,
professional relationships, staffing stability, school climate, prior school history, communication,
alignment between district decisions and school-level needs, and the availability of
implementation support. Again, we discuss this in detail later in the report.

This domain is especially important because research on educational change and policy
implementation suggests that schools and districts interpret and enact reforms through existing
organizational conditions.26 Educational change is not linear. As a result, it is shaped by a
number of factors, such as district-school coordination, local fit, flexibility, preparation for
implementation, and capacity-building or capacity for change.27 For this reason, Domain 5 helps
the report address a critical implementation question. This was something that PPS voiced
wanting to know more about: how much change can a school or system absorb before its
capacity to support students, sustain staff, and maintain coherent learning environments
becomes strained?

Integration of Qualitative and Quantitative Evidence
The evaluation was designed to integrate quantitative, qualitative, and survey data so that the
report could answer both outcome-oriented and experience-oriented questions. For example,
enrollment data can show whether SEGC schools became more balanced numerically, while
qualitative data can show whether school communities experienced those changes as stable,
equitable, adequately supported, or in other ways. Similarly, student outcome data can show
whether achievement, attendance, or discipline patterns changed, while family and staff data can
show whether students experienced belonging, safety, cultural affirmation, and access to strong
learning environments.

This integration was especially important because SEGC was not a single intervention with a
single effect. It was a set of related changes that unfolded differently across schools. For that
reason, the report uses multiple forms of evidence to examine both system-level patterns and
school-level variation.

Limitations and Interpretive Cautions
Like every research audit, this evaluation has limitations. As such, we suggest that several
limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

First, the qualitative findings reflect the perspectives of participants who took part in interviews
and focus groups. These perspectives provide important insight into how SEGC-related shifts
were experienced within particular school communities. However, they should not be interpreted
as representing every perspective within a given school. Schools are complex communities, and
individuals within the same school may have experienced these shifts in different ways.

Second, the qualitative school sample was purposefully selected to generate deep insight from
information-rich cases rather than to produce a statistically representative sample of all SEGC-
impacted schools. The sample included 9 of the 20 impacted schools and was designed to
support transferability, not population-level generalization.

Third, the report attends to nuance, tension, and variation because SEGC-related changes were
complex and uneven. That said, the findings should not be read with broad strokes. Thus, the
findings should not be read as suggesting that SEGC was experienced in the same way by every
school, family, staff member, or student group. Instead, the report identifies recurring patterns
while also preserving important differences, contradictions, and context-specific experiences.

Fourth, as previously mentioned, SEGC occurred during a period of broader instability and
change. COVID-19, a global racial uprising, enrollment decline, budget reductions, staffing
changes, district leadership transitions, construction projects, housing pressures, and broader
demographic changes all shaped school experiences. For this reason, we, in this report, avoid
                                                                                                                                      37


attributing every post-SEGC change solely to SEGC. Instead, this report examines how SEGC-
related changes interacted with other conditions.

Fifth, the available data did not allow every minoritized28 group or experience to be examined
with equal precision. For example, some qualitative conversations did not consistently identify
staff race or ethnicity, which limited the ability to make strong systemwide claims about the
experiences of personnel of color based solely on qualitative data. Similarly, some quantitative
analyses of people of color should be interpreted cautiously when sample sizes are small.

There were also limitations to the administrative data. Administrative data was not
comprehensive of all PPS students and staff. First, personnel data were limited to instructional
and front-office staff. It did not include employees in roles such as nutrition and custodial
services. Second, student enrollment records exclude students who enrolled in PPS after October
1st of each school year. It records students who leave the district during the year and mid-year
movement across schools. However, total school enrollment is likely to be slightly
underestimated. Third, the data do not include high school students. This means that we do not
observe high school outcomes for students in grade 8, such as whether they remained in a DLI
program in high school and whether they transferred out of the district (unless their families
reported a transfer before the end of the previous school year).

Finally, this report should be read as an equity-centered evaluation intended to support learning,
accountability, and improvement. Again, its purpose is not to assign blame to individual people
or schools, but to help PPS understand what SEGC changed, how those changes were
experienced, where equity goals were advanced or constrained, and what future districtwide
change efforts should do differently.

Enrollment Balance Improved, but Within a Context of Declining Enrollment and
Enrollment Projection Gaps
Enrollment across SEGC schools became more balanced, especially at the middle school level.
The difference between the highest- and lowest-enrolled SEGC middle schools narrowed from
approximately 320 students in 2020–21 to about 220 students in 2024–25, as Kellogg Middle
School opened and Harrison Park converted from a K–8 to a middle school. SEGC elementary
school enrollment also became more uniform, with the difference between the highest and lowest
enrolled elementary schools narrowing by more than 100 students.

However, these fluctuations should be interpreted within the broader context of enrollment
decline. SEGC elementary school enrollment declined, on average, by 17% between the 2020–21
and 2024–25 school years, compared with a 10% decline among non-SEGC schools. Middle
school enrollment declined by an average of 9% in SEGC schools and 5% outside SEGC schools
over the same period.

This means that some of the apparent balancing occurred within a shrinking enrollment context
rather than through growth or stabilization across all schools. It is important to note that
enrollment projections were overestimated by 20 or more students at seven of 20 SEGC schools
(35%) and underestimated by at least 20 students at four schools.35 These projection gaps have
important implications for school planning, resources, and stability. When enrollment is
meaningfully lower or higher than projected, schools may face midstream adjustments to
staffing, schedules, course offerings, intervention supports, and enrichment opportunities.

 Overall Finding:

 Following SEGC-related program relocations, participants described some DLI programs as
 more coherent and easier to navigate, especially Spanish DLI. However, the data also show

Guiding Research Questions:

   1. To what extent did access to DLI, electives, or advanced programs expand for historically

   3. To what extent is student displacement happening in DLI programs as a result of SEGC

DLI Access Improved in Some Areas but Fell Short of Overall Enrollment Targets
Total DLI enrollment in SEGC-impacted schools reached 86% of projected targets by 2024–25
and remained approximately 280 students short of the 1,959-student target established in the
2021 SEGC plan (Table 1.1). Total SEGC DLI enrollment declined by 9% between 2020–21 and
2024–25, even as districtwide DLI enrollment held steady because non-SEGC DLI programs
gained more than 100 students during the same period.

However, the aggregate trend masks substantial variation by language program type. Spanish
DLI, the largest program, reached 104.5% of its 2025 projection in aggregate. Vietnamese DLI,
though much smaller than the Spanish program, more than tripled in enrollment. By contrast,
Mandarin and Russian DLI programs lost about 20% of enrollment and Japanese declined by
about 5%. These findings suggest that SEGC-related DLI changes did not produce a uniform
pattern across language programs. Some programs showed enrollment growth or moved closer to
projected enrollment targets, while others experienced enrollment contraction. In some cases,
participants also described program relocation as creating greater coherence, but that coherence
was not evenly experienced across all DLI programs.

student groups and that families’ decisions to stay, follow, or leave may be shaped by language

This finding is consistent with national research on dual language and school choice which
indicates that DLI programs can operate as specialized choice programs, where participation is
shaped by race, class, language background, transportation, information, and family capacity.
Prior research has found that DLI programs may attract white, English-dominant, and more
affluent families, and can sometimes shift program priorities away from the language-
minoritized communities that these programs were originally designed to serve (Valdez et al.,
2016;37 Chaparro, 2021;38 Cervantes-Soon et al., 2017).39 In this context, the overrepresentation
of speakers of languages other than English among DLI leavers may reflect a form of program
self-sorting in which families with greater flexibility may be more able or willing to commit
when a program relocates, while families with stronger linguistic, cultural, or programmatic ties
may be more likely to leave.
Moreover, the Mandarin DLI findings illustrate how relocation can reshape program composition
even when enrollment remains relatively stable. One elementary Mandarin DLI strand
maintained its pre-SEGC enrollment level after relocating, while middle school Mandarin DLI
enrollment declined and remained below the SEGC forecast. At the same time, the share of
heritage language speakers increased substantially, indicating that relocation may have changed
not only the size but also the composition of the program.

Burden

displaced from the school, with the Black student enrollment dropping from 10% to 2%, while
the Latino/a/x share increased from 49% to 69%.

Domain 3: School Leaders, Educator, and Staff Experiences

 Overall Finding:

 During the SEGC implementation year, school-based staff were asked to manage substantial
 transition responsibilities. The data show short-term staffing disruption during that period, with
 staff of color experiencing the most pronounced measurable impact. Across schools, staff
 described a gap between their commitment to supporting students and families and the
 resources available to do so. Many understood what students and families needed, but did not
 consistently have the time, staffing, information, district guidance, or transition infrastructure
 necessary to provide that support.

Guiding Research Questions:
   1. To what extent did the SEGC process affect administrator, educator and staff morale and
      retention, especially among personnel of color?

   2. To what extent did administrators, educators and staff feel equipped to support students
      and families during and after SEGC-related changes?

   3. How do administrators, educators, and staff, particularly those of color, experience the
      SEGC changes in terms of emotional well-being and professional support?

   4. How did administrators, educators, and staff describe the experiences of students and
      families from historically underserved communities after SEGC-related shifts?

Staff Morale Was Most Strained Where SEGC Overlapped with Staffing Loss, Turnover,
and Role Instability
Across schools, participants often described morale not as a single dip tied only to SEGC
changes, but as a sustained response to annual cuts, un-assignment of staff, role reassignment,
and ongoing instability. Some participants described wanting to quit, taking mental-health leave,
or working in a “doing more with less” environment that eroded job security and made burnout
feel imminent.

At some schools, strong internal culture and perceptions of leadership effectiveness and tenure
buffered these effects but did not eliminate strain. Participants at some schools described them as
calm, relationally strong, and internally coherent, even while staff worried about future cuts. In
those schools, leadership and peer culture were essential, but they did not remove the stress
created by what participants’ described as district instability, budget uncertainty, or the demands
of implementing SEGC-changes.

Principal and administrator turnover also mattered. In 2021–22, 55% of principals at SEGC-
impacted schools left their schools, compared with 26% at non-SEGC schools. Districtwide,
schools with the highest staff retention were more likely to have had stable principal leadership,
                                                                                                                      53


while schools in the bottom quartile of staff retention almost always experienced principal
turnover. This suggests that leadership stability was a key condition shaping staff retention and
school capacity during SEGC implementation.

The Implementation Year Produced an Acute and Racially Uneven Staffing Disruption
After the 2022–23 school year, 32% of staff at SEGC schools left their school, compared with
only 19% at non-SEGC schools. By 2024–25, the gap had narrowed to two percentage points,
suggesting that the disruption was acute but temporary in the aggregate. Assistant principals
were especially affected, with more than 50% (7 of 13) leaving their school in SEGC-impacted
schools compared with 26% in the rest of the district.

Across multiple schools, staff perceived that low-income communities and communities of color

transportation, language access, chronic absenteeism, and specialized support capacity as
mechanisms through which inequity was experienced.

Latino/a/x families, more developmentally appropriate after returning to a K–5 model, or richer
as comprehensive middle school environments. This means the staff findings do not support a
simple harm-only story. They suggest challenging, uneven and simultaneous effects. In that,
some students and families gained access to more coherent or affirming environments, while
others experienced loss, disruption, or reduced access.

SEGC Members Experienced a Gap Between Participation and Influence
Most SEGC members who participated in the evaluation felt they had very limited influence over
final decisions. Many invested substantial time, expertise, and energy into the process, but came
away believing their input did not meaningfully shape district outcomes. Participants described a
sharp disconnect between the promise of participation and the reality of advisory-only power.
This distinction matters. SEGC members often described the coalition itself as respectful,
collaborative, and mutually supportive. However, they distinguished that experience from their
sense that PPS district leadership and the board did not meaningfully act on SEGC’s collective
input. In other words, members could feel heard by one another while still feeling unheard by the
decision-makers who controlled final outcomes.

Perceptions of Predetermined Outcomes Undermined Trust
SEGC members repeatedly described key decisions as effectively pre-set from their perspective,
especially around which schools could be included in the process, which program relocations
were considered, which school configurations were possible, and what was “on the table.”40
From their perspective, these constraints made the process feel less authentic, less transparent,
and less equitable.

Domain 5: Factors that Shaped School Experiences with SEGC Changes

 Overall Finding:

 SEGC-impacted schools did not unfold as a single, uniform experience across schools. The
 data suggest that schools experienced and responded to SEGC-related changes differently in
 relation to several contextual conditions, including the type, number, and intensity of change,
 leadership stability, staffing capacity, school culture, prior history, program identity, district
 support, and accumulated strain. This finding helps explain variation in how school
 communities made sense of, absorbed, and experienced the same district initiative.

Guiding Research Question:

     1. How did SEGC-related changes play out across the schools, and what factors shaped how
        schools experienced, absorbed, and responded to those changes?

SEGC Was Experienced as Cumulative and Layered, not as a Single Transition
Across the qualitative data, participants described SEGC-related changes as cumulative and
layered rather than as one isolated transition. Schools were not only responding to boundary
changes or program relocations, but they were also navigating school mergers, feeder pattern
changes, grade reconfiguration, construction, pandemic recovery, labor actions, budget
reductions, staffing instability, and leadership turnover at both the central office and school
levels. Participants described this as a dense web of transitions and, in some cases, as a multi-
year erosion.

This finding is important because districts often evaluate changes one at a time, while schools
experience them cumulatively. A single boundary change may seem manageable on paper, but it
can feel very different when layered on top of prior program loss, staffing reductions, leadership
turnover, construction disruption, declining enrollment, or weakened family trust.

As mentioned previously, research on educational change and policy implementation helps
explain this pattern. Schools do not simply receive and implement district decisions in a uniform
way, they interpret and enact reforms through existing organizational conditions, including
leadership capacity, professional relationships, staffing stability, school climate, and alignment
between district decisions and school-level needs.41 More recent research similarly emphasizes
that educational change is shaped by preparation, local fit, coordination, flexibility, and capacity-
building.42

families experienced belonging. Participants described grief, neighborhood loss, disrupted
relationships, and remade school identities in schools where SEGC-related changes altered
school identity or program identity.

At the same time, even boundary and feeder pattern changes could produce deep forms of loss
when they reduced enrollment, weakened feeder stability, reduced racial or socioeconomic
diversity of students, or made it harder to sustain a comprehensive school model. This means that
future PPS planning should examine both the formal type of change and the local school context
in which that change will land.

Stable Leadership and Strong Internal Culture Helped Schools Absorb Change
As mentioned previously, participants across several schools described supportive principals,
strong staff relationships, coherent routines, and shared school identity as protective factors.
These schools were not unaffected by SEGC-related changes. Rather, their leaders and adult
cultures helped them maintain school climate, support families, and keep daily routines
functioning.

However, the findings also caution against over-relying on school-level resilience. Strong
leadership and staff culture often buffered harm that district systems did not adequately prevent.
Where district support was late, partial, unclear, or mismatched to school realities, schools
improvised. They held family distress, solved communication problems, interpreted unclear
information, and built local supports. This suggests that school-level resilience helped schools
survive change, but it should not substitute for stronger district planning, staffing,
communication, and transition infrastructure.

District Support Was Often Experienced as Late, Partial, or Mismatched
Across schools, participants repeatedly described district support as too little, too late, or not well
aligned with the realities schools were facing. Examples included missing planning documents,
late or confusing data, limited transition infrastructure, unclear guidance, expectations that were
not met, support that arrived after the moment had passed, and assistance that did not address the
core organizational challenges schools were experiencing.

These accounts connect directly to the broader implementation question that Domain 5 was
added to address: how much change can a school or system absorb before its capacity to support
students, sustain staff, and maintain coherent learning environments becomes strained? The
SEGC findings suggest that capacity to absorb change depends on more than the formal policy
decision. They further suggest that it depends on timing, prior school history, leadership stability,
staffing depth, class size, staff morale, school climate, shared identity, student need, the quality
                                                                                                 63


of district support, and whether families and staff experience the process as fair and well-
resourced.

Implications for Future PPS Reconfiguration Decisions
Future PPS reconfiguration decisions should include an implementation burden assessment
before final decisions are made. This assessment could ask what each school has already
absorbed, what type of change is being proposed, how much relational or identity disruption the
change may create, what staff and leadership capacity currently exists, what student needs must
be supported, and what transition infrastructure the district will provide.
The strongest cross-school finding is not that SEGC affected every school in the same way. It did
not. Some schools experienced SEGC-related changes as a pathway to stronger coherence,43
better facilities, more developmentally appropriate grade configurations, or more culturally
affirming programs. Other schools experienced the changes as loss, instability, staffing strain,
inequity, weakened trust, and diminished neighborhood connection. In several schools, both
were true at once.
Overall, SEGC is best understood as challenging district change filtered through local school
capacity, history, identity, leadership, staffing, and community politics. Future PPS decisions
should therefore avoid assuming that a district-level policy will land evenly across schools. The
same decision can create different experiences depending on the conditions into which it is
introduced.

Section 2A: Lessons Learned and Recommendations
                                                                                                  66


                                         Lessons Learned

The findings above point to several lessons for PPS as it considers future enrollment balancing,
rightsizing, boundary, program relocation, or school reconfiguration decisions. These lessons are
not intended to relitigate SEGC, but to help the district learn from how SEGC-related changes
were planned, implemented, experienced, and absorbed across schools. We share a few lessons
learned and, in doing so, offer some recommendations for PPS.

SEGC changes did improve enrollment balancing at the middle school level. However,
overall, SEGC schools continued to experience declining enrollment.
These changes did not reverse enrollment declines but more so redistributed it. SEGC changes
made some middle schools more balanced in their enrollment, but it arguably did so by moving
students around within a context of continued overall decline in SE Portland and across the
district. In that sense, SEGC functioned more as a redistribution strategy than as an enrollment
recovery strategy.

PPS should be cautious and honest about making promises and providing support it might
not actually deliver, and it must publicly track follow-through.
Broken promises or even the perceptions of them were among the most trust-damaging elements
in the preliminary findings. Participants repeatedly described promised staffing, small class sizes,
transition support, and follow-up structures that were later reduced, delayed, or denied. This
turned SEGC implementation strain into a trust problem. A major lesson is that PPS should not
make promises during contentious transitions unless they are budgeted, operationalized, feasible
and protected. In addition, several SEGC schools were below the anticipated enrollment
projections for 2025–26.

SEGC type changes require staff support and culture-building in impacted schools, not just
student and program reassignment.
Where schools were merged, reconfigured, or asked to absorb new communities, our findings
suggest that the hardest work was often cultural, relational, and instructional. Staff had to
navigate different norms, expectations, reputations, and community assumptions. Without
explicit support, these tensions could linger for years. In some places, they did. Thus, for future
closures and/or consolidations, PPS might consider providing supports for staff such as co-
planning time for new staff to get to know each other, work discuss student supports, shared
norm-setting convenings, conflict mediation, community-building activities and routines,
orientation for incoming families and students, deliberate identity work for newly configured
schools.

Anchored directly in the data from this analysis, participants identified nine different categorical
types of supports they need or what benefited from during SEGC changes. However, we realize
                                                                                                 67


that given the budget challenges that PPS is experiencing that they will not be able to provide all
of these. Nonetheless, participants discussed having the following supports:

Communication and Transparency: Participants said they needed clearer, earlier, and more
consistent communication throughout the transition process. This included timely explanations
of decisions, honest descriptions of trade-offs, and direct acknowledgment of potential negative
impacts rather than only emphasizing the benefits. For example, people wanted principals and
staff to receive information early enough to plan, and they wanted families and community
members to have real two-way channels to ask questions, give feedback, and understand how
their input would be used.

Staffing and Workload: Many participants emphasized the need for additional staffing support
during transitions (as perceived to be promised), rather than reductions in staff. This included
more teachers, paraprofessionals, specialists, counselors, social workers, behavior support staff,
restorative justice staff, special education staff, and multilingual learner staff. For example,
participants described the need for stable staff assignments to reduce churn and turnover,
protected mental health and behavior roles, and additional adults in buildings to help schools
manage the increased workload that comes with mergers, boundary changes, and program shifts.

Class Size and Time: Participants also identified smaller class sizes and more time as critical
supports. They wanted class sizes to be capped or reduced, especially in schools serving students
with greater needs. They also called for additional planning and collaboration time, including
release time, stipends, substitutes, or temporary staffing. For example, staff needed time to
redesign schedules, align instruction, prepare for new grade configurations, and work through the
practical demands of transition without adding more unpaid labor.

Mental Health, Social-Emotional Learning (SEL), and Trauma-Informed Support: Participants
said schools needed concrete mental health, social-emotional, and trauma-informed supports, not
just promises that such supports would be available. This included counselors, social workers,
SEL programs, trauma-informed training for staff, and intentional community-building spaces.
For example, participants named the need for healing circles, school events, rituals, and other
practices that would help students, families, and staff process loss, uncertainty, and change.

Equity, Language, and Program Supports: Participants emphasized that equity-focused supports
were essential, particularly for students and families most likely to be affected by school
changes. This included protecting and properly resourcing DLI programs, providing translation
and interpretation for meetings and written materials, and offering family navigators or liaisons
to help families understand their options. For example, participants wanted schools receiving
more students or students with greater needs to receive additional resources, not simply be
expected to absorb those changes with the same staffing and budget.
                                                                                                68



Support for SEGC Members and the Process: SEGC members said they needed stronger support
to participate meaningfully in the process. This included clear orientation and training on their
role, scope, and equity lens. As well, they needed access to complete and understandable data
and transparent facilitation and decision-making processes. For example, members wanted clear
information about enrollment, building capacity, demographics, and program impacts, as well as
honesty about which decisions they could actually influence, and which decisions may have
already been made.

Leadership and Culture: Participants wanted visible and responsive district leadership
throughout the transition process. This meant leaders showing up in schools, listening to
concerns, helping solve problems, and supporting school culture and safety, not only managing
logistics. For example, principals wanted earlier involvement and real decision-making power,
while staff wanted district leaders to help with behavior systems, culture-building, and the
relational work needed to bring school communities together.

Collaboration and Relationship and Culture-Building Time: Participants said merged or
reconfigured school communities needed dedicated time to build relationships and align around
shared expectations. This included time for staff to co-create school vision, develop common
routines, align instructional practices, and build trust across teams. For example, participants
wanted structured processes for staff from different schools to come together before the
transition, rather than being expected to form a shared culture after the school year had already
begun.

Operational and Logistical Supports: Participants also named practical logistical supports
needed to make transitions work. These included long-term transportation solutions for new
boundaries and program locations, updated curriculum and materials for new grade
configurations and languages, and central transition-planning tools such as templates, checklists,
and communication plans. For example, schools needed clear systems to manage transportation
changes, staffing shifts, family communication, and instructional adjustments in a coordinated
way.

The type of change matters (intensity) as much as, and sometimes more than, the number
of changes (quantity).
One of the clearest findings was that not all changes were experienced equally. Program
relocation, feeder loss, whole-school conversion (e.g., into a whole school DLI), and grade
reconfiguration often produced deeper disruption than a boundary shift alone, especially when
they altered school identity, belonging, or access to key programs. At the same time, the number
of changes still mattered because schools facing multiple and simultaneous changes often
described cumulative disruption and difficulty regaining a sense of equilibrium. The lesson is
that PPS might consider the nature, intensity, and interaction of those changes in schools.
In addition, our findings suggest that the same type of change can have different meanings
depending on school level. For example, elementary participants often described feeder or
boundary changes in terms of continuity and family relationships. Middle school participants
more often described them in terms of enrollment viability, class offerings, elective access,
                                                                                                     70


staffing, and whether a school could remain robust. PPS should consider avoiding “one-size-fits-
all transition planning” and instead tailor support by school level. For example, elementary
schools may need stronger family orientation, pathway support, and continuity-building and
middle schools may need protection for electives, counseling, staffing, and program breadth.

Schools are not starting from the same place (organizational capacity for change), so the
district should not assume they can absorb the same amount or type of change.
Our preliminary findings repeatedly suggest that schools entered SEGC with different levels of
stability, staffing, leadership continuity, enrollment strength, school culture, program coherence,
and community trust. Some schools were already fragile or carrying accumulated strain. Other
schools had stronger internal systems and could buffer disruption more effectively. A major
lesson, then, is that PPS should not apply the same change logic uniformly across schools.
Before any future closure or reassignment process, the district might assess each school’s
readiness and capacity to absorb change.

Trust-(re)building is shaped by (perceptions of the) process, not just the outcome(s)
Across our findings, a repeated lesson that emerged was that people judged PPS not only by
what decision was made, but by whether the process felt transparent, honest, responsive, and
equitable, especially since all schools in SE were not part of SEGC. Additionally, long meetings,
inaccessible materials, shifting rationales, late data, and decisions that appeared predetermined
all undermined trust. In some cases, participants felt that community voice was invited mainly to
legitimate decisions that had already narrowed.

Leaving some Southeast schools out of the rebalancing process undermined trust and made
it harder to achieve more fully balanced and equitable rebalancing
This preliminary finding suggests that because declining enrollment was a SE-wide challenge
(even districtwide), future rebalancing, consolidation, or closure efforts should also be
districtwide in scope, rather than concentrating the hardest decisions in only some parts of SE.
Across the findings, participants repeatedly described the exclusion of more inner SE schools,
often described as whiter, more affluent, and more politically protected, as a major reason they
lost trust in the process and doubted the district’s application of its racial equity and social justice
lens.

                                       Recommendations

We have organized our recommendations around pre, during, post, and ongoing rightsizing
district supports.

Pre-Rightsizing Decision(s)
Be explicit about tradeoffs and equity tensions
PPS should name tradeoffs directly and transparently, acknowledging that the same decision can
produce meaningful gains while also creating real burdens. For instance, a consolidation may
strengthen programming and staffing while increasing transportation demands for families. A
DLI relocation may improve program coherence and staffing stability but reduce neighborhood
access or shift who can participate. A boundary adjustment may balance enrollment in one
building but reduce racial or socioeconomic diversity or destabilize enrollment patterns in
another. A closure may reduce facility costs yet disrupt trusted relationships and student
belonging. Our findings repeatedly show that the same change(s) can generate benefits and
harms within the same school community, which is why explicitly naming tradeoffs is essential
for equity-centered decision making.

Create a simple equity impact summary for every school closure or consolidation scenario
Our findings suggests that SEGC-impacted schools ended up serving higher shares of students of
color and multilingual learners (i.e., emergent bilingual students), and students receiving special
education services than projected, and participants strongly questioned whether the benefits and
burdens that SEGC changed brought about were distributed equitably.

Specifically, for each proposed closure, consolidation, program move, or boundary change
option, PPS should provide a clear impact summary that shows the number and percentage of
students affected, disaggregated by race and ethnicity, multilingual learner status, special
education status, and poverty indicators such as FRPL, where available. PPS should also identify
which students are participating in key programs, so the district can transparently show how
proposed changes may shift access to specialized offerings and supports across student groups.
This can honestly be a dashboard. Additionally, as the district develops criteria for well-
resourced schools, those criteria should be used as a filter through which all scenarios might be
assessed.

Build scenarios with principals before the public rollout
Building scenarios with principals before a public rollout might strengthen both the quality and
legitimacy of district decision-making. During our qualitative data collection, principals often
suggested that they understand building-level consequences and community dynamics in ways
that broad, centralized processes can miss. At the same time, PPS should acknowledge openly
that principals may have competing interests, including understandable protectiveness over their
                                                                                                                              72


school’s enrollment, staffing, reputation, and the possibility of closure or consolidation. That
territoriality does not undermine the entire process, but it does create a predictable incentive
structure and an emotional reality when people are responsible for a community they love.
Perhaps, the district can address this by setting clear norms and guardrails up front and ground
scenario work in shared equity goals and transparent criteria, and pair principal input with trusted
community partners and equity accountability checks so that scenario-building does not become
something that is viewed as “behind-the-scenes” by community and district stakeholders.

Define “well-resourced schools” through a concrete, funded, and feasible staffing model
PPS should clearly define what it means by a “well-resourced school” before using that phrase to
justify boundary changes, consolidation, closure, or other rightsizing decisions. While the
definition can be aspirational,44 it should also specify the staffing, services, programs, and
learning conditions that students and families should be able to expect at each grade level. At
minimum, this should include expectations for classroom staffing, specialists, intervention
supports, counseling and mental health supports, special education services, multilingual learner
supports, enrichment and electives, planning time, leadership capacity, and family/community
engagement supports (we discuss this in greater detail in section 6).

The well-resourced schools definition should also be tied to a realistic staffing and resource
model that PPS can fund and sustain for multiple school years (e.g., five-year window). Given
the district’s declining enrollment, PPS should be cautious defining “well-resourced schools” in
ways that depend on one-time adjustments, temporary funds, or enrollment assumptions that may
not hold over time. Instead, the district should identify what it can realistically deliver, what
enrollment levels are needed to sustain those resources, and what additional supports will be
provided if schools fall below those levels because of continued enrollment decline.

If a proposed closure, consolidation, or boundary change is intended to create well-resourced
schools, PPS should be able to demonstrate not only that enrollment numbers improve on paper,
but that students will experience more stable staffing, stronger supports, broader programming,
and more equitable access to high-quality learning environments over time, and specifically
which groups of students might experience this.

Use projection ranges instead of single enrollment estimates
Our preliminary findings indicate that in SEGC-impacted schools’ enrollment projections were
overestimated at several SEGC schools. Several schools were more than 50 students below
projections, and several other schools were more than 20 students above projections. That

Therefore, PPS should show scenarios such as expected enrollment, lower-than-expected

Conduct a cumulative equity burden analysis
The preliminary findings from our report are very clear that schools did not experience SEGC as
a single event. They experienced it as layered disruption (including, for example, school
mergers, program relocations, feeder loss, boundary shifts, grade reconfiguration, construction,
pandemic disruption, labor actions, budget cuts, and leadership turnover). Therefore, before
deciding which school communities absorb more change and which school communities have
experienced the most burdens of change, PPS should identify which schools have already
experienced disruptions such as prior boundary changes, program relocations, grade
reconfigurations, leadership turnover, staff reductions, feeder pattern loss, high staff turnover,
etc. This could also be a map or dashboard.

Pre and Post Rightsizing Decision(s)

Develop a robust, funded transition plan prior to any Board vote that spells out what
implementation and multi-year supports will look like in practice
Our preliminary findings suggest that PPS could create a transition plan that includes time and
space for new leadership, staff to build community and instructional and planning routines. In
addition, the plan might include student transition activities, family orientation and navigation
support, multilingual communications, transportation planning, continuity of special education
services, support for multilingual learners, staffing and scheduling plans, culture, belonging, and
community-building strategies. Again, our preliminary findings suggest that many schools felt
left to invent transition supports on their own, and this support is designed to make sure that does
not happen again.

Ongoing per Rightsizing Decision(s)

Support learning environments with staffing reviews and rapid adjustments
PPS should conduct a recurring enrollment, staffing, and class size review cycle at key intervals
after implementation of rightsizing, including before school starts, after the first 30 school days,
after the first quarter, and at midyear. Each review should assess staff and school needs as well
                                                                                                  74


as compare actual enrollment and student need to projections and to clearly defined thresholds,
and it should trigger real time adjustments when conditions exceed planned capacity. If class
sizes, special education caseloads, multilingual learner needs, intervention access, or behavior
and climate needs exceed planned thresholds, additional staffing, coaching, student supports, or
schedule changes should be provided in whatever capacities PPS can. This is important because
it can help the district create a critical internal feedback loop that supports staff and learning
environments in the period when they are most fragile.

Visit schools, implement monitoring and feedback loops to build trust and public
accountability
This recommendation comes directly from our preliminary finding that trust was weakened not
only by the decisions themselves, but by how communities experienced the process over time.
Across our data collection, participants described broken promises, weak follow-through,
inconsistent communication, and limited visibility into whether concerns raised by schools and
communities were ever acted upon. The evidence suggests that these weak feedback loops made
the process feel less fair, less transparent, and less responsive, which in turn intensified mistrust
and made implementation harder. In this sense, we view monitoring and feedback loops as a core
trust-building strategy. This could be progress and feedback loops on measures that are important
to schools, the district, and families.

      Perspectives of Families and Caregivers of Students in SEGC Impacted Schools

Family Perspectives Across SEGC-Impacted School Communities
This section draws on cross-school focus groups with families to better understand how they
experienced SEGC-related changes. Our data includes 11 family focus group conversations,
including approximately 35 parent and caregiver participants whose children were currently or
formerly enrolled in schools affected by SEGC or related Southeast school changes.

The conversations included families whose native languages are English, Chinese, Vietnamese,
and Spanish-language or bilingual Spanish-English. Families represented a wide range of school
experiences, including children who remained in their schools, children who moved schools as
SEGC changes were implemented, children who participated in Spanish or Mandarin Dual
Language Immersion pathways, children receiving special education services, and children
whose elementary, middle, or high school pathways crossed multiple schools before, during, and
after SEGC-related shifts.

The schools represented through caregivers’ direct student experiences or school pathways
included Lent, Bridger/Creative Science, Kellogg, Harrison Park, Woodstock, Creston, Hosford,
Franklin, Cleveland, Glencoe, Lewis, Arleta, Sellwood, Roseway Heights, Winterhaven, Vestal,
and one unspecified PPS K–8 to K–5 transition. Families also discussed Atkinson, Marysville,
Grout, Whitman, Lane, Mount Tabor, McDaniel (high school), and other schools as
consequential to SEGC-related boundaries, feeder patterns, program pathways, transportation,
special education placement, or future enrollment decisions. Because several caregivers
described children’s movement across multiple schools, these school connections should be
understood as overlapping rather than mutually exclusive.

Overall, families described SEGC not only as a boundary or program process, but as a set of
changes that affected daily routines, transportation, class size, peer relationships, school identity,
after-school access, sports participation, special education access, DLI pathways, and long-term
feeder patterns. Families also described some real gains, especially where schools built stronger
DLI communities or more welcoming middle school environments. However, those gains often
existed alongside frustration with the process, unresolved pathway issues, transportation burdens,
class size instability, and perceived inequities in whose communities were asked to absorb the
most change.

Families Described Learning Environments as More Crowded, Less Stable, and Less
Supported
Across school communities, families described learning environments as affected by class size
increases, staffing instability, reduced services, and inconsistent access to supports. In some
schools, families described a pattern of small classes one year and much larger classes the next,
                                                                                                   77


or stable staffing one year and significant reductions the next. Families connected these
fluctuations to enrollment shifts, budget pressures, and boundary decisions that they believed left
some schools under-enrolled and vulnerable to cuts.

Several families described reduced access to trusted adults, specialists, counselors, library
services, intervention support, after-school offerings, or special education-related supports.
These reductions were especially concerning when students had IEPs, sensory needs, language
needs, or other support needs that required predictable adult relationships and smaller learning
environments. In these accounts, learning environment quality was not defined only by
curriculum. It was also defined by whether students could access consistent adults, reasonable
class sizes, supports, electives, and stable routines.

Families also described a self-reinforcing cycle in some schools: boundaries or enrollment shifts
reduced enrollment, reduced enrollment led to staffing cuts, staffing cuts made the school appear
less attractive or less stable, and that instability risked further enrollment loss. This concern was
especially pronounced in family accounts related to Creston and other smaller neighborhood
schools, where families worried that enrollment loss, staffing reductions, and declining program
access could make future closure or consolidation seem inevitable. Parents stated:

  “There was a reduction in available services at the school…and an increase in class sizes.”

 “There might be a single third grade at Creston next year…my kid’s kindergarten was 15 kids,
 first grade was like 16 kids…and then moving to a 35 kid third grade class feels unnecessary.”

Families Described DLI as Both a Source of Belonging and a Fragile Commitment
Families across Spanish and Mandarin DLI pathways described dual language immersion as a
major source of belonging, cultural affirmation, academic value, and long-term commitment.
Several families described DLI as the reason they chose a school, stayed through disruption,
accepted longer commutes, or remained committed to PPS. Families connected DLI not only to
language learning, but also to multicultural communities, cross-cultural relationships, and the
kind of city they hoped their children would help build.

Several families described Lent’s whole-school Spanish DLI model as a meaningful gain,
especially because Spanish was more visible and central throughout the school, multicultural
programming was stronger, bilingual support staff were present, and families could see a clearer
schoolwide identity around Spanish language and culture. At the same time, those families
described the process of getting to that model as confusing, inequitable, and under-supported.
Families raised concerns about long bus rides, late transportation decisions, crowded classrooms,
unclear registration, leadership instability, insufficient transition staffing, and promises that were
not fully honored.
                                                                                                78


Mandarin DLI families raised related but distinct concerns. They described Woodstock as a
school where the Mandarin program and neighborhood program had coexisted in a culturally
meaningful way. Families described the shift to Harrison Park as creating a long commute,
fragmenting relationships, and leading some Mandarin-speaking families to leave the program.
Some families felt PPS described the move as serving Chinese-speaking families without
sufficiently understanding distinctions among Chinese language communities or listening to the
families most affected.

Across DLI communities, families described a gap between the commitment PPS asks families
to make and the commitment families felt PPS made in return. Families are often told that DLI is
a long-term K–12 commitment. Yet some experienced SEGC as a disruption of that very
commitment. Parents said:

      “It just feels like you asked us to commit to something that you’re not committed to.”

          “The whole school DLI is great…but the process…was a little convoluted.”

       “Whole school DLI is great. It needs to be funded, and it needs to be prioritized.”

Families Described Transportation, Geography, and After-School Access as Core Equity
Issues
Families described students spending significant time on buses or in cars, children getting up
much earlier than siblings, long commutes replacing walkable neighborhood routines, and
students being assigned to schools farther from home even when another school was much
closer. Families also described safety concerns connected to major roads, long walking distances
to bus stops, traffic conditions near schools, and students traveling home in the dark during
winter months.

Transportation also affected access to the full school experience. Families described after-care,
sports, and electives as harder to access when students attended schools far from home or when
middle school sports were tied to high school clusters rather than the middle school students
actually attended. Several families described the current sports and feeder arrangements as
especially difficult for Creston, Hosford, Franklin, Cleveland, Woodstock, Harrison Park, and
other pathway-affected students. In these accounts, SEGC-related changes did not only
determine where students went during the school day. They shaped whether students could
participate in after-school activities, maintain friendships, and fully belong to the school
community. Participants commented:

   “My children get on the bus at 7:06 a.m.…they are on the bus for a very, very long time.”
                                                                                                 79


“The split feeder pattern at the middle school has made middle school sports almost impossible
                                 for many kids to participate in.”

“Kids are missing out on sports because this boundary issue was never resolved.”

Families Described Loss of Cohort, Friendship, and Community Continuity as a Major
Harm
Families repeatedly emphasized that school transitions affected children’s friendships, peer
groups, parent networks, and sense of community. Several caregivers described children leaving
elementary school cohorts, entering middle school with only one or a few familiar peers, or
having to choose between friends assigned to different high schools. Families often described
these impacts as emotionally significant, particularly for middle school students, for whom peer
belonging was viewed as central to well-being.

Families described some students having to rebuild friendships after years of community
formation, while others were separated again at the high school level because earlier feeder
questions were not resolved. Families also described parent community disruption, including the
loss of walkable relationships, weaker parent networks, and difficulty accessing directories or
communication tools needed to rebuild community. Parents stated:

   “The hardest part for our family was losing our community. My son basically lost all of his
                            friendships. We lost our parent group.”

  “We just want our kids to stay with their friends. And I think that’s pretty reasonable to ask.”

                          “It felt like we almost moved to a new place.”

Families Perceived Inequitable Burdens Across Schools and Communities
Across language groups and school communities, families perceived that SEGC-related
disruption was not distributed equitably. Families repeatedly described Title I schools, schools in
the outer Southeast, DLI families, immigrant families, families with fewer resources, and
students receiving special education services as carrying greater burdens. By contrast, they
perceived some more affluent or politically powerful communities as being more protected from
major changes.

Families described this not only as an outcome issue, but as a process issue. Several participants
believed that families with more time, legal resources, English fluency, political connections, or
ability to attend multiple meetings had more influence over final decisions. Families also noted
that the communities most affected by change are often the least able to attend evening meetings,
                                                                                                80


track multiple scenarios, interpret technical documents, or advocate repeatedly across a long
process. Participants described their perceptions:

“The people who can afford the time and the resources to be there are the ones who are heard.”

 “An interpreter is just checking an equity and inclusion box if you don’t do anything with what
                                        you’re hearing.”

                       “If you’re going to do equity, you got to do it all.”

Families Said Trust Depends on Clear Communication, Real Co-Design, Follow-Through,
and Repair
Families described communication as confusing, circular, delayed, and often mediated through
PTA networks or parent-to-parent communication rather than clear district systems. Some
families said they had difficulty tracking which scenario was current, what had been decided,
what remained open, and what the implications would be for transportation, staffing, high school
pathways, or after-school access. Others said they did not hear about important issues until
decisions had already been made.

Families also described mistrust when district promises or expectations did not materialize. This
included concerns about transition staffing, transportation, community-building support, after-
care, unresolved feeder patterns, and promised follow-up phases. Several families emphasized
that the process does not end when the Board votes or when students are reassigned. The district
must continue supporting the transition for multiple years and must track whether the intended
benefits actually occur.

According to participants, PPS should not only communicate better but should create ongoing
mechanisms for course correction. Families want clear criteria, transparent data, targeted
outreach to affected communities, multilingual engagement, youth and family voice, and
evidence that input changes decisions. They also want district leaders to account for cumulative
disruption, asking not only whether a proposed change makes sense on paper, but how much
change a given school community has already absorbed. Parents said:

                               “It felt like they weren’t listening.”

 “We only found out late about possible changes. We want more time and real options, not just
                             being told what’s already decided.”

          “They need to know that the process isn’t over once they make the change.”
                                                                                               81


 “The process isn’t over once they make the change… they can’t walk away and not support the
                              transition for the subsequent years.”

Summary of Cross-School Family Findings
Taken together, the family data show that SEGC-related changes were experienced not only
through where students were assigned, but through whether they could remain with peers, access
after-school activities, receive services, participate in sports, maintain language pathways, and
trust that the district would finish the work it started. Families described some real gains,
especially at Lent and Harrison Park, where several caregivers praised school staff, school
community, DLI programming, and middle school opportunities. However, these gains existed
alongside frustration with the process, unresolved pathway issues, transportation burdens, class
size instability, and perceived inequities in whose communities were asked to absorb the most
change.

Overall, the cross-school family findings are less about a narrow story about boundaries and
programs and more of a broader story about the lived infrastructure of school change:
transportation, staffing, class size, cohort continuity, language pathways, after-school access,
special education support, family communication, and trust. Families’ central message was that
future district changes must be evaluated not only by whether enrollment looks more balanced on
paper, but by whether students and families experience the change as stable, supported, fair, and
humanizing.

                                       School-Specific Family Perspectives

The school-specific family perspectives below summarize the schools and student pathways that
were discussed most directly across the family focus groups and interviews. These summaries
are not intended to provide a comprehensive family analysis of every SEGC-impacted school.
Rather, they highlight the schools that were named by participating parents and caregivers,
represented through their children’s current or former enrollment, or discussed because they
were directly connected to families’ SEGC-related experiences, including school moves, DLI
pathways, transportation, feeder patterns, special education placement, or access to programs and
supports. Since family participation was not evenly distributed across all schools, the depth of
discussion varies by school. For this reason, these school-specific summaries should be read as
illustrative family perspectives from the 11 family focus groups, not as representative findings
for every family or every school community. Finally, our findings here do not include all 20
SECG-impacted schools but, again, only the schools that were discussed during our data
collection.45

Arleta
Families described Arleta as a school that had lost significant resources and staff after SEGC-
related changes. They pointed to reductions in counselor time, support roles, and building-level
leadership capacity as examples of how the school’s support had been weakened. Families also
described a loss of racial diversity and community strength, connecting enrollment drops and
resource cuts to disproportionate impacts on historically marginalized communities.

Arleta was also discussed through the lens of possible future closure. Families expressed fear
that the school was being allowed to wither, which reinforced perceptions of inequity. In these
accounts, Arleta was not described as simply experiencing normal enrollment change. Rather,
families saw the school as part of a broader pattern in which schools serving more historically
underserved communities lose resources, become less stable, and then are treated as candidates
for additional disruption.

Atkinson
Families discussed Atkinson as a consequential school in the SEGC process, particularly in
relation to Spanish DLI, neighborhood boundaries, and perceptions that some school
communities had more influence over final decisions than others. Some families described
Atkinson as a school that retained a Spanish DLI strand while other schools were moved toward
whole-school DLI models or experienced more substantial program and boundary changes.
Families perceived this as inconsistent with the district’s stated rationale that immersion strands
should be consolidated or that similar program rules should apply across schools.

Lent
Families connected to Lent described one of the most nuanced sets of experiences in the family
data. On one hand, several caregivers described Lent’s whole-school Spanish DLI model as a
meaningful gain. Families spoke positively about hearing more Spanish throughout the school,
stronger multicultural programming, bilingual support staff, culturally affirming events, and a
growing sense that Lent had become a more coherent Spanish immersion school community.
Some families described their children as happy, well-supported, and thriving at Lent, and
several praised the school’s current direction, staff, and community-building efforts.

At the same time, families described the process of getting to that model as confusing,
inequitable, and under-supported. Families who moved from Bridger to Lent described losing
walkable access to school, longer commutes, early bus times, transportation uncertainty, and a
loss of neighborhood connection. Some caregivers described students riding buses for long
periods or families spending substantial time driving children to and from school. Families also
described concerns about larger class sizes after the move, including classrooms that became
difficult to physically navigate and students with IEPs or sensory needs receiving less
individualized attention in larger settings.

Families raised concerns about promised supports that did not fully materialize. Several
caregivers described additional transition staffing or funding as something that had been
promised or expected but then reduced, delayed, or pulled back because of budget constraints.
Families also described early implementation challenges, including unclear registration, late
confirmation of enrollment, lack of a permanent teacher at the start of kindergarten, leadership
instability, and limited district promotion of Lent as a Spanish DLI option. In these accounts,
Lent was both a place where families saw authentic programmatic and cultural strengths and a

place where families felt PPS did not provide the level of transition support needed to make the
change as successful and equitable as it could have been. Parents stated:

        “Whole school DLI is great. It needs to be funded, and it needs to be prioritized.”

Marysville
Marysville was discussed by families as a school affected by SEGC-related displacement and
receiving-school dynamics, particularly in relation to Lent neighborhood students who did not
enroll in Lent’s Spanish DLI program. Some families described neighborhood students who were
reassigned away from Lent and into Marysville or other schools, emphasizing that those students
and families experienced major disruption even if they were not always the most visible voices
in the process.

Families also raised concerns about whether Marysville families and Lent neighborhood families
were adequately heard before decisions were finalized. Several participants described a
perception that Spanish-speaking families, emergent bilingual families, and families from lower-
income communities were not fully informed or centered in decision-making. During one family
focus group, Marysville was named in connection with students and families who had to cross
major roads or adjust routines after being reassigned.

Marysville also surfaced through secondhand family accounts about receiving students from
Lent and concerns that some existing families interpreted the arrival of new students through
deficit-based or stigmatizing narratives. While these are not direct testimony from Marysville’s
students or staff, they point to a broader implementation lesson. The lesson is that schools need
intentional support for community-building, anti-bias and antiracism work, and shared identity
formation when new student groups arrive.

Lewis
Families described Lewis as a strong academic and community environment that supports
students well, yet they felt it was undervalued and under-resourced by the district. Parents
praised the school’s academic quality and sense of community but argued that district decisions
that impacted the school through SEGC changes did not adequately recognize or protect what
was working.

Post-SEGC changes, families also raised concerns about large class sizes and the loss of staff and
services, including supports for students receiving special education services. They pointed to
inequities in class size caps between focus schools and neighborhood schools, arguing that Lewis
and similar schools should not be punished or neglected because they are doing well. In their
view, Lewis reflected a larger concern that high-performing neighborhood schools serving

racially, socially, and culturally diverse communities can still be weakened by boundary
changes, staffing reductions, and inequitable resource allocation. One parent noted:

“Lewis is a great school with strong academics and community, but the district’s decisions don’t
                    reflect that. It feels like they don’t value what’s working.”

Woodstock
Families connected to Woodstock described the school as a place where the Mandarin DLI
program and neighborhood program had historically coexisted in a meaningful way. Some
caregivers described Woodstock as a school community where families valued both language
learning and broader intercultural connection. For these families, the school was not only a
program site, but a community where DLI and neighborhood students interacted and where
families had built long-standing relationships.

Families described the shift caused by SEGC changes of the Mandarin DLI middle school
pathway away from Hosford and toward Harrison Park as highly disruptive. Some caregivers
described Mandarin-speaking families leaving the program after the change, either because they
did not feel heard or because the new pathway did not match what they had committed to when
they entered the program. Families also described large class sizes and reduced sections in the
elementary Mandarin program after enrollment declined, with one account describing a shift
from two smaller sections to one much larger class and fewer dedicated language and English
teaching supports.

Families raised concerns that PPS framed the move as beneficial for Chinese-speaking families
without fully understanding distinctions among Chinese language communities or listening
carefully to affected families. In these accounts, Woodstock became a key example of how a
district may intend to strengthen DLI access, but still produce harm if it misreads community
geography, language identity, school culture, and families’ reasons for choosing a pathway.

Harrison Park
Families discussed Harrison Park in several different ways. For some families, Harrison Park
became a receiving middle school for Mandarin DLI students or students reassigned through
boundary and feeder changes. Some caregivers described the transition to Harrison Park as
difficult because it involved a longer commute, fewer familiar peers, and challenges building
parent networks. Families described students entering middle school with only a small group
from their prior school or without a strong connection to the new school community.

At the same time, several families spoke positively about Harrison Park as a school. Caregivers
praised the school’s teachers, staff, remodeled facility, welcoming student-led transition
activities, and the middle school opportunities students eventually experienced. Some families
                                                                                                  86


said their children came to like Harrison Park more than expected and found strong support
there. These positive accounts are important because they show that families could
simultaneously critique the district process and appreciate the school that received their children.

Still, families identified unresolved implementation challenges. These included long bus rides,
late or inefficient transportation routes, difficulty accessing parent directories or family contact
information, limited support for rebuilding community, and sports or activity barriers tied to high
school feeder clusters. Families also described Harrison Park as managing multiple high school
feeder patterns, which made communication, athletics, and future transitions more complex for
small cohorts.

Kellogg
Families discussed Kellogg primarily through Spanish DLI, middle school pathway, and feeder
pattern experiences. Some parents described Kellogg as the middle school serving students from
Lent or other Spanish DLI pathways, and some families raised concerns about the school’s
earlier climate and behavioral history. At the same time, families also noted that the school
climate had improved over time and that leadership and staff were working to build a stronger
sense of community.

Kellogg also appeared in relation to neighborhood assignment and proximity. Some families
living near Kellogg described frustration that their assigned neighborhood middle school was
farther away, which required their students to pass a closer school to attend another one. These
comments point to a broader family concern about the relationship between program pathways,
neighborhood boundaries, school proximity, and daily family logistics.

Families also raised concerns about access to full DLI programming at the middle school level.
Some caregivers expressed that the DLI pathway needs to be treated as a full K–12 program,
with stronger articulation from elementary to middle school and from middle school to high
school. In these accounts, Kellogg was not only a school destination, but part of a larger program
pipeline that families believed PPS needed to support more coherently.

Creston
Families described Creston as a small neighborhood school that, through SEGC changes, had
been affected by unresolved feeder patterns, enrollment imbalance, and staffing reductions.
Some caregivers described Creston as a strong and beloved school that nevertheless felt like it
was being weakened over time by boundary decisions and declining enrollment brought about by
SEGC changes.

Families raised concerns that Creston students were moved from a K–8 structure into new
middle school pathways, but that the district did not fully complete the follow-up work needed to
                                                                                                 87


resolve high school pathways, athletics participation, or cohort continuity. Some caregivers
described Creston students moving together to Hosford for middle school but then facing splits
between Franklin and Cleveland for high school. Families experienced this as disruptive because
students built friendships in middle school and then faced separation again when moving to high
school.

Families also connected Creston’s enrollment decline to staffing cuts. They described the loss of
beloved educators, reductions in services, and the possibility of much larger class sizes in future

“There might be a single third grade at Creston next year… my kid’s kindergarten was 15 kids,

staffing levels, but also about the long-term consequences of repeated reductions. In their view,
protecting Creston requires interrupting the cycle in which declining enrollment leads to fewer
resources, fewer resources weaken the school’s appeal, and weakened appeal further threatens
enrollment.

Hosford
Families discussed Hosford mainly through the lens of Creston students’ middle school and high

generally positive as a middle school experience. They emphasized that the school staff did what
they could within the structure they were given.

The concerns about Hosford centered less on the school’s internal quality and more on the policy
environment around it. Families described confusion and frustration that Creston students
attending Hosford were still assigned to Franklin for high school, while many of their Hosford
peers were assigned to Cleveland. This created uncertainty and emotional stress for students who
had built friendships in middle school but faced future separation at the high school transition.

Families also described middle school sports and athletics access as a major issue. Because some
sports were organized around high school clusters rather than the middle school students
attended, caregivers said students could not easily participate with their Hosford peers. Families
described this as especially harmful for students whose social belonging depended on
participating with friends. As one parent noted:

  “We just want our kids to stay with their friends. And I think that’s pretty reasonable to ask.”

Bridger Creative Science School
Families connected to Bridger Creative Science described multiple layers of change. Some
Spanish DLI families described moving from Bridger to Lent and experiencing a significant loss

and SEGC added another layer of instability.

Families also described Bridger and Creative Science as sites where school identity and program

Families also discussed the use of portables and mixed messages about whether buildings could

rationale for changing configurations yet later continued to be used. In these accounts, Bridger
Creative Science became an example of how families experienced district communication as
inconsistent or difficult to trust.

Clark Building
The Clark building appeared in family accounts primarily through its connection to Creative

middle school students as feeling marginalized, especially when the school culture or
programming became more oriented toward K–5 students.

Families also described climate concerns, including a perception that the environment became
more punitive or compliance oriented. These concerns were especially salient for neurodivergent
students and students who benefited from restorative or relationship-based approaches. The
Clark building accounts therefore highlight that school reconfiguration is not only about where

Roseway Heights appeared in family accounts connected to student transition, support networks,

Lane
Lane appeared in family accounts mainly as a middle school connected to boundary shifts,

Summary of School-Specific Family Perspectives
Across these school-specific accounts, families described SEGC-related changes as varied,
layered, and highly consequential for daily life. Some schools and programs were experienced as
real gains, particularly where families saw stronger DLI identity, welcoming staff, improved
middle school opportunities, or more cohesive school cultures. At the same time, families
repeatedly described unresolved harms, including transportation burdens, loss of peer cohorts,
class size instability, staffing reductions, unclear communication, special education access
barriers, and a sense that some school communities were protected while others were expected to
absorb repeated disruption.

Altogether, and again, the school-specific family perspectives show that future enrollment and
program decisions should not be evaluated only by whether the district creates more balanced
numbers on paper. Families want PPS to examine how changes actually land in specific school
communities and student pathways. That means attending to transportation, class size, staffing,
DLI continuity, special education placement, after-school access, sports participation, peer
continuity, language access, and the amount of prior disruption each school community has
already experienced. Future district processes will need to pair technical enrollment planning
with school-specific implementation supports and visible accountability for whether students and
families experience the changes as stable, fair, and humane.

     Family and Community Voice, Engagement, and Decision-Making of SEGC Members

Building trust with families and community members during SEGC was a complex and
consequential task. SEGC members were asked to participate in a process connected to balancing
enrollment in the southeast part of the district.

Family and community voices are not peripheral to equitable district decision-making. They are
foundational to whether district decisions are experienced as legitimate, responsive, and worthy
of trust. Family engagement research frames families as full partners in their children’s
education, not simply recipients of information or audiences for decisions that have already been
made (Ishimaru, 2019; Mapp & Bergman, 2019).46 47 This is especially important for families
and communities that have been historically excluded from district decisions, including those
families who have experienced limited access to timely information, language access barriers,
racial marginalization, economic exclusion, or prior harm in school systems.

As such, equitable engagement requires more than outreach, public comment, or compliance
with required processes (Ishimaru, 2019). It requires relationship-building, transparent
communication, attention to power imbalances, and meaningful opportunities for families and
trusted community advocates to shape the decisions that affect their children, schools, and
neighborhoods (Ishimaru, 2019; Ed Trust, 2024).48

In this section, we draw on data gathered through SEGC member interviews, focus groups,
surveys, meeting materials, and related documents (e.g., board documents, notes from prior
consulting firms, etc.) to better understand how members experienced voice, engagement, and
decision-making throughout the process.

In this chapter, we specifically address the following questions:
    1) In what ways did SEGC members feel their input was heard, valued, and reflected in final
         decisions, if any?

     2) How can future district efforts, especially those related to enrollment and program
        changes, better build trust and support meaningful engagement with families and
        communities?

Analytic Note on Lived Experience, Observation, and Participant Perception
In our analysis, we distinguished between participants direct lived experiences and observations,
such as what they saw, did, heard, experienced, or recalled from the SEGC process, and their
broader perceptions or interpretations of what those experiences meant. This distinction matters
because perceptions are often grounded in lived experience, but they may also reflect
participants’ efforts to make sense of patterns, motives, decisions, and power dynamics that
extend beyond any single moment or meeting.

The claims in this section that describe specific emotions, moments, actions, or process details
are grounded in the direct accounts shared by SEGC members that we were able to speak to
during the focus groups and interview. These include members describing not feeling heard,
feeling that their time had been wasted, recounting voting experiences, receiving data shortly
before decisions were made, abstaining from the final vote, believing that some schools were
positioned for closure, or naming ways their own school communities were affected by
enrollment changes. These accounts draw on participants’ own words and detailed recollections
of meetings, votes, letters of dissent, data requests, communication challenges, and school
community impacts.

At the same time, some findings in this section reflect participants’ broader interpretations of the
SEGC process, not only discrete events that can be confirmed through administrative records or
formal documentation. For example, when members described their influence as limited,
characterized certain outcomes as feeling predetermined, named barriers between SEGC
members and district decision makers, or described responsiveness as occurring only after
significant resistance, we understand these accounts as meaningful evidence of how participants
experienced and made sense of the process. These characterizations synthesize patterns across
data collection sessions and should not be read as claims that every SEGC member had the same
experience. Rather, they illuminate how participants interpreted district motives, Board
dynamics, decision-making patterns, and the overall credibility of the process.

 Actionable Recommendations Based on SEGC Members’ Experiences to (Re)Build Trust
         and Support Meaningful Engagement with Families and Communities?

Drawing on the experiences of SEGC members, participants’ experiences (both directly and
indirectly) offered a set of concrete recommendations for how future district efforts, especially
enrollment and program changes, might build trust and support meaningful, equitable
engagement.

Clearly define the purpose, scope, and tradeoffs before launching future district change
processes and groups

First, participants stressed the importance of clear, stable goals and scope, and of honoring those
commitments throughout the process. Members believed one of the fundamental problems early
on in SEGC was that the overarching goal was never fully clarified or was set incorrectly. Some
participants argued that, given widespread under-enrollment, consolidation should have been an
explicit target, but instead the work devolved into, what one participant described as “mitigating
the impact of an already flawed plan.” As one survey respondent framed it:

“In the previous SEGC process, when district level administrators were asked if they had a goal,
 mission, or desired outcome for the SEGC process the initial response was that they wanted to
    hear from families. Although that sounds good in public venues, it also displays a lack of
                   direction, leadership, and compass (moral and logistical).”

Participants noted that when high schools and inner SE schools were removed from
consideration, it directly contradicted the stated aim of addressing district-wide equity. For future
efforts, they argued that the district should explicitly define its goals (for example, consolidation
versus boundary change or boundary fine-tuning), commit to considering all schools, and avoid
closing off parts of the city midstream in the process. When constraints or changes are
unavoidable, the district should provide a transparent rationale rather than simply announcing a
narrowed scope.

Relatedly, members urged the district to be transparent with communities about the real trade-
offs and be honest about pros and cons of future decisions. Participants described their own
attempts, within SEGC, to generate alternative scenarios that each prioritized different values
(such as balanced enrollment, proximity, or stability, etc.) and to compare outcomes. One
member argued that the district should adopt a similar approach with the broader public:

 “Trust people to understand tradeoffs. When you provide options, give a real accounting of the
 pros and cons. Show one scenario that prioritizes balanced enrollment, another that prioritizes
                                                                                                 100


 proximity, and so on. Then people can see the tradeoffs and understand why a certain path was
                              chosen, even if it's not their ideal.”

  “Find ways to meaningfully include family input and then name how that input is included in
                                         decisions.”

Participants felt that this kind of candid comparison would help families see that there is no
“perfect” option, and that the chosen path reflects a reasoned judgment rather than hidden
agendas.

Use clear, direct communication and consistent data to strengthen public trust
A second major recommendation concerned communication style and consistency. Participants
described district communications as overly long, abstract, and lacking in concrete information.
They criticized what one participant called “gobbledygook” which includes dense messages that
used many words but offered little clarity. This problem, they noted, was already repeating itself
in communications about new right-sizing work. One member summarized:

“The statements say so many words and say so little. Families are supposed to sit down and read
 a wall of words with almost no actual information. Say what you mean, be honest with people.
             They can handle it. They just want information. Give them the truth.”

Participants also highlighted the damage done when district representatives presented enrollment
and capacity figures in public that did not match the numbers SEGC members had been working
with. This inconsistency, participants said, undermined credibility for both school representatives
and the district. The recommendation here is that the district must make sure its data is internally
aligned before it is shared in public and must correct any discrepancies openly and quickly.

Design engagement so committees and families can access, understand, and influence the
process from the beginning
Third, SEGC members who participated in this research emphasized that engagement for
committees and families must be accessible, equitable, and genuinely influential. They pointed
out that while the district did eventually provide direct access to data consultants and
sophisticated tools, this happened far too late in the process and only under heavy pressure. For
future processes, they recommended making such tools and expertise available from the
beginning, alongside support that helps non-technical participants understand and apply the
information. This implies offering training, clear explanations, and interactive opportunities to
see how proposed changes would affect different schools and communities, rather than simply
delivering static spreadsheets or dense reports.
                                                                                                101


Participants also called for reducing the barriers that kept many families from engaging
meaningfully. They noted that the volume and complexity of materials required substantial free
time, literacy, and technology access, which effectively excluded many caregivers who worked
multiple jobs, had younger children, or lacked relevant professional experience. Important to
note, a large portion of the SEGC work took place during COVID-19, which required many
meetings to be held online. However, participants suggested providing shorter, multilingual
explainers, visual maps, and summaries, as well as offering stipends (might not be possible with
the district’s current fiscal conditions) or supports such as childcare, and using varied
engagement formats, including small-group sessions and more informal settings. The goal, they
argued, is to design processes that do not only elevate the voices of people with the most time
and confidence. One survey respondent suggested:

“Necesitan tener representantes presente en la escuela, tener juntas en Español, mandar folletos
  a casa con mucho tiempo en anticipación de cambios.” (They need to have representatives
  present in schools, to have meetings in Spanish, to send pamphlets home with plenty of time
                                  before anticipated changes.)

Similarly, another recurring recommendation was to hold more, and earlier, listening sessions
that focus on open-ended input rather than reaction to pre-baked plans. Members recalled that
prior community sessions often came after the district had already developed a preferred
scenario, so families were essentially being asked, “Do you have thoughts about this plan?”
rather than, “What are your priorities before we draft anything?” One participant reflected:

“It felt like community sessions only happened once there was already something being worked
with. It wasn't, ‘Come and give us your feedback’ at the start. It was, ‘Here's the plan. What do
                                        you think of it?’”

To build trust, they argued, the district should engage communities early in defining problems
and values and then return with drafts that explicitly show how initial feedback has been
incorporated, or, where it has not, why that is the case.

Apply the racial equity and social justice lens through concrete decisions, not symbolic
language
A fourth set of recommendations revolved around applying the equity lens substantively, not
symbolically. Many participants were clear that the district’s equity language alone did not build
trust. In fact, repeating an equity statement at every meeting while excluding inner southeast
schools and focusing changes on outer southeast made the lens feel hollow. One member
captured this dynamic:
                                                                                                 102


“They kept reading the racial equity and social justice lens at the beginning of each meeting, but
once those inner southeast schools were off the table, it just felt like a ritual. We kept saying the
 words, even though what we were doing was just shuffling the most under-resourced students
                                         and schools.”

Other participants said that they warned about equity language being used selectively to protect
specific programs or communities, rather than to center the needs of students who have been
historically marginalized. They recommended that future efforts tie the equity lens concretely to
which schools and student groups are included in the conversation, and that the district publish
clear equity impact analyses for each scenario. They also urged the district to prioritize direct
voices from affected communities over interpretations by more advantaged advocates “speaking
for” them.

Honor promised supports and create public accountability for follow-through
Fifth, participants underscored the need for PPS to follow through on promises and maintain
continuity of responsibility. They pointed to specific commitments that, in their perception, were
not honored, most notably additional staffing for schools negatively impacted by SEGC changes.
One participant described working at a school that had been promised a set amount of extra
staffing as part of the SEGC plan, only to find that leaders had to apply annually for a portion of
those positions and were repeatedly denied:

“They promised additional staffing to impacted schools and then backtracked. Our principal had
 to apply to be allowed to request what we were supposedly guaranteed. Year after year she was
    denied most of it. If you promise that support as part of a plan, schools should just get it.”

Participants also linked unfulfilled promises to leadership transitions. They noted that a key
central office leader was retiring at the end of the process, and they felt that promises made under
that leader were not carried forward with the same level of ownership. This left them with the
impression that certain assurances had been used to secure their cooperation on the final plan,
without strong mechanisms to ensure delivery afterward. Overall, participants’ recommendation
is that the district should only promise supports that are budgeted and structurally embedded, and
should create institutional accountability, through board resolutions, policy, and public tracking,
so commitments can survive changes in personnel.

Make courageous, student-centered decisions that prioritize equity over political comfort
Sixth, several participants spoke about the importance of PPS making courageous, student-
centered decisions, even when it angers more privileged, white, affluent, and vocal groups.
Participants observed that previous boards and administrators seemed especially reluctant to
make decisions that would upset affluent communities, and that this avoidance contributed to the
                                                                                                   103


district revisiting the same issues years later. One member suggested that the district must accept
that some families will be unhappy and that this is not, in itself, a sign of failure:

“Maybe they could let people be mad at them. I've never seen an entity so afraid of people being
 mad at them. Some people are going to be mad. The question is whether you're doing the right
                                    thing for students.”

Another participant added that failing to make hard choices during the SEGC process had real
consequences for both equity and stability across many southeast schools. This recommendation
suggests that building trust is not only about designing a more robust engagement process and
communication, but also about the district demonstrating that, when it must choose between
competing interests, it consistently centers students and long-term equity rather than short-term
political comfort. As one survey respondent stated:

“Deberían tomar en cuenta, que para los estudiantes es difícil el cambio de escuelas frecuenté y
       también para los padres. Es difícil retomar confianza en el personal, los maestros y
compañeros.” (They should take into consideration that changing schools frequently is difficult
for students and also for parents. It is difficult to regain trust in staff, teachers, and classmates.)

Support affected schools before, during, and after major enrollment or program changes
Seventh, another recommendation that comes from our conversation with SEGC members is that
major enrollment or program change (including school closures and consolidations) should be
paired with a robust implementation and support plan for affected schools. Participants
highlighted ongoing harms from unbalanced enrollment, staffing instability, transportation
problems, and the erosion of school identities and programs. Future efforts should include
concrete commitments to support transitions before, during, and after the changes. For example,
this might include preserving key program elements where possible, making sure that merged or
relocated schools have real access to libraries and electives, and designing transportation that
supports participation in the full school day and after-school activities. The district should also
plan for emotional and community support (this might include counseling, community-building
events, and targeted family engagement) for students and families whose schools close or are
substantially reconfigured. Eve Ewing, a leading scholar on school closures, refers to this as
institutional mourning to describe the grief, loss, and emotional aftermath communities
experience when a shared institution, such as a neighborhood school, is closed (Ewing, 2018).

Begin future processes by acknowledging past harms and establishing clear community
guardrails
Finally, given the history of repeated disruptive processes and the trust that has already been lost,
future efforts should begin with acknowledgement and visible guardrails. Participants described
decades of shifting configurations and boundary changes in the southeast area of the district and
                                                                                                104


said this most recent process deeply damaged whatever trust remained. Before moving forward
with school closures or consolidations, the district should publicly acknowledge specific past
harms and missteps (such as, according to some participants, ignored dissent in SEGC,
unbalanced outcomes, and perceived inequities in which communities were included), and
articulate how new processes will be different. Co-designing guardrails with community
members (for example, independent facilitation, data transparency measures etc.) can create
visible structures that people can monitor and hold the district accountable to, rather than being
asked simply to trust that the process will be better.

In sum, these recommendations do not guarantee trust or satisfaction, but they respond directly to
the failures and harms that SEGC participants described. Implemented together, they would
represent a concrete shift toward more honest, supported, and collaborative engagement with
families and communities, with the hope of mending areas of broken trust.

    Broader Research Context: Enrollment Balancing, School Closures, Consolidations,

SEGC was not designed or implemented as a school closure initiative. However, it belongs in
conversation with the school closure, consolidation, rightsizing, and district reconfiguration
literature because it involves many of the same core mechanisms through which closure and
consolidation processes affect students, families, staff, and communities. SEGC changed
attendance boundaries, relocated programs, altered feeder patterns, shifted grade configurations,
and redistributed students, staff, programs, and resources across southeast schools. These kinds
of decisions can produce many of the same equity concerns documented in the school closure
literature, including displacement, loss of school community, disproportionate burdens across
racial and socioeconomic groups, changes in access to programs and supports, transportation
challenges, and questions about whether receiving schools are prepared and resourced to serve
students well. For that reason, in this section, we draw on broader national research on school
closures, consolidations, rightsizing, boundary changes, program relocation, and school
reconfiguration to help clarify the equity questions PPS should consider when making future
districtwide changes.

As such, the purpose of this section is twofold.

First, we review research beyond Portland in which districts are responding to declining
enrollment, budget pressure, underutilized buildings, aging facilities, and varied access to
equitable educational opportunities.

Second, this research will provide an interpretive lens that this report will return to when
analyzing the SEGC findings. In later sections, we will situate the PPS findings in conversation
with this research to examine where SEGC reflects broader national patterns, where PPS’s
experience differs, and what lessons could inform future enrollment balancing, rightsizing,
boundary, program relocation, or school reconfiguration decisions.

Finally, in this section, we use the research literature to address several key questions that can
help PPS situate what is happening in the district within a national context and make equitable
future decisions about rightsizing, enrollment balancing, boundary changes, program relocation,
and school reconfiguration. Specifically, we ask:

   1.   Why do districts typically close, consolidate, or reconfigure schools?
   2.   Do school closures or major reconfigurations improve student outcomes?
   3.   Do districts typically save money when they close or consolidate schools?
   4.   Who typically bears the burden of school closures and related reconfiguration decisions?
   5.   What conditions are essential for making school closure, consolidation, rightsizing,
        boundary-change, or program-relocation processes as equitable as possible?
                                                                                                                            107


     6. What does this body of research imply for PPS as we interpret the SEGC findings and
        consider future districtwide changes?

Within each part of this section, we summarize what the research says and then briefly consider
what that evidence means for PPS.

Why Districts Typically Close, Consolidate, or Reconfigure Schools
Across districts in the United States, especially urban districts, school closures and related
rightsizing decisions are typically justified through several overlapping rationales, including (a)
declining enrollment, (b) budget deficits, (c) underused facilities, (d) aging or deteriorating
building conditions, (e) low academic performance, (f) school choice pressures and charter
school growth, and (g) efforts to create stronger or more comprehensive learning environments.49
50 51
      These rationales often appear together, but not always. For example, a district may be losing
students and revenue while also operating more buildings than it can afford, maintaining aging
facilities, and trying to provide a broader (and even more robust) set of academic, enrichment,
intervention, and specialized programs.

However, the empirical literature suggests that these decisions are rarely about enrollment alone.
For example, in districts like Chicago, Philadelphia, New York City, Detroit, and other districts,
closures and reconfiguration efforts have been tied to a combination of underutilization, fiscal
stress, academic accountability, facilities conditions, and broader governance reforms.52 53 54
Each rationale carries different implications. Closing or reconfiguring a school because it is
under-enrolled is not the same as doing so because the building is unsafe, academically low-
performing, or because the district is trying to expand access to stronger or more resourced
programs. When these rationales are collapsed into a single “rightsizing” frame, communities
may experience the process as unclear, overly technical, or predetermined.

For PPS, this means conversations about school size, utilization, and consolidation should clearly
distinguish among the different reasons a school may be considered for reconfiguration (e.g.,
from a K–8 to K–5), consolidation and/or closure. In other words, PPS should avoid treating
enrollment or utilization as a single diagnosis. Those numbers may point to a concern, but they
do not explain what is causing the concern (or even what historically created the concern) or
what kind of response would best serve students and families. For example, a school may be

Do School Closures or Major Reconfigurations Improve Student Outcomes?
The empirical research on school closures and consolidations is mixed, but it consistently shows
that improved student outcomes are not automatic. This happens in part because student
outcomes depend heavily on the quality of the receiving school, the strength of the transition
process, the continuity of student relationships, the level of disruption students experience, and
the capacity of the receiving school to serve both incoming and currently enrolled students (this
last point is really important and something that came up in our SEGC findings).55 56 57 58

Across studies of school closures in urban districts in the nation, a consistent pattern emerges,
which is that students who are displaced because of closures are more likely to benefit when they
attend schools that are substantially stronger than the schools they leave. However, when
students instead move to schools that are similar in performance, under-resourced, or
organizationally strained, the hoped-for academic benefits often do not materialize.59 60 61 62 This
is one of the most important lessons for PPS. A closure, consolidation, boundary change, or
program relocation should not be judged only by whether students are reassigned somewhere
else that improves building capacity and utilization. It should be judged by whether students are
reassigned to schools with demonstrably stronger learning conditions, resources, and the capacity
to serve them well.

Do Districts Typically Save Money When They Close or Consolidate Schools?
School closures are often presented as a fiscal strategy, especially when districts face declining
enrollment and budget deficits, which are very significant realities. However, the limited
literature in this area suggests that districts typically do not save as much money as they may
expect, at least not in the short term. Savings are possible, but they are often smaller, slower,
more dependent on labor reductions, and more complicated than closure narratives suggest.69 70

This is often the reality because the majority of school operating spending is tied to personnel,
not buildings. Nationally, salaries and benefits account for approximately 80% of current public
school expenditures (and in some districts is higher), which means that consolidation does not
automatically generate large savings unless it also changes staffing, programming,
transportation, or facility costs.71 In other words, closing a building may reduce some costs
related to utilities, maintenance, administration, and operations, but the largest savings usually
come from staffing changes. Edunomics Lab, which is a Georgetown University research center
that explores and models complex education finance decisions to inform policy and practice,
summarizes this fiscal reality with a useful rule of thumb. They suggest that when a district has
under-enrolled schools, closing 1 of every 15 schools may save about 4% of a district’s budget,
mostly through labor costs. More specifically, these savings may come through layoffs, attrition,
vacancy reductions, reassignment, or reduced future hiring. Facilities savings may occur, but
they are usually much smaller because personnel represent the overwhelming share of district
spending. For this reason, districts should be transparent about whether projected savings depend
on reducing staff positions and whether any savings will be reinvested into instruction and
student-facing supports. Additionally, facilities savings may occur, but they are usually much
smaller because, again, personnel is often the largest portion of district spending.72

This means that closure savings depend on whether the district can actually reduce ongoing
costs. If students move to receiving schools that require additional teachers, counselors,
administrators, paraprofessionals, transportation, renovations, safety supports, or transition staff,
the savings may be reduced or delayed. If closed buildings still require maintenance, security,
insurance, or eventual reuse planning, the savings may be smaller than anticipated. If families

What Is Essential for Making the Process as Equitable as Possible?
The research suggests that humanizing and equitable closure, consolidation, rightsizing,
boundary-change, or program-relocation processes require much more than public meetings.
They require clear criteria, upfront analysis of race, geography, and class, honest tradeoff
analysis, meaningful community influence, receiving-school readiness, funded transition plans,
and long-term monitoring.89 90

In this section, we summarize what the literature suggests is essential.

list is developed. These criteria should include more than utilization and enrollment. They should
include components such as student need, facility condition, program access, transportation
burden, receiving-school quality, special education capacity, multilingual learner supports, staff
stability, neighborhood impact, racial equity, and the history of prior disruption, to name a few.91

Conclusion
In sum, the broader research provides a clear caution. Future rightsizing, closure, consolidation,
boundary, or program relocation decisions should not be driven by utilization rates or enrollment
numbers alone. Enrollment matters, especially in an enrollment-driven finance system, but
enrollment is not the same as educational quality or equity (even though there is a relationship
between the two). Again, a school can be under-enrolled and still be deeply important to
students, families, staff, and surrounding neighborhoods. A school can be efficiently utilized and
still lack adequate staffing, intervention, enrichment, counseling, special education services,
multilingual learner supports, or school climate capacity.

A central question for PPS should therefore be whether any proposed change will create more
equitable, stable, well-resourced, accessible, culturally affirming, and academically strong
learning environments for students. Before any final decision, PPS should be able to show why
the change is necessary, what alternatives were considered, how racial equity was weighed,
whether affected students will move to stronger and better-resourced schools, whether receiving
schools are ready, what transition supports will be funded, how transportation and access
burdens will be reduced, how staff stability will be protected, how community input changed the
proposal, how much the district realistically expects to save, and how outcomes will be
monitored over time.

Put simply, the lesson from the research is not that districts should avoid all difficult decisions.
The lesson is that closures, consolidations, boundary changes, and program relocations are more
defensible when they are part of a credible plan to improve educational opportunity, not merely
redistribute students or reduce the number of buildings.

Across the literature, as noted earlier, school closures and related forms of district
reconfiguration are rarely only technical decisions. Districts often frame these decisions through
rationales such as enrollment decline, fiscal pressure, building utilization, academic performance,
and program efficiency, but their consequences are educational, relational, racialized, spatial, and
organizational.107 The SEGC findings strongly affirm this broader point. Even without closing
schools, PPS’s boundary changes, program relocations, grade reconfigurations, feeder shifts, and
enrollment balancing efforts raised many of the same equity questions that appear in the school

       ●    whether families experience the process as equitable

What SEGC Findings Confirms
SEGC findings confirm that technical enrollment balance does not automatically produce
schools having what they need to serve students and families well.

One of the clearest lessons SEGC findings confirm is that technical improvement does not
automatically translate into equitable educational improvement. The school closure and

rightsizing literature repeatedly shows that closing, consolidating, or reassigning students does

More specifically, this finding matters because it challenges the assumption that enrollment
balance, by itself, is a sufficient equity outcome. In PPS, enrollment distribution became more

shares of students of color, multilingual learners, and students receiving special education
services than anticipated. The central equity question, then, is not simply whether schools
became more balanced numerically. The deeper question is whether schools had the staffing,
intervention systems, enrichment opportunities, special education services, multilingual supports,
and adult capacity needed to serve their actual students well.

SEGC findings, therefore, confirm a core lesson from the broader literature, which is that
districts should not confuse technical rationality with educational sufficiency (i.e., schools
having what they need to serve students and families well). A technically balanced system can
still be under-resourced. In other words, a school can be more efficiently configured and still
lack the conditions students and staff need to thrive. For PPS, future rightsizing or enrollment
balancing efforts should treat enrollment balance as one indicator within a broader educational
equity and adequacy framework, not as proof that equity has been achieved.

SEGC Findings Confirm That Receiving-school Readiness and Implementation Capacity
Matter.
The broader literature consistently shows that student outcomes depend heavily on the capacity
and quality of receiving schools. In other districts, the benefits or harms of school closure were
shaped less by closure itself and more by where students went, what conditions awaited them,
and whether receiving schools could absorb additional students without destabilizing their own
learning environments (Bifulco & Schwegman, 2020; Brummet, 2014; de la Torre & Gwynne,
2009; Gordon et al., 2018; Kemple, 2015; Steinberg & MacDonald, 2019).

SEGC findings thus confirm this lesson, but in the context of boundary changes, program
relocation, feeder shifts, and grade reconfiguration rather than formal school closure. In our
study of SEGC, staff repeatedly described having to manage complex changes with insufficient
guidance, late or incomplete information, and limited district transition infrastructure. Some
schools relied heavily on local leadership, school culture, internal support teams, translation
work, and staff improvisation to support students and families.
                                                                                                 120



This pattern broadens the concept of receiving-school readiness. In SEGC, readiness was not
only about whether schools had physical space for students. It was about whether schools had the
staffing, leadership stability, family communication systems, intervention capacity, special
education supports, multilingual supports, and cultural as well as emotional infrastructure to
absorb change. SEGC confirms that district reconfiguration is also an implementation challenge,
not only a planning challenge. A district can design a technically coherent boundary or program
plan, but if school communities are left to absorb the emotional, logistical, and instructional
burden without sufficient support, the change can feel destabilizing even when some other goals
are met.

SEGC Confirms That Reconfiguration Burdens Are Not Evenly Distributed.
The school closure literature consistently shows that closure burdens are racially and
economically uneven. Majority-Black schools have been disproportionately likely to close, and
closures have often affected communities already shaped by disinvestment, housing instability,
school instability, and racial segregation (Eddins et al., 2024; Ewing & Green, 2022; Greene-Bell
& Pearman, 2024; Hahnel & Pearman, 2023). Ewing’s concept of institutional mourning and
Green’s work on community responses to closure also show that schools function as
neighborhood anchors, not merely service-delivery sites (Ewing, 2018; Green, 2017).

SEGC findings confirm this broader lesson, even though SEGC did not close schools. Staff and
SEGC members perceived that low-income communities and communities of color bore
disproportionate disruption. SEGC members who participated in the process reported extremely
low confidence in procedural fairness and believed that the benefits and burdens of the changes
were not distributed equitably. This concern is further strengthened by our finding that SEGC
schools served substantially higher shares of students from low-income backgrounds than SE
Portland schools that were not included in the process.

SEGC therefore shows that the equity questions raised by school closure research also apply to
boundary changes, program relocation, and school reconfiguration. A district does not have to
close a school for communities to experience inequitable burden, loss of access, racialized
displacement, weakened trust, or the perception that some communities were protected while
others absorbed change. This is one of the most important interpretive lessons for PPS, which is
that racial equity analysis should not begin only when closure is on the table. It should guide
every major decision that redistributes students, programs, staff, and resources.

SEGC Confirms That Staff Stability Is Central to Student Stability.
The broader literature increasingly emphasizes that closures and major reconfigurations affect
staff as well as students and families. Staff displacement, morale decline, professional
uncertainty, and the loss of trusted adults can weaken the relational infrastructure that students
                                                                                                 121


and families depend on (Eddins et al., 2024; Gordon et al., 2018). SEGC strongly confirms this
point.

The SEGC implementation year produced a significant staffing disruption. According to our
quantitative analysis, staff of color experienced especially sharp effects, including higher rates of
school departure and district exit compared with staff of color in non-SEGC schools. Latino/a/x
staff departure patterns were shaped in part by DLI program relocation, while Black staff
experienced persistently elevated turnover and higher involuntary termination rates. Our findings
also show that staff morale was most negatively affected where SEGC-related change overlapped
with repeated staffing loss, role instability, annual cuts, and leadership turnover.

This finding matters because future rightsizing conversations often focus heavily on students and
buildings, while treating staff movement as a secondary operational issue. The SEGC findings
suggest that this would be a mistake. Staff are part of the student support infrastructure. They
carry relationships, language access, family trust, institutional memory, cultural knowledge, and
daily implementation capacity. If reconfiguration disrupts staff of color, bilingual staff, trusted
support staff, or stable school leadership, the district may weaken the very conditions needed for
students to experience the new configuration as supportive.

SEGC Confirms That Participation Without Power Can Deepen Distrust.

The school closure and rightsizing literature consistently shows that community and family
engagement can become a site of distrust when communities believe decisions are predetermined
or when participation does not meaningfully shape outcomes (Eddins et al., 2024; Nuamah,
2022; Syeed, 2019). SEGC strongly confirms this lesson. SEGC members often described the
coalition itself as collaborative and respectful but distinguished that internal experience from
their sense that PPS leadership and the board did not meaningfully act on SEGC input. They also
reported limited confidence in decision rules, low trust in rightsizing, and strong disagreement
that benefits and burdens were distributed equitably.

This finding is especially important because SEGC was designed as an engagement-centered
process. The findings suggest that participation alone is not the same as shared power.
Community members may feel heard by one another while still feeling unheard by decision-
makers. They may invest time, expertise, and emotional labor into a process and still conclude
that the final decisions were constrained, symbolic, or predetermined.

For PPS, the implication is direct, which is that future engagement processes should define what
is open for influence, what is not, what constraints are real, how data will be used, how
alternatives will be modeled, and how community input will shape final decisions. Otherwise,
community engagement can produce the appearance of inclusion while deepening mistrust.
                                                                                              122



What SEGC Complicates

SEGC Complicates the Research Because It Did Not Produce Broad Measurable Academic
Harm.
One important way SEGC findings complicate the school closure literature is that it did not
produce the kind of broad academic disruption often documented in large-scale school closure
contexts. Research on Chicago’s 2013 closures found negative academic effects for students
from closed schools, including reading and math effects that emerged around the closure
announcement and persisted longer in math (Gordon et al., 2018). By contrast, the SEGC
matched analyses found that students attending SEGC schools had broadly similar academic and
behavioral outcomes to matched peers in non-SEGC schools, with some positive associations for
Black students, elementary students, multilingual learners, DLI students, and students in schools
with higher free or reduced-price lunch eligibility.

This complicates the broader literature in useful ways. To be clear, it does not mean SEGC was
experienced as easy, equitable, or well-supported. Rather, it means the measured academic and
behavioral outcomes did not show broad harm relative to matched peers. The SEGC findings,
therefore, reveal a gap between measured outcomes and lived experience. Middle school families
reported fewer positive perceptions of learning environments than elementary families, and staff
described many learning environments as caring but structurally strained. Thus, SEGC
complicates some closure findings in its quantitative outcome pattern, but it confirms the broader
literature’s point that academic outcomes alone do not capture the full cost of major
reconfiguration.

SEGC Complicates the Research Because DLI Relocation Did Not Produce Widespread
District Exit but Still Produced Program-level Sorting.
Another way SEGC complicates the literature is that SEGC-related DLI relocation did not
appear to produce widespread district exit among DLI students. DLI students whose programs
moved largely stayed in PPS, and DLI students left PPS at lower rates than non-DLI students.
This pattern suggests that DLI participation may have functioned as a retention factor for some
students and families, even during program relocation.

However, this should not be read as evidence that relocation was harmless. The findings show
that relocation still mattered for who followed the program, who left the program, who absorbed
transportation burdens, and how program composition changed. Students who left relocated DLI
programs more often spoke a language other than English at home, suggesting that families with
different levels of program attachment, transportation flexibility, and schooling alternatives
responded differently to relocation.
                                                                                                 123


This finding complicates the broader literature because it shows that district exit is not the only
way to understand displacement. In many closure contexts, researchers ask whether students
leave the district or transfer to lower or higher-performing schools. SEGC shows that program
relocation can produce a more subtle form of sorting, which is not necessarily district exit, but
program-level sorting shaped by race, language, family capacity, program attachment, and
transportation.

SEGC Complicates Simple Success or Harm Narratives.
SEGC also complicates any simple narrative that district reconfiguration either worked or failed.
The findings show that the same change could produce gains and harms in the same school site.
Some schools experienced stronger program coherence, more developmentally appropriate
configurations, or greater cultural affirmation for particular student groups. At the same time,
those same changes sometimes reduced neighborhood access, disrupted school identity,
weakened diversity, created transportation burdens, or damaged trust.

This complication is important for PPS because rightsizing conversations are likely to generate
polarized narratives, which is very common in districts across the nation. Some stakeholders may
point to efficiencies, new programs, or enrollment balance as evidence of success. Others may
point to displacement, weakened trust, or staffing disruption as evidence of harm. The SEGC
findings suggest that both can be true. The more important analytic task is to identify the
distribution of benefits and burdens across students, families, staff, programs, and
neighborhoods.

What SEGC Adds to the Research

SEGC Adds Evidence That Racialized Displacement Can Occur Through Program
Relocation and Reconfiguration, Not Only Through School Closure.

One of the most important contributions of the SEGC findings is that they show racialized
displacement can occur through program relocation and school reconfiguration, not only through
formal school closure. The conversion of one school to whole-school Spanish DLI coincided
with substantial student movement, including the near disappearance of Black students from the
school and a large increase in Latino/a/x enrollment. At the same time, some participants
described the full-school Spanish DLI model as more intentional, coherent, and culturally
affirming for Latino/a/x families.

This is not a simple harm story or a simple benefit story. It is an equity tradeoff. The same
change that strengthened linguistic and cultural affirmation for one racially minoritized and
historically underserved community also displaced another racially minoritized and historically
underserved group and reduced neighborhood access for non-DLI families. This adds to the
                                                                                                  124


closure and rightsizing literature by showing that programmatic equity and racial equity must be
analyzed together. Put simply, a change can advance one equity goal while undermining another.

For PPS, this is a critical finding. Future program relocation decisions should not assume that
expanding or consolidating a specialized program automatically advances equity. The district
should ask questions like:

   ●   Which students gain access?
   ●   Which students lose access?
   ●   Which students must travel farther?
   ●   Which students lose neighborhood continuity?
   ●   Which families have the resources to follow the program?
   ●   Which groups become more concentrated, more isolated, or less visible in the new
       configuration?

SEGC Adds Evidence That Schools Can Experience Cultural Affirmation and Institutional
Loss at the Same Time.
The SEGC findings also add to the literature by showing that the same school can be experienced
simultaneously as a site of loss and a site of affirmation. Closure literature often emphasizes
institutional mourning, neighborhood loss, and community displacement (Ewing, 2018; Green,
2017). SEGC findings affirm these concepts but adds complexity in that some schools
experienced loss of neighborhood identity, walkability, diversity, or prior community structure
while also becoming more coherent, affirming, or developmentally appropriate for specific
student groups.

This is especially visible in the DLI findings. Some participants described stronger program
coherence, cultural affirmation, and linguistic identity after consolidation. Others described loss
of neighborhood access, reduced diversity, diminished elective access, or weakened school-
community ties. These findings suggest that major district reconfiguration should not be
evaluated as simply harmful or beneficial. The analytic task is to identify the distribution of gains
and losses across groups.

This adds to the literature by emphasizing simultaneous equity effects. Future PPS decisions
should therefore avoid one-dimensional equity claims. A decision may be good for multilingual
program coherence and harmful for neighborhood access. It may create a stronger middle school
model and reduce racial or socioeconomic diversity. It may improve facility access and still
damage trust. A more mature equity analysis must hold these tensions together rather than
choosing one story.
                                                                                                  125


SEGC Adds Evidence That Cumulative Change Load May Matter as Much as the Formal
Policy Change.
The broader implementation literature argues that schools interpret and enact reforms through
existing organizational conditions, including leadership capacity, staff culture, staffing stability,
school climate, and district-school alignment (Aldridge & McLure, 2024; Bryk et al., 2010;
Honig & Hatch, 2004; McLure & Aldridge, 2022; Spillane et al., 2002). SEGC findings add to
this literature by showing that the effects of district reconfiguration depend not only on the
formal change itself, but also on the cumulative change load schools are already carrying.

Across schools, participants described SEGC as layered on top of other disruptions such as
pandemic recovery, construction, budget cuts, labor actions, leadership turnover, school mergers,
grade reconfiguration, feeder changes, and prior program shifts. Participants described this not as
a single transition, but as a dense web of transitions or multi-year erosion. This finding is
important because districts often evaluate each change separately, while school communities
experience changes cumulatively.

For PPS, the implication is that future rightsizing or reconfiguration processes should include an
implementation burden assessment (as mentioned previously). Before asking a school to absorb
another major change, the district should examine what that school has already experienced,
including leadership turnover, staff loss, enrollment decline, prior boundary changes,
construction disruption, program relocation, student need, family trust, and internal capacity. A
change that appears reasonable in isolation may become destabilizing when layered onto
accumulated strain.

SEGC Adds Evidence That Local Leadership and School Culture Can Buffer Harm but
Cannot Substitute for District Support.
SEGC also adds to the broader literature by showing how school-level leadership and internal
culture mediate the impact of district reconfiguration. In several schools, stable leadership,
strong staff culture, relational trust, and trauma-informed routines helped staff and families
navigate change. These schools were not unaffected but rather they had stronger internal capacity
to absorb strain.

At the same time, we caution against romanticizing local resilience. Strong school cultures often
buffered harm that district systems did not adequately prevent. Staff repeatedly described district
support as late, partial, unclear, or mismatched to school realities. In this sense, school-level
leadership functioned as a compensatory mechanism. It helped schools survive the change, but it
did not erase the need for stronger district planning, communication, staffing, or transition
infrastructure.
                                                                                               126


This adds to the literature by clarifying that “school capacity” should not become an excuse to
shift implementation burden downward. Future PPS processes should identify where local
leadership is strong but also ask what the central office must provide so schools are not required
to carry district change through local improvisation alone.

SEGC Findings Add Evidence That Procedural Equity May Matter Even When Student
Experiences Remain Relatively Positive.
A striking SEGC finding is that SEGC members reported deep dissatisfaction with the process
and equity outcomes, while also reporting student experiences that were comparable to other
families. This adds to the literature by showing that dissatisfaction with reconfiguration does not
always reflect narrow self-interest or direct personal harm. In PPS, some of the people closest to
the process judged it harshly precisely because they had inside knowledge of how decisions were
made, what data were available, how alternatives were constrained, and how burdens were
distributed.

This finding matters for future engagement. Districts sometimes interpret community criticism as
resistance to change or dissatisfaction with individual assignments. The SEGC findings suggest a
different possibility in which participants may support the need for change while objecting to
how the process defines problems, controls data, narrows options, distributes burdens, or fails to
act on equity commitments. This aligns with research showing that low responsiveness in closure
processes can undermine democratic trust and civic participation (Nuamah, 2022).

For PPS, procedural fairness should be treated as an equity outcome in its own right. A process
can produce some student-level stability and still damage trust if participants experience it as
opaque, constrained, or inequitable. Future processes should therefore assess not only whether
students are doing well after implementation, but also whether communities believe the district
acted transparently, consistently, accountably, and equitably.

Overall Interpretation
Together, the SEGC findings confirm, complicate, and add to the broader research on school
closures, rightsizing, boundary changes, and district reconfiguration. SEGC findings confirm the
literature’s central caution that technical solutions do not automatically produce educational
equity. It also confirms the importance of receiving-school readiness, staff stability, meaningful
community engagement, racial equity analysis, transportation burden, and implementation
support.

At the same time, SEGC complicates the literature because it did not produce broad measurable
academic harm, because DLI relocation did not produce widespread district exit, and because
several changes produced gains and harms simultaneously. These complications are important
because they prevent the report from reducing SEGC to a simple success or failure story.
                                                                                               127



Finally, SEGC adds to the research because it shows that many equity concerns associated with
school closure can emerge even when no school is formally closed. Boundary changes, program
relocation, grade-span reconfiguration, feeder shifts, and enrollment balancing can still produce
displacement, loss, staff disruption, unequal burden, weakened trust, and institutional mourning.
They can also produce gains, including stronger program coherence, more developmentally
appropriate configurations, and more culturally affirming environments for some students. The
core equity task is to examine the distribution of those gains and burdens.

Again, one of the most important lessons for PPS is that future rightsizing, boundary,
consolidation, or program relocation decisions should be evaluated through an educational
equity, sufficiency and implementation-readiness lens. The district should not ask only whether a
building is underutilized or whether enrollment is balanced. It should ask whether the proposed
change will create stronger and more equitable learning environments, whether receiving schools
are prepared, whether historically underserved communities are protected from disproportionate
burden, whether staff stability is preserved, whether transportation and access barriers are
addressed, whether families have meaningful influence, and whether the district has the capacity
and resources to keep its promises.

A Brief Note on Focus Options and Equity
Focus options were not a primary focus of this evaluation. However, they emerged during data
collection as an important issue that PPS will likely need to address in current rightsizing
conversations and decisions. We briefly discuss focus options here because they are connected to
several broader questions at the center of rightsizing such as:

   ● how students and programs are distributed across the district
   ● how enrollment patterns affect school stability
   ● how family choice shapes access to opportunity, and whether future decisions will
     advance or undermine the district’s commitment to well-resourced, racially equitable and
     socially just schools

In this sense, focus options are not treated as a separate topic, but as part of the larger equity
context in which PPS must make decisions about boundaries, consolidations, program locations,
and school consolidations and/or closures. Below, we briefly discuss our understanding of the
history of focus options in PPS, how they intersect with race, equity, enrollment, and access to
opportunity, and offer considerations for how the district might attend to these issues in future
rightsizing decisions.

As such, PPS’s rightsizing work must be understood against the district’s longer history of
student assignment, desegregation, transfer, and focus option policies dating back to the 1970s.
                                                                                                  128


Historical accounts of PPS’s desegregation efforts show that the district relied heavily on
voluntary transfer and one-way busing approaches that placed the burden of integration primarily
on Black students and other students of color, rather than requiring shared responsibility across
the district.108 By the early 1970s, PPS’s Schools for the Seventies plan sent Black students to
schools across the district while allowing white students to voluntarily attend majority Black
schools, which was a design shaped in part by fears that mandatory reassignment of white
students would produce backlash and accelerate white and middle-class flight from the
district.109 In this way, PPS’s enrollment policies often attempted to manage racial equity
concerns while also protecting the preferences of often white, middle-class, and more privileged
families.

This history did not end with busing. As PPS moved through later desegregation, transfer,
magnet, and focus option policies, in-district choice became a central mechanism through which
families could navigate the district. These policies created meaningful opportunities for some
students, but they also allowed families with greater information, mobility, and social capital to
access preferred schools and programs while neighborhood schools serving historically
underserved communities were often left more vulnerable to enrollment loss, instability, and
reduced program offerings.110 PPS’s own enrollment and transfer policy now states that the
district seeks to provide “equal access to educational options” and promote “equity and
diversity” in student transfers and admissions, which underscores that choice systems must be
actively governed to prevent unequal access and unequal consequences (Portland Public Schools,
2015).

That history matters for current and future rightsizing decisions. If PPS is not careful, school
closures, consolidations, boundary changes, and program relocations could reproduce the same
pattern under a new name, which is protecting the preferences of families most likely to
mobilize, opt out, or leave, while asking Black, Latino/a/x, Indigenous, immigrant, low-income,
and other historically underserved communities to carry the heaviest burden of system change.

Equity-centered rightsizing cannot be held captive by the anticipated departure, resistance, or
“exit” of privileged families. To be clear, this does not mean dismissing their concerns or
excluding them from the process. It means refusing to let their preferences function as an
informal veto over decisions intended to create a more coherent, stable, well-resourced, and
racially equitable and socially just school system.

Therefore, the central question for PPS should not be, “What plan will least disrupt the families
most likely to leave?” (not that anyone is asking that in PPS but that is a question that has
emerged nationally). The central question should be, “What plan best advances equitable access

Enrollment and the Conditions for “Well-Resourced Schools”
We included this section in response to questions raised by PPS central office leaders about
enrollment, school size, and the conditions necessary to create “well-resourced schools.”

This section is not intended to function as a formal school finance study. Such a study would
require a more extensive fiscal analysis, including detailed examination of staffing formulas,
school-level budgets, facilities costs, transportation costs, projected enrollment, and the specific
resource model PPS intends to use to define a well-resourced school (which was not the purpose
of this evaluation).

While this report does not offer a formal school finance analysis, it draws on relevant research,
available district and state documents, and our equity-centered interpretation of the SEGC
findings to identify key considerations PPS should weigh when connecting enrollment, staffing,
resources, and school quality.

Additionally, it is important that this section should not be read as determining which schools
PPS should close, consolidate, reconfigure, or maintain based on the enrollment numbers and
school sizes discussed below. If PPS and the Board consider closures or consolidations, those
decisions must be made through a local process that includes extensive community engagement,
Board deliberation, school-level analysis, and other factors.

The purpose of this section is to support PPS in making locally grounded and equity-centered
decisions about enrollment, school size, and resource allocation. It clarifies the enrollment,
staffing, funding, and program conditions PPS should consider before any future closure or
consolidation decision is made.

The central argument of this section is straightforward, which is enrollment matters because
Oregon’s school finance system is largely enrollment driven. Through the State School Fund,
Oregon distributes operating revenue using an equalization formula based heavily on weighted
average daily membership, or ADMw. As a result, enrollment changes directly shape the
resources available to PPS to staff schools, sustain programs, and provide students with a
complete educational experience. Given this reality, this section addresses five guiding
questions:

   1. Why should PPS derive its own enrollment standards rather than importing school-size
      numbers from other districts?
   2. How might PPS determine enrollment ranges for well-resourced schools in a way that
      begins with equity, staffing supports, and equitable student experience rather than
      building utilization alone?
                                                                                                                       134


      3. What does the research on school size suggest, especially for students who have been
         historically underserved and what are the implications for PPS?
      4. What enrollment ranges might make well-resourced elementary, middle, and K–8 schools
         financially possible in Portland under Oregon’s current funding conditions?
      5. What can school consolidations and closures realistically accomplish?

Question 1: Why should PPS derive its own enrollment standards rather than importing
school-size numbers from other districts?

This section begins by clarifying why enrollment standards for “well-resourced schools” should
be locally derived rather than borrowed from other districts or only reduced to building
utilization rates. As such, we want to caution PPS from simply borrowing an enrollment number
or school size from another district for the purposes of achieving well-resourced schools. We
make this caution for several reasons.

First, this matters because enrollment thresholds are shaped by local funding formulas, labor
costs, staffing models, facilities conditions, student need, and enrollment trajectories. Therefore,
a number that appears reasonable in, for example, Seattle, Denver, San Francisco, or San
Antonio may not be fiscally or educationally practical in Portland. While peer districts can help
PPS understand how other systems have approached enrollment decline, school consolidation,
and “well-resourced school” planning, their numbers should inform PPS’s approach rather than
determine Portland’s threshold.

This is especially important in Oregon because, as stated earlier, the State School Fund
distributes revenue to districts through a weighted enrollment formula. Oregon uses ADMw, or
weighted average daily membership, to account for additional costs associated with serving
particular student populations and district conditions. In this context, enrollment is not only a
demographic measure. It is also a revenue base that determines what schools can staff and
sustain.112

Oregon distributes the majority of school operating revenue on a per student basis. For PPS,
sustained enrollment decline is not simply a facilities problem caused by empty seats. It is a

Question 2: How might PPS determine enrollment ranges for well-resourced schools in a
way that begins with equity, staffing supports, and equitable student experiences rather
than building utilization alone?

Again, we want to make clear that determining the appropriate enrollment range for a well-
resourced school is a decision PPS should generate through a transparent local planning process
and not impose through a single numerical threshold. School size should be treated as an equity
question instead of only as a technical one. In other words, the central question is not only, “How
many students should a school have?” but rather, “What teaching conditions, staffing resources,
student supports, and equitable learning opportunities should every PPS student be able to count
on in every school?”

Step One: Define What Constitutes a Well-Resourced School and Its Staffing Model
The starting point for determining school size should be a values and equity decision that is
tested against fiscal reality. Given PPS’s structural deficit and sustained enrollment decline, the

the system can reliably and feasibly sustain.

Thus, this step is especially important because a well-resourced school cannot be defined only by
building utilization, enrollment efficiency, or the number of students assigned to a campus. It
must be defined by the actual teaching conditions, student supports, staff roles, and learning

Step Two: Translate the Staffing Support Model into Classrooms or Sections per Grade
After PPS defines what constitutes a well-resourced school and the staffing support model of a
well-resourced school, the district should translate that vision into the number of classrooms or
sections needed at each grade level. This is a critical step because the practical unit of school
design is not the total enrollment number by itself. It is also the grade level cohort.

For elementary schools, PPS should consider how many classrooms per grade are needed to
provide manageable class sizes, stable staffing, collaboration among teachers, student support,
and flexibility when enrollment fluctuates. For example, an elementary school with too few
students per grade may not be able to sustain multiple classrooms at each grade level without
either increasing costs or combining classrooms in ways that can reduce instructional coherence.

Conversely, a school with enough students to sustain multiple classrooms per grade can more
easily support teacher collaboration, student grouping, intervention, and schedule flexibility.
For middle schools, the question might shift from classrooms per grade to sections per grade
because of the departmentalization in middle schools. Thus, PPS should consider how many
sections are needed in each grade to support a full academic schedule, electives, intervention,
enrichment, counseling, special education services, multilingual learner supports, and teacher
teaming. The Evidence-Based Model emphasizes that adequate school staffing includes not only
core teachers, but also specialist teachers, instructional coaches, counselors, nurses, librarians,
tutors, and other student support roles.127 This means that middle school enrollment ranges
should be tied to the schedule and staffing model required to offer a full middle-grade program.

Given the work of SEGC, PPS might be looking to phase out K–8 schools. However, we address
them here since the district still has K–8 schools. For K–8 schools, PPS should consider both
dimensions at once. In other words, K–8 schools must sustain elementary classrooms and middle

Question 3: What does the research on school size suggest, especially for students who have
been historically underserved and what are the implications for PPS?

The research on school size does not support a simple conclusion that smaller schools are always
better or that larger schools are always more efficient. Instead, the literature points to a set of
tradeoffs (regarding school size) that PPS must hold together. The research suggests that smaller
schools often provide stronger relationships, greater student visibility, easier family connection,
and more intimate school climates.132 133 Larger schools often provide more staffing flexibility,
more program breadth, stronger grade-level or subject-area teams, and greater capacity to offer
specialists, electives, and intervention support.134 However, it is important to note that neither
size produces educational quality by itself. Size matters because of what it enables or constrains.

Several studies suggest that smaller schools can be especially beneficial for students from low-

schools, especially for students from low-income backgrounds.135 Other research similarly found

schools enrolling fewer than 400 students were associated with stronger teacher collective

At the same time, the research cautions against treating smallness itself as the intervention. More
recent reviews have found that school size effects on academic achievement are mixed, modest,
and highly dependent on context. For example, Luyten, Hendriks, and Scheerens’ synthesis

including belonging, safety, student participation, school climate, and relationships.137 Egalite

achievement tended to decline as school size increased, particularly in higher grades, but also
emphasized that the effects varied by context and were not mechanically determined by
enrollment alone.138

The key lesson for PPS is that school size operates through mechanisms. Smaller schools may
make it easier for adults to know students well, notice when students are struggling,
communicate with families, and build shared responsibility among staff. These conditions are
especially important for students who have experienced marginalization in schools,
displacement, poverty, racialized exclusion, and/or inconsistent access to institutional support.
However, larger schools may make it easier to provide and staff a broader educational program.
Larger schools can often support more stable grade-level teams, full-time specialists, stronger
intervention systems, elective offerings, arts, music, physical education, counseling, and other
academic, social-emotional, enrichment, and staffing supports that students also need.

size. The goal should be to identify an enrollment range where schools are large enough to

Question 4: What enrollment estimate ranges (not exact number) might make equitable
and well-resourced elementary, middle, and K–8 schools financially possible in Portland
under Oregon’s current funding conditions?

As stated previously in this section, PPS should consider Oregon’s QEM as an adequacy
benchmark, but not as a final enrollment target. The QEM is useful because it identifies a
prototypical elementary school of 360 students and a prototypical middle school of 500 students,
with average class sizes of approximately 20 students in elementary schools and 20.8 students in
middle schools.139 However, again, the QEM’s prototype school structure was developed as a
statewide funding model using composite schools, not as a precise enrollment standard for every
PPS school. Again, the Oregon Department of Education notes that the Quality Education
Commission was appointed in 1999 to validate and refine the Oregon QEM and that the model
helps lawmakers estimate the cost of providing the education programs necessary for Oregon
students to meet state goals. For this reason, PPS should treat QEM as a minimum adequacy
floor rather than a complete contemporary model of what every well-resourced school requires.

This caution is especially important because, as stated above, Oregon has not funded schools at
the level required to fully implement the QEM. As noted above, the 2024 QEM report estimates
that full implementation for the 2025 to 2027 biennium would require $13.526 billion, which is

electives, teaming, and student supports, PPS may want to stress test the middle school number
closer to 100 students over five years.

services, multilingual learner supports, and team-based structures for early adolescents.

students per class or section. If the school declined to approximately 600 students over time, it

grade at about 22 students per class or section. However, PPS should be clear that a 650 student

intervention groups, advisory structures, and specialized middle grade programming.

Question 5: What can school consolidations and closures realistically accomplish?

In theory, school consolidations and closures can increase the probability that enrollment
conditions under which “well-resourced schools” become financially and feasibly possible. The
logic is that PPS may be able to use existing resources more efficiently by reducing some
duplication across under-enrolled buildings, concentrate staff and programs in fewer school sites,
and spread some fixed administrative, operational, and facility costs across a larger number of
students.140 However, this is a theory of resource efficiency, not a guarantee of equitable
educational improvement.

Importantly, school consolidations and closures cannot solve the larger macro conditions that are
producing, and even intensifying, enrollment and fiscal pressure in the district. PPS’s enrollment
challenges are largely shaped by forces that extend beyond the district’s direct control, including
declining birth rates, lower kindergarten and early-grade capture rates, family migration patterns,
housing costs, housing production patterns, and the share of age-eligible families who choose
PPS rather than other public, charter, private, or homeschool options.141 Portland’s broader
housing context also matters. The city’s housing reports continue to identify affordability as a
major challenge for Portland residents with rents and home prices outpacing incomes and
creating conditions that affect whether families with children can remain in the city.142 These are
not problems a school district can solve through fiscal cuts, consolidations, or closures.

Additionally, school consolidations and closures cannot resolve the broader state funding
conditions in which PPS operates. Oregon’s QEM identifies the resources needed to support a
quality education and is explicit that districts need sufficient funding to implement the best
practices associated with the state’s education goals. Even if PPS uses its existing resources more
efficiently, consolidations and closures do not automatically make the state funding system more
adequate, reduce housing costs, increase birth rates, reverse family displacement, or eliminate
competition through school choice mechanisms. These forces arrive at the district’s front door
and shape the realities of school enrollment, staffing, and budgeting, but again, they cannot be
solved by closing buildings.

This distinction matters because research suggests (which we reviewed earlier in this study) that
districts often overestimate the savings produced by school closures and underestimate the costs
that remain after students leave a building, which plays into a district's ability to deliver on its
promises of well-resourced schools. The reality is that relatively little research has accurately
evaluated long-term district savings from closures and that savings are often substantial only

closures often fail to produce the expected fiscal benefits because personnel costs make up the
majority of district expenses, buildings can be difficult to sell, and closed schools may continue

The building-disposition problem is especially important. Closing a school does not immediately
eliminate the cost of owning the school building. Districts may continue to pay for maintenance,
security, insurance, utilities, surveillance, repairs, vandalism response, environmental review,
demolition, broker fees, legal work, and staff time associated with managing, marketing, leasing,
or selling the property.145 The Pew Charitable Trusts’ study of shuttered public school buildings
in Atlanta, Chicago, Cincinnati, Cleveland, Detroit, Kansas City, Missouri, Milwaukee,
Pittsburgh, St. Louis, Tulsa, and Washington, D.C. found that districts must often pay for
maintenance, security, and insurance while they search for new occupants, which helps to
explain why the net operating savings from closing a building are often less than expected.

Given this, PPS should not assume that closed school buildings can be sold quickly or for the
revenue originally projected. The research does not provide a single reliable national average for
how long it takes districts to sell closed schools, because the timeline depends on a number of
variables such as the local real estate market, building condition, zoning, neighborhood context,
deed restrictions, political priorities, and whether the district wants to sell, lease, repurpose, or
hold the property. However, the available evidence suggests that disposition of school buildings
can often take years, and in some cases have taken a decade or more. Therefore, PPS should treat
building sale revenue as uncertain, delayed, and context-dependent, not as an immediate or
guaranteed offset to closure costs. In addition, another lesson for PPS is that a closure plan
should include a building disposition plan, a maintenance and security cost estimate, a
community reuse process, and a realistic timeline for sale, lease, reuse, or even demolition before
closure decisions are finalized.

about their expectations (not exact answers) about the following questions before any final
decision:

significant political capital and causing real community harm in exchange for a budget
adjustment rather than an equitable educational improvement. It also risks placing too much
explanatory weight on school-level decisions while leaving the larger drivers of enrollment
decline and fiscal stress underexamined. The enrollment question and the resources question are
inseparable. PPS should treat them that way. Enrollment can help create the conditions for “well-
resourced schools,” and consolidations and closures may help PPS use existing resources more
efficiently. But resource efficiency should not be treated as the end goal. The central question is
whether students, families, and educators experience the result as more stable, more supported,
and more equitable.

Conclusion and Final Thoughts
                                                                                                151


This evaluation shows that SEGC was not experienced as a single, uniform district initiative. It
was experienced differently across schools depending on the type and intensity of change, each
school’s prior history, leadership stability, staffing capacity, program identity, family trust, and
the level of district support available before, during, and after implementation. Across the
findings, one overarching lesson is clear: technical changes to boundaries, programs, feeder
patterns, and enrollment distribution do not automatically produce equitable or well-resourced
schools. Equity depends on how decisions are made, how burdens and benefits are distributed
(and dare we say eliminated), how schools are supported to absorb change, and whether students,
families, and staff experience the process as transparent, responsive, and fair.

The SEGC findings point to both caution and possibility. In some places, SEGC-related changes
were associated with greater enrollment balance, stronger program coherence, more culturally
affirming environments, or more developmentally appropriate school configurations. In other
places, school communities experienced loss, staffing instability, reduced trust, transportation
burdens, weakened neighborhood connection, and implementation strain. In several schools, both
realities were true at once.

Future districtwide change efforts should therefore begin with clearer goals, transparent decision
rules, meaningful community engagement and influence, strong implementation planning,
receiving-school readiness, and explicit attention to racial equity. PPS should also assess the
amount of change each school has already absorbed before asking it to absorb more. If the
district uses the lessons from SEGC to strengthen planning, communication, staffing, transition
support, and accountability, future enrollment balancing and rightsizing efforts can move beyond
technical reconfiguration toward a more coherent and equitable vision of well-resourced schools
for all students.

Our team is deeply grateful for the opportunity to conduct this evaluation and to learn from the
many PPS students, families, educators, staff, school leaders, district leaders, and community
members who contributed their time, insight, and lived experience to this work. We recognize
that SEGC touched deeply held questions about school identity, racial equity, neighborhood
belonging, language access, staff stability, and public trust. We do not take lightly the
responsibility of documenting those experiences with care, honesty, and dignity.

As PPS moves forward, our hope is that this report serves not only as a record of what happened,
but as a resource for what can be learned. The work ahead will require courage, transparency,
humility, and sustained commitment. It will also require the district to hold complexity well: to
acknowledge harm where harm occurred, to preserve what communities value, to build on what
worked, and to make future decisions in ways that are more inclusive, better supported, and more
clearly connected to the educational experiences students deserve. The ultimate measure of
future districtwide change should not be whether plans look coherent on paper, but whether
                                                                                                    152


students, families, and staff experience schools as places of racial equity, social justice,
belonging, stability, opportunity, and care.

        Dr. Green’s Overall Takeaways on the Use and Interpretation of This Report
As the lead researcher for this SEGC Equity Audit Evaluation, I want to be clear about how this
report should and should not be used. This report should not be read as a justification for school
closures, consolidation, or any predetermined future rightsizing decision in Portland Public
Schools. Decisions about school closure, consolidation, boundary changes, program relocation,
or school reconfiguration are local decisions that should be made with the people most directly
affected by the consequences, including students, families, educators, school staff, school
leaders, and communities. This report is intended to help PPS understand what can be learned
from SEGC, not to authorize or validate any specific future action.

I also recognize that in climates where school closures, consolidations, or rightsizing decisions
are being considered, reports like this can be interpreted in many different ways. Some readers
may look for findings that support a position they already hold, whether in favor of or against
future school changes. I ask readers not to read into this report any interpretation beyond what is
actually written. We wrote what we mean in the report. Where the evidence supports a claim, we
tried our best to name that claim. Where the evidence is limited, mixed, or based on participant
perceptions, we state that as clearly as possible. If clarification is needed about any finding,
interpretation, limitation, or recommendation, I encourage readers to contact me directly before
attributing meanings to the report that are not stated in the text.

Again, for this report, the purpose is to help PPS understand how SEGC-related changes were
experienced by students, families, staff, school leaders, and SEGC members, and what those
experiences suggest for future districtwide decision-making.

In short, this report should be used as a learning document. It offers evidence about what SEGC
participants, school communities, and families experienced. It identifies patterns of trust,
disruption, support, and inequity, and it raises questions PPS should address before undertaking
future districtwide changes. If PPS moves forward with future rightsizing or reconfiguration
efforts, the findings in this report suggest that the district must do so with greater clarity, stronger
implementation support, deeper community partnership, and visible accountability to the
students, families, and educators most affected.

Finally, the future of PPS should not be guided by enrollment numbers, facility utilization,
budget pressure, or program efficiency alone. Those considerations matter, but they should be
held accountable to the district’s stated commitments to racial equity and social justice. If racial
equity and social justice are truly the ideals PPS espouses, then they must function as the
heartbeat, guiding force, and arbiter of district decision-making. They should shape how
                                                                                              153


problems are defined, whose experiences are centered, how tradeoffs are evaluated, how burdens
are distributed, and whether proposed solutions actually and equitably improve the daily
educational lives of students, families, and educators. Again, the central question for PPS is not
only whether future decisions are technically defensible, but whether they are experienced as
just, transparent, humanizing, and worthy of public trust.
