PORTLAND PUBLIC SCHOOLS​
BOARD OF EDUCATION MEMORANDUM


   To: Portland Public Schools Board of Education

   From: Dr. Kimberlee Armstrong, Superintendent and Dr. Kelli Charles, Interim Chief Accountability
   and Equity Officer

   Date: November 4, 2026

   Subject: Focal Student Outcomes and Educational Equity: Understanding Student Achievement,
   Opportunity, and District Accountability​


Purpose

The purpose of this memorandum is to provide the Board of Education with an overview of
the November 4, 2026, presentation, Focal Student Outcomes and Educational Equity. The
presentation fulfills the district's Oregon Statewide Assessment System (OSAS) reporting
responsibility while focusing intentionally on the educational experiences and academic
outcomes of four focal student populations: Black students, Native American students,
multilingual learners, and students with disabilities.

The presentation is intended to deepen the Board's understanding of the historical and
educational contexts that have shaped opportunities for these students; examine current
academic performance across Portland Public Schools (PPS); highlight district efforts to
support student success; and identify areas requiring continued attention and improvement. I
will be joined by district leaders representing each focal student population, who will share
student strengths, successes, challenges, and the district's ongoing responses.

The Board received the 2025 Oregon Statewide Assessment System (OSAS) Results
Memorandum in October 2026. Building upon this assessment reporting, the November 4
presentation moves beyond proficiency results to examine educational opportunity,
instructional effectiveness, and the district's collective responsibility for improving student
outcomes.

The central question guiding this presentation is:
To what extent are we delivering on the promise of public education for every ​
student, and what must we do differently to ensure that all students experience ​
meaningful academic success?

Background and Context

Public education has not historically provided equitable access, experiences, or opportunities
to all students. Race, language, disability, and identity have shaped students' access to
educational institutions, rigorous instruction, appropriate supports, and pathways to future
opportunity. For Black students, the history of American education includes legal exclusion,
segregation, and persistent disparities in educational opportunity. Native American students
and communities have experienced educational policies intended to suppress Indigenous
languages, cultures, and identities. Multilingual learners have encountered educational
approaches that have not always recognized linguistic diversity as an asset, while students
with disabilities have historically faced exclusion from general education and unequal access
to rigorous academic instruction.

These histories are distinct and should not be treated as interchangeable. However, they
provide essential context for understanding why educational equity requires intentional
examination of institutional practices, access to opportunity, and student outcomes. PPS
recognizes that historically underserved describes the experiences students have had within
educational systems, not their abilities, potential, or aspirations. Our focal student populations
bring significant cultural, linguistic, intellectual, and community strengths to our schools. This
presentation approaches educational equity through both an asset-based understanding of
students and a clear expectation of institutional accountability. The responsibility of the
district is to create and sustain educational conditions in which every student can achieve.

Overview of Student Achievement

The presentation examines 2025–26 OSAS results in English language arts and mathematics,
with attention to districtwide proficiency, selected grade-level benchmarks, statewide
comparisons, and patterns over time. The findings reveal areas of progress, significant
differences among student populations, and persistent challenges requiring focused
instructional attention.

Black Students
In 2025–26, 19.7% of Black students in PPS in Grades 3–8 met the state proficiency ​
standard in English language arts, compared with 26.3% of Black students statewide. ​
In mathematics, 13.2% of Black students in PPS met proficiency, compared with 17.8%
statewide. These results indicate that PPS trails the statewide performance of Black students
in both subjects. The presentation also examines grade-level benchmarks, including
third-grade literacy and mathematics performance, to better understand the consistency of
academic progress. The findings reinforce the need to examine access to rigorous
grade-level instruction, the effectiveness of academic interventions, and the consistency of
educational experiences for Black students across schools and regions.

Native American Students

In 2025–26, 22.0% of Native American students in PPS in Grades 3–8 met proficiency in
English language arts, compared with 26.3% statewide. In mathematics, 20.6% of Native
American students in PPS met proficiency, exceeding the statewide rate of 17.6%. The
mathematics results represent an area of relative strength that warrants further examination.
At the same time, proficiency in both subjects demonstrates the need for continued
improvement.

Because Native American student populations are relatively small in some grades and
regions, proficiency percentages can fluctuate significantly from year to year. Interpretation
must therefore consider the number of students tested, suppressed data, and multiyear
patterns. The district's response must continue to emphasize rigorous academic instruction
alongside culturally sustaining practices, meaningful family and Tribal community
engagement, and recognition of Native American students' identities, languages, and
histories.

Multilingual Learners

In 2025–26, 6.2% of PPS students identified as current English learners in Grades 3–8 met
proficiency standards in English language arts, and 8.5% met proficiency standards in
mathematics. These results reflect students currently receiving English language
development services and should not be interpreted as representative of all multilingual
students, including former English learners who have attained English proficiency. For
context, statewide proficiency rates for current English learners were 6.1% in English language
arts and 6.1% in mathematics. However, the statewide results include Grades 3–8 and 11,
while the PPS results reflect Grades 3–8 only. Because the grade spans differ, these ​
figures should not be interpreted as directly equivalent comparisons.

The results underscore the importance of strengthening the connection between language
development and academic achievement. English language development and rigorous
academic instruction must occur together. Students should have meaningful opportunities to
engage with grade-level content while receiving explicit language instruction and appropriate
scaffolds. The district's ongoing work includes strengthening integrated English language
development, collaborative instructional practices, and access to rigorous core instruction.
Continued examination of English language development services, assessment practices, and
targeted academic supports will be essential to accelerating student learning and
achievement.

Students with Disabilities

In 2025–26, 34.0% of PPS students receiving special education services in Grades 3–8 met
proficiency in English language arts, compared with 18.8% of students with disabilities
statewide. In mathematics, 31.6% met proficiency, compared with 15.9% statewide. These
results indicate substantially higher proficiency rates for PPS students with disabilities than
the corresponding statewide student population. While these comparisons are encouraging,
most PPS students receiving special education services are not yet meeting state proficiency
standards.

Continued improvement requires attention to meaningful access to grade-level instruction, the
effectiveness of specially designed instruction, individualized supports, and the alignment of
services with measurable academic progress.

District Response: Successes, Challenges, and Priorities

Following the review of achievement data, district leaders will provide focused presentations
addressing each of the four focal student populations. These presentations will highlight the
distinct histories and strengths of students, the successes and challenges observed in their
educational experiences, and the district's current work to strengthen academic achievement
and access.

The presentations will address:
   ●​ Black students: Educational experiences, academic outcomes, and efforts to
       strengthen culturally responsive instruction, student belonging, and ​
       achievement.
   ●​ Native American students: Academic opportunities, cultural affirmation, ​
       engagement with Native families and communities, and culturally sustaining
       educational practices.
   ●​ Multilingual learners: English language development, access to grade-level academic
       instruction, and coordinated language and instructional supports.
   ●​ Students with disabilities: Inclusive educational opportunities, specially designed
       instruction, meaningful access to grade-level content, and improved student outcomes.

While the needs and experiences of these student populations are distinct, the district's
collective responsibility is consistent: students must experience rigorous instruction,
appropriate supports, high expectations, and learning environments that recognize and build
upon their strengths.

From Investment to Instructional Impact

The presentation concludes by examining the relationship between district investments,
instructional implementation, and student achievement. PPS has made investments in
high-quality instructional materials, professional learning, assessment systems, and student
supports. These investments establish important foundations for improving academic
outcomes. However, the presence of instructional materials, professional development
opportunities, or intervention systems does not independently demonstrate that students are
experiencing improved learning. The district must increasingly examine how effectively these
investments translate into classroom practice and measurable academic progress.

This requires attention to four interconnected priorities:

   1.​ Consistent access to high-quality, grade-level instruction. Students must experience
       rigorous, evidence-based instruction supported by adopted instructional materials and
       effective teaching practices.
   2.​ Timely and effective academic supports. Students requiring additional instruction or
       intervention must receive responsive supports without unnecessary removal from
       grade-level learning opportunities.
   3.​ Instruction responsive to student strengths and needs. Educational practices ​
       must account for linguistic, cultural, disability-related, and individualized ​
       learning needs while maintaining high expectations.
   4.​ Evidence-based monitoring and continuous improvement. Student learning evidence
       must inform instructional adjustments, professional learning, resource allocation, and
       decisions about the effectiveness of district initiatives.

The district's responsibility extends beyond documenting what has been implemented. It
includes determining whether implementation is effective, whether students are benefiting,
and what changes are necessary when intended results are not achieved.

Implications for District Accountability and Continuous Improvement

The findings presented on November 4 reinforce the importance of connecting educational
equity to the district's ongoing improvement and accountability efforts. The Office of
Accountability, Equity, and Continuous Improvement will continue working across
departments to strengthen the use of student outcome data, support the examination of
instructional conditions, and promote greater coherence between district priorities,
investments, and results.

Particular attention must be given to identifying persistent patterns of underperformance,
examining variation across schools and regions, and ensuring that improvement efforts are
responsive to the experiences of focal student populations. The district must also recognize
that student achievement cannot be understood through assessment results alone.
Attendance, access to rigorous coursework, school climate, belonging, effective instructional
supports, and other educational conditions contribute to students' opportunities to learn and
succeed.

A meaningful approach to accountability requires the district to examine both student
outcomes and the conditions that influence those outcomes. This work is central to ensuring
that educational equity is reflected not only in district commitments, but also in the
educational experiences and academic progress of students.

Looking Ahead

The November 4 presentation is intended to support continued Board and district discussion
about the relationship between educational opportunity, student achievement, and
institutional responsibility. The findings underscore the need for sustained attention ​
to the academic experiences of historically underserved students, a deeper ​
understanding of variation across the district, and a disciplined approach to evaluating ​
the effectiveness of instructional practices and investments.

The district's goal is not simply to improve proficiency percentages. It is to ensure that
students leave Portland Public Schools prepared to pursue postsecondary education,
meaningful careers, economic opportunity, and futures of their own choosing. Our
responsibility is to create educational conditions that make those opportunities possible for
every student.

Board Consideration

This presentation is provided for the Board's information, discussion, and continued oversight
of district efforts to improve student outcomes and advance educational equity.

No Board action is requested at this time.
