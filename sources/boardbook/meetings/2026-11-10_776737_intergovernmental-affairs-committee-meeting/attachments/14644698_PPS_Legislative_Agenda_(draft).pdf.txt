Board Legislative Principles

The Portland Public Schools (PPS) Board of Education presents this legislative agenda to guide PPS’s
legislative engagement in the 2027 legislative session. Portland Public Schools is at a critical juncture as
we navigate growing challenges that directly impede our ability to carry out the core mission: to provide
rigorous, high-quality academic learning experiences that are inclusive and joyful. Decreasing enrollment,
limited revenue, and rising costs have led PPS to the point where the district is facing a $65 million dollar
deficit.

Persistent funding shortfalls, exacerbated by inflationary pressures and the rising cost of essential
services, are further complicated by the heightened social and emotional needs of our students.
Additionally, broader community instability—driven by reductions in federal support programs like
SNAP and Medicaid, alongside intensified immigration enforcement—places an unsustainable burden on
our students, educators, and school leadership. Together, these factors are placing an enormous burden on
schools and present threats to core student service and ongoing school stability. The PPS Board is
committed to working alongside state leaders to address these root challenges and place schools in
Portland and Oregon on a path to becoming the best in the nation.

Priority #1: Provide Adequate Funding to Meet Students Needs: ​

State education funding continues to fall behind rising operational costs, while recent federal reductions to
Medicaid and SNAP have further increased demands on schools and community partners. To preserve
critical student services and prevent devastating cuts to classroom resources, the Board calls on the
Legislature to at the very least fully fund the Current Service Level (CSL) established by Governor Tina
Kotek, proposed at $12.57 billion. This still leaves PPS with a shortfall and we encourage consideration
of any options that can close that gap. CSL must be recognized as the baseline threshold required to
maintain educational operations for most districts rather than an aspirational goal. Additionally, the Board
urges the Legislature to protect Corporate Activity Tax revenues for Student Success Act investments and
other dedicated K–12 education funds. Finally, the Board supports strategic efforts to raise revenue to
minimize cuts and even expand essential student-facing services.

Priority #2: Public Employee Retirement System Relief

PPS faces a steep increase in its PERS employer rate obligations due to the expiration of several side
accounts established by the sale of pension obligation bonds in 2002 and 2003. School districts should not
be forced to absorb escalating pension costs at the expense of classroom instruction and vital student
services. For the 2027–28 school year, PPS projects an estimated $65 million shortfall, with
approximately two thirds directly driven by rising PERS rates. To prevent severe impacts on student
learning, the PPS Board of Education urges the Governor and the State Legislature to allocate $500
million from the Education Stability Fund to buy down PERS rates for school districts statewide.

Priority #3: Adequately Fund Special Education Services

School districts have limited ability to control many of the fast-growing costs in their budgets, and
available funding has not kept pace with their federal obligation to provide a free and appropriate
education to all students regardless of disability status. As the cost of providing high-quality,
evidence-based practices for students with disabilities continues to balloon, the Board urges the
Legislature to provide school districts with support to address these escalating expenses, improving
long-term stability and protecting core student-facing services.

    ●​ Fully Fund the High Cost Disabilities Account: The state partially reimburses districts for
       eligible special education costs exceeding $30,000 per student. However, chronic underfunding of
       the High Cost Disabilities Account leaves districts responsible for absorbing the majority of these
       expenses. The Legislature should significantly increase appropriations to the account to provide
       meaningful reimbursement and reduce the financial burden on local school budgets.
    ●​ Lift the Special Education Funding Cap: Oregon's 11% cap on state special education funding
       reimbursement falls far short of the actual number of students receiving special education
       services. At PPS, 17% of students are qualified to receive services. As a result, districts serving
       higher percentages of students with disabilities must divert limited general fund resources to
       cover the growing gap. The Legislature should lift the cap and add adequate funding to ensure
       state funding more accurately reflects the actual cost of providing special education services.
    ●​ Fully Fund Regional Inclusive Services (RIS): Over the past several biennia, state funding for
       Regional Inclusive Services has not kept pace with the increasing costs of delivering these critical
       services. The Board urges the Legislature to align funding with the true cost of service delivery
       and fully fund the program at its current service level.


Priority #4: Modernize the State School Fund and The Quality Education Model

Oregon's K–12 funding formula was established over three decades ago and no longer reflects the true,
complex cost of educating today's diverse student population. The Board calls on the Legislature to
establish a statewide School Funding Modernization Commission—with robust stakeholder engagement
that includes families, educators, district leadership, and school board members —to evaluate and update
the formula to ensure every school district can deliver high-quality, inclusive education. Any updates to
the State School Fund formula must be implemented thoughtfully, ensuring that the revised model adds
new resources to meet district needs rather than simply redistributing existing funds.

Priority #5: Strengthen Educator Preparation Programs

Strengthen educator preparation programs to ensure that new teachers are fully equipped to serve students
with complex learning needs. This includes ensuring that new teachers are trained in evidence-based
practices to effectively support students who are learning English and students who are struggling to learn
to read. The state legislature can accomplish this by:
     ●​ Codifying standards for literacy instruction developed by the Early Literacy Educator Preparation
         Council;
     ●​ Expanding funding for Educator Preparation Programs (EPPs) to strengthen program capacity;
     ●​ Bolstering certification programs to ensure that all new teachers in Oregon and teachers from out
         of state demonstrate proficiency in instructional delivery.

Priority #6: Reject additional requirements that are not paired with additional funding

Ensure every new administrative or legal requirement placed on school districts is fully supported by state
funding. Amid existing budget shortfalls, unfunded requirements force districts to divert resources
directly away from students and classrooms. The state legislature must also examine existing mandates to
identify and eliminate costly burdens that compromise instructional funding.
    ●​ Instructional Time: The Board supports expanding instructional time but adequate and
         sustainable funding must reflect the full operational costs of an expanding school year.
    ●​ Class Sizes: The Board recognizes that class size is an important factor in student learning. The
         Board opposes making class size a mandatory subject of collective bargaining unless it comes
         with specific funding to support smaller class sizes.

Priority #7: Modernize the structure of the Public Employee Retirement System (PERS): Currently,
school districts are obligated to contribute to the retirement pensions of both current and retired
employees. Funding for the pension system comes directly out of the State School Fund—the same source
that supports core student services. As policymakers work to evaluate how Oregon's school funding
compares to other states, PERS obligations distort the picture because they are included in those funding
formulas. To clarify how much state funding actually directly supports student services versus pension
obligations, the state should examine options to better separate and manage PERS costs.
