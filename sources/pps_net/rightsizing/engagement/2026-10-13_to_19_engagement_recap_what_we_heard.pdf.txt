                             Rightsizing Engagement Recap: What We Heard
                                          October 13 - 19, 2026

Executive Summary
This memo is a weekly summary created by the Data & Accountability team to share public feedback on
the proposed Rightsizing Scenarios. It covers community input gathered between October 13 and
October 19, 2026. These weekly reports exist to make the decision-making process open and
transparent.

During the week of October 13–19, Portland Public Schools hosted two public engagement sessions:
    ●​ The Village Table | October 14
    ●​ Community Voice: Special Education Families | October 15
    ●​ Board of Education Community Connection | October 17
    ●​ School Impact: Northeast Region | Oct 17
    ●​ Latino/a/e Community Conversation | October 19

This summary is based on 1,501 survey responses and participation across five community events. 95%
of participants represent school communities directly affected by potential closures, boundary shifts, or
program changes. Across all regions, families identified transportation, safe walking routes, and keeping
student peer cohorts together as essential supports during any transition. Community members raised
equity concerns, urging the district to protect Special Education and Dual Language Immersion programs
while addressing physical facility inequalities between buildings. Regionally, priorities varied from middle
school feeder patterns in the Southeast and aging facilities in the West to preserving Title I services and
cultural history in the North and Northeast.

In this What We Heard memo, we present survey responses disaggregated by impact region, as well as
summaries of what we heard at community engagement events across the district.

Introduction
Every Monday, from when the rightsizing plans were released on October 5th until Superintendent
Armstrong gives her recommendation to the board, the Data and Accountability team will gather and
summarize public feedback. The purpose of these reports is to be open and clear. We want to share
what community members have told us and explain how we will use that information. This memo shares
what we have heard from our community between October 13 - 19, 2026.

Scenario Feedback Survey Findings to Date
As of October 19th, we have received 1,501 responses from parents/guardians, PPS staff, students, and
community members representing all of our school communities! About 95% of respondents
represented school communities affected by the rightsizing scenario proposals in some way, including
school closure, school consolidation, program changes, or boundary changes. See Tables 1-3 for a
breakdown of respondent demographics, and see Appendix A for counts of responses by school to date.

Open-Ended Response Analysis:
The survey asked the following open-ended questions:
    ●​ What specific support (e.g., transportation to school, students staying with their classmates)
        would you need if school changes occur all at once instead of over several years?
    ●​ What specific resources or help should the district provide to support schools affected by
        rightsizing?
    ●​ Our goal is to create a model that is fair and equitable to all communities. Did we miss any
        important equity issues, community history, or student needs in creating these models? If yes,
        please tell us what we missed and how we can address it.
    ●​ What is one thing you would like us to know about your school community?

Below, we present emerging findings from all open-ended questions across four themes: supports and
resources, equity analysis, emerging themes, and emerging questions. We further disaggregate these
responses by regional cluster.

Across all regions, families say that reliable transportation to and from school and keeping friend groups
together are essential supports for students experiencing change. Many respondents stressed that the
district must provide free transportation, clear bus routes, and safe walking paths across busy roads.
Parents also strongly agree that students should move together in whole classrooms or grade-level
cohorts so they do not lose their primary social support network. Additionally, community members
frequently asked PPS to retain familiar teachers and hire more mental health counselors, social workers,
and transition aides to help students adapt to new environments.

Families also cautioned the district not to repeat past mistakes that harmed vulnerable groups.
Responses repeatedly highlighted that low-income students, families of color, English language learners,
and Special Education (SPED) students face the highest risk during school closures. Community members
also pointed out physical inequalities between buildings, stating that moving students into older facilities
without working heating, air conditioning, or safe play areas creates an unfair learning environment.

Finally, a major theme across all open-ended feedback is a deep lack of trust in district leadership and
data. About one in five respondents questioned whether enrollment projections are accurate. Families
expressed frustration with the decision-making timeline and requested a clear multi-year roadmap
rather than repeated minor adjustments every few years. Concerns about class size, building capacity,
and losing tight-knit school cultures were also widespread.

North Region
Feedback from the North region (Jefferson and Roosevelt cluster) strongly focuses on racial equity,
historical community impact, and Dual Language Immersion programs. Nearly a third of comments (32%)
urged the district to protect Spanish Dual Language Immersion programs from being split up or shut
down. Community members cited North Portland's history of gentrification, displacement, and past
school closures, warning the district not to place an unfair burden on Black, Brown, and low-income
families. Responses also stressed the need to preserve Title I wraparound services, free meal programs,
and culturally responsive staff who reflect the student body.

Northeast Region
In the Northeast region (Grant and McDaniel cluster), families talked about teacher retention and
keeping academic tracks intact. Almost a third of respondents urged the district to keep experienced

teachers and support staff in place rather than making budget cuts or frequent staff reassignments.
Parents also expressed strong concern about Dual Language Immersion pathways so students do not
drop out mid-way through their education. Finally, responses highlighted the need to rebalance
boundaries between overcrowded facilities and under-enrolled neighboring schools without breaking
apart cohesive neighborhood communities.

Southeast Region
In the Southeast region (Cleveland and Franklin cluster), community feedback centers heavily on middle
school transitions, feeder patterns, and student traffic safety. Over a quarter of comments from this
region specifically focused on the shift between K-8 schools and standalone middle schools. Parents
emphasized that younger students will need dedicated yellow bus service and crossing guards if forced
to cross busy roads like Powell, 82nd, and Division Streets. Keeping student cohorts and friend groups
together was another top priority, as parents worry that splitting kids up across different schools will
hurt their social and emotional well-being.

West Region
In the West region (Ida B. Wells and Lincoln cluster), community concerns centered on aging school
buildings and geographic barriers. Almost a third of families discussed facility conditions, like lack of air
conditioning, seismic safety concerns, and of space in receiving schools. Because Westside
neighborhoods feature steep hills, winding roads, and very few sidewalks, parents stressed that walking
to school is unsafe and that district-provided busing will be necessary. Families in this cluster also
expressed the highest level of distrust toward district enrollment figures, demanding raw data and
financial transparency before any closures occur.

Emerging Questions
Community members brought forth many questions about the proposed scenarios. Common questions
themes include:
   ●​ How will students travel safely to new schools, and will free busing be provided?
   ●​ What will happen to specialized programs like Dual Language Immersion (DLI)?
   ●​ How will the district guarantee that friend groups and student cohorts stay together?
   ●​ Are receiving school buildings large enough and in good condition to handle more students?
   ●​ Why is the district closing neighborhood schools instead of cutting administrative spending?
   ●​ How can families trust the district's enrollment numbers?
   ●​ What will happen to closed buildings?

Opportunities
     ●​ District leaders have the opportunity to shift the narrative and administrative process from
        closing schools to merging two schools. By intentionally integrating school cultures, staff,
        traditions, and names, PPS can honor community history and lessen transition trauma.
     ●​ Village Table Members encourage PPS staff to build a unified, public data dashboard
        containing audited facility costs, enrollment projection methodologies, DLI metrics, and
        staffing models.
     ●​ The district can pioneer robust transition protocols specifically tailored for SPED,
        neurodivergent, and DLI students—including transition days, physical facility retrofits, and
        dedicated spaces for community partners (SUN, RESJ, Childcare).
     ●​ Village Table members want this space to be used for two-way communication - instead of just
        making comments and identifying questions, members want meetings to be a space where
        district representatives can provide transparent answers.


Questions for District Leadership
     ●​ How will staffing allocations, seniority, and reassignments operate under existing PAT contracts
        during closures vs. mergers, and on what exact timeline will staff and families see school-level
        staffing plans?
     ●​ What specific instructional and operational metrics define a "well-resourced school," and how
        will PPS guarantee that increased school size translates into better teaching and support
        rather than just higher student density and lost elective/para support?
     ●​ How has the district accounted for physical accessibility (elevators/ADA), classroom capacity,
        sensory needs, and elopement/safety protections for SPED focus classroom students being
        moved to receiving schools?
     ●​ How will PPS mitigate the loss of Title I funding at specific schools (e.g., Markham,
        Brentwood), prevent DLI consolidation from creating racial isolation?
     ●​ How was the decision made to consolidate DLI programs into campuses? And if this proposal
        is moved forward, what safety plans will be in place to protect vulnerable students and
        families?
     ●​ If rightsizing moves forward, there will be schools remaining that have enrollment below the
        identified thresholds due to geographic isolation and other factors. Will they be left vulnerable
        for future rightsizing? How will these buildings be served/will they also be well-resourced?

                                             Appendix C:
                        Community Voice: Disability Voices (October 15) Summary



Location: Kellogg Middle School
Number of community members in attendance: 44

                                             What We Heard

Some families that attended this session expressed that K-8 schools can offer a supportive environment
for neurodivergent students. These parents shared that K-8s can help keep special education students in
public schools, even if district data shows similar academic results between K-8 and 6-8 models overall.
While some acknowledge that K-8 teachers handle heavy workloads, parents value the strong
community culture and effective special education support these schools provide.

Parents involved in the Disability Voices community want clear information about future staffing levels,
funding, and class sizes. Many disagree with the idea that larger schools are better, noting that crowded
classrooms overstimulate neurodivergent children. Families are asking for specific student-to-teacher
ratios, and want information about staffing numbers right away. Without concrete details on support
staff, parents find it difficult to trust the proposed changes.

Families worry that building plans focus heavily on square footage and building age rather than actual
accessibility and the physical needs of the communities served by a building. They noted that buildings
like Creston feature specialized equipment, including acoustic carpeting and emergency deaf reader
boards. Parents want to know if receiving schools will match these essential physical accommodations.
Additionally, parents are concerned that moving the Columbia Regional Deaf and Hard of Hearing
program away from Creston will disrupt a closely integrated community. Moving the program to a new
location would require retraining new staff because all staff in the building support the students in this
program, not just the program-specific educators.

Questions Raised
   ●​ How are students and staff being engaged and included in this process?
   ●​ Will receiving school buildings have the same physical accommodations and trained staff?
   ●​ What will staffing levels, special education support, and class sizes actually look like?

                                           Appendix D:
                   Board of Education Community Connection (October 17) Summary

Location: McDaniel High School
Number of Attendees: 102

                                             What We Heard
In this Community Connection, many attendees acknowledged that the district is facing a structural
deficit and declining enrollment, and agreed that systemic changes are necessary. However, many
attendees expressed that the timeline is a barrier to feeling ready for a decision. Participants want more
transparency in how the proposed scenarios came to be and expressed the desire to evaluate multiple
alternatives.

Some families expressed concern about how Special Education and neurodivergent students are
included in the proposed scenarios. Some expressed that the needs of students receiving special
education services were “treated as an afterthought.” Moving high-needs students into larger schools
may threaten their physical and emotional stability. Additionally, some participants expressed concern
that the proposed shifts to Dual Language Immersion (DLI) programs create unfair barriers for immigrant
families and students of color. Relocating or splitting DLI programs forces working parents to choose
between cultural education and local neighborhood access.

Still others expressed concern regarding commuting to school. Redrawing boundaries sometimes
disrupts walkability and may require multiple bus transfers that strain parents without personal
transportation. Additionally, merging campuses threatens to strip Title 1 funding from sites, while leaving
vacant buildings in neighborhood hubs. Instead of rushing to close schools by December, some
community members want the district to explore state rainy-day funds and build a clearer
implementation model that proves it will not harm the students who need support the most.

Questions Raised
   ●​ What specific data and facility trade-offs justify closing or moving individual schools?
   ●​ How will the district protect Special Education (SPED) students from overcrowding and loss of
       services?
   ●​ How will the district address the commuter burdens placed on working families?
   ●​ Are there examples of other districts where school closures at this scale improved student
       outcomes?
   ●​ How will the district prevent vulnerable schools from losing Title 1 funding when boundaries
       change?

                                            Appendix F:
                      Latino/a/e Community Conversation (October 19) Summary

Location: Hosford Middle School & Virtual
Number of Attendees: 20 in personal 17 virtual

                                            What We Heard
Many of the Latino/a/e community members in attendance opposed PPS’s proposed school closures and
Dual Language Immersion (DLI) consolidations. Speakers stressed that moving neighborhood DLI
programs into consolidated single-site schools may segregate learning environments, expose
undocumented families to immigration enforcement, and place heavy commute burdens on working
parents.

Participants want more information about why this decision was made. Some asked for research and
evidence that whole-school DLI models are as effective or better for students’ academic and social
development than having co-located programs.

Families were also concerned that there has not been enough engagement with the Latino/a/e
community broadly, wanting more opportunities to respond and be engaged about changes, especially
those that affect the Spanish-language DLI program.



Questions Raised
   ●​ Is there research or data that shows that whole-school Dual Language Immersion programs have
       equal or better academic or social outcomes than co-located neighborhood programs?
   ●​ What transportation and safety plans exist to address bus rides, hazardous driving corridors, and
       winter travel?
   ●​ What protective measures will PPS implement to ensure undocumented parents and extended
       family members are not exposed to ICE during school drop-offs and pick-ups?
   ●​ Will consolidating Dual Language Immersion programs reduce the overall number of seats
       available for native Spanish speakers, and what will the ratio of native speakers to neighborhood
       students be at receiving schools?
   ●​ Why are program changes happening now instead of making gradual adjustments?
