                             Rightsizing Engagement Recap: What We Heard
                                     November 3 - November 9, 2026

                                        Executive Summary
This memo is a summary of public feedback on the proposed rightsizing scenarios. It covers community
input gathered between November 3 - November 9, 2026.

As of November 3rd, we have received 3,559 responses from parents/guardians, PPS staff, students, and
community members representing all of our school communities. About 91% of respondents
represented school communities affected by the rightsizing scenario proposals in some way, including
school closure, school consolidation, program changes, or boundary changes.

In this What We Heard memo, we present survey data collected to date, including a deep dive into the
perspective of staff and families in schools identified as receiving schools in the rightsizing scenarios.

Introduction
Every Monday, from when the rightsizing plans were released on October 5th until Superintendent
Armstrong gives her recommendation to the board, the Data and Accountability team will gather and
summarize public feedback. The purpose of these reports is to be open and clear. We want to share
what community members have told us and explain how we will use that information. This memo shares
what we have heard from our community between November 3 - 9, 2026.

Scenario Feedback Survey Findings to Date
As of November 9th, we have received 3,559 responses from parents/guardians, PPS staff, students, and
community members representing all of our school communities! Responses have been collected in
English, Simplified Chinese, Spanish, Vietnamese, and Russian. The survey is available in Somali, but no
Somali-language responses have been collected. About 91% of respondents represented school
communities affected by the rightsizing scenario proposals in some way, including school closure, school
consolidation, program changes, or boundary changes. See Tables 1-3 for a breakdown of respondent

Open-Ended Response Analysis

The survey asked the following open-ended questions:
    ●​ What specific support (e.g., transportation to school, students staying with their classmates)
        would you need if school changes occur all at once instead of over several years?
    ●​ What specific resources or help should the district provide to support schools affected by
        rightsizing?
    ●​ Our goal is to create a model that is fair and equitable to all communities. Did we miss any
        important equity issues, community history, or student needs in creating these models? If yes,
        please tell us what we missed and how we can address it.
    ●​ What is one thing you would like us to know about your school community?

This week, we are focusing our data analysis on the experiences and perspectives of receiving school
stakeholders. The majority of rightsizing discussions thus far have focused on the experience of closing
schools or moving programs. This week, we are pivoting to consider the experience of staff and families
who are part of school communities with potentially changing boundaries to identify their needs and
concerns. Below, we present the perspective of families and staff in receiving schools.

Receiving Schools Family and Community
A total of 1,245 parents/families from schools facing an attendance boundary change responded to the
survey. A primary concern among receiving families centers on building capacity, facility strain, and
expanding class sizes. Parents worry that absorbing students from closing schools will result in crowded
classrooms and strain shared building infrastructure (e.g., cafeterias, gymnasiums, libraries, and
playgrounds). Families emphasize that accepting boundary shifts must not come at the expense of higher
student-to-teacher ratios or overcrowding, calling on the district to guarantee physical building upgrades
and baseline staffing allocations.

Transportation logistics and student commute safety are also a major worry. Parents highlight that
redrawing boundary lines forces children to travel further distances across busy thoroughfares, major
traffic corridors, or unfamiliar neighborhoods. Families stress the loss of walkable neighborhood school
zones and demand district-provided busing, expanded crossing guard coverage, and coordinated
safe-routes-to-school infrastructure to ensure students can commute safely.

Preserving student cohorts and peer relationships is a high priority for receiving families. Respondents
repeatedly request that boundary lines keep neighborhood clusters and friend groups together rather
than splitting feeder patterns across different receiving schools. Parents point out that social continuity is
critical for student wellbeing during major district transitions, urging PPS to align feeder pathways so that
elementary student cohorts can progress into middle and high school together.

Receiving families emphasize that welcoming displaced students requires dedicated social-emotional and
mental health support. Parents request that the district fund full-time counselors, social workers, and
community liaison staff to help integrate new families and address transition stress. They also call for
district-backed funding for joint community-building events, welcoming committees, and after-school
programming (such as SUN community school programs) to foster a sense of belonging and
culture-blending between existing and incoming student populations.

Receiving Schools Staff Feedback
A total of 179 staff members who reported being affiliated with proposed receiving schools responded to
the survey. The primary concern for receiving school educators is operational staffing logistics. Staff want
clear, early timelines for full-time equivalent allocations, unassigned staff lists, and transfer notices
ahead of the end of the school year. They warn against repeating past late-spring staffing delays, which
hinder master scheduling and curriculum planning. Educators also express concern over contractually
mandated, seniority-based transfers, noting that shifting teachers and support staff without considering
existing school cultures or programmatic needs can disrupt working environments at receiving schools.

Staff also expressed concern that expanding boundaries to absorb students from adjacent,
higher-income neighborhoods would lower a receiving school's percentage of low-income students
below federal funding thresholds. Staff stress that combining distinct student populations requires
deliberate resources rather than assuming student bodies will integrate naturally.

Educators also reported that current special education case managers, speech-language pathologists,
counselors, and behavior specialists are already operating at capacity. They emphasize that absorbing
new students will significantly increase caseloads and behavioral support needs. Staff request that the
district proactively allocate additional special education staff, mental health counselors, and SUN
community school programming to receiving buildings before enrollment increases occur, rather than
reacting after problems arise.

Finally, staffed stress that merging school communities involves far more than moving student rosters in
a database. They request dedicated, district-funded time and financial resources for joint staff planning
sessions, coordinated PTA transitions, and structured welcoming events for incoming families. Staff
emphasize that successful school consolidation depends on proactive community-building efforts
designed to help incoming students and staff feel welcomed and supported from day one.
