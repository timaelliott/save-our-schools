The purpose of this report is to present a preliminary analysis of community feedback about Portland
Public School’s rightsizing process. This report serves two purposes. First, it exists to provide
transparency and serve as an active listening tool to reflect back what we are hearing from community
members regarding the rightsizing process. Additionally, it provides a comprehensive summary of what
we have heard from community members between October 6-28, 2026, which can provide guidance for
PPS Leadership and the Board of Education as decisions are made regarding rightsizing.

Synthesizing What We Heard: Process and Analytic Methods
Throughout the month of October, there have been two primary pathways for collecting community
voice: a feedback survey and community engagement events. The survey is available in all PPS-supported
languages, and asks community members to give feedback on rightsizing, rate scenarios against the
guiding principles, and provide insight into the tradeoffs involved in rightsizing. Respondents are also
given the opportunity to respond to open-ended questions to share their perspectives. The district
hosted community-specific (e.g., Black/African American families, Spanish speaking families, Native
families, etc.) engagement events as well as general listening sessions (e.g., Board Community
Connections).

These two forms of engagement are designed to complement one another. Surveys are wide-reaching
and can accommodate a larger volume of feedback. Community engagement sessions create space for
people to come together in affinity spaces to share feedback and ask questions. Culturally specific
community engagement sessions (specifically for Black/African American families, Native families,
Latino/a/e families, and Disability Voices) were created to hear directly from communities that have
historically been underrepresented in surveys. PPS’ Equity Lens calls us to prioritize the outcomes of
focal student groups (Native, Black, multilingual, special education, low-income), and these culturally and
community-specific engagements are designed to support that priority.

All community feedback, from the survey as well as community engagement sessions, are analyzed by
members of the Data and Accountability department. Responses are reviewed individually, and then
deductively coded to align with the Guiding Principles established by Resolution 7307. Next, responses
are analyzed inductively, looking for themes, concerns, and questions emerging from community
responses. Evidence supporting and challenging all themes are reviewed, as are counternarratives that
may arise. Additionally, concerns that may not be repeated frequently but do warrant follow up (e.g.,
“What will happen to food pantry infrastructure that has been installed in schools that are slated to
close?”).

Emerging Themes

Across the community engagement sessions and responses to the Rightsizing Scenario Feedback Survey,
five clear themes emerged. Below, we describe each theme in detail.

Something must be done
Despite widespread pushback against specific scenario details, community members broadly recognize
the district’s underlying financial reality and long-term enrollment decline. Parents, educators, and
community leaders acknowledge that maintaining small, under-resourced campuses has already
diminished the student experience by reducing student’s ability to engage with art, music, physical
                                                                                                           3
education, counselors, and reading interventionists. Families see the real impacts of recent budget cuts
on daily instruction and agree that status-quo operations cannot continue, but they urge the district to
exhaust central administrative cuts before closing neighborhood classrooms

Neighborhood schools are community pillars
Across the district, respondents ranked strong neighborhood schools as their highest priority among the
school board's guiding principles, with many families viewing local schools as irreplaceable community
hubs. Community members emphasize that neighborhood schools preserve local history, foster
multi-grade relationships, and protect connections with trusted educators. Walkability is also a central
concern, as parents worry that closing neighborhood campuses forces young children onto busy streets
and lengthy bus rides. Beyond daily logistics, families associate the loss of neighborhood schools with a
diminished sense of physical and emotional safety, and worry that removing a local school strips
neighborhoods of community assets like SUN after-school care and local volunteers.

Data skepticism and the demand for transparency
Community members express skepticism regarding the enrollment forecasts and financial metrics used
to justify the proposed rightsizing scenarios. Survey respondents and meeting attendees repeatedly
challenge the accuracy of Portland State University’s (PSU) demographic models, pointing to historical
forecasting errors and noting that projections fail to account for upcoming housing developments.
Families criticize the criteria used to evaluate school viability and demand independent demographic
audits, transparent data dashboards, and clear proof of central office administrative spending cuts
before neighborhood schools face closure.

Clarity and the student experience
Participants across all stakeholder groups emphasize that they cannot evaluate rightsizing proposals
without seeing comprehensive and concrete operational plans. Families want clear frameworks detailing
school-level staffing allocations, bus routes, traffic studies, and safe street crossing infrastructure for
routes crossing major corridors or river bridges. Furthermore, parents ask for explicit menus of support
that outline how receiving schools will handle transition trauma, preserve special education pullout
spaces, and maintain full after-school childcare capacity.

Concerns about Spanish DLI consolidation
Proposed consolidations of Dual Language Immersion (DLI) programs raise significant concerns regarding
equity, access, safety, and commute burdens. While the district frames whole-school DLI models as a way
to optimize bilingual staffing and resources, Latino/a/e families and immersion educators worry that
centralizing programs will isolate native Spanish speakers and reduce heritage-language seats. Working
parents without private transportation warn that long commutes will force them to pull their children
out of immersion pathways, while families share fears that cross-city transit heightens exposure to
federal immigration enforcement (ICE).

Tensions and Tradeoffs in Rightsizing
Analysis of community input also provides insight into the tensions and tradeoffs that families are facing
when making hard decisions about rightsizing schools. Tension exists between centering neighborhood
schools and maintaining specialized choice programs. In survey data, 67% of affected respondents feel
the proposed scenarios over-emphasize choice programs at the expense of foundational neighborhood
schools. At the same time, families enrolled in focus options defend their specialized models. These
families emphasize that alternative educational approaches provide a sense of belonging, hands-on
                                                                                                            4
learning, and sensory safety nets for neurodivergent and marginalized students that traditional
neighborhood schools cannot replicate.

Additionally, community feedback highlights conflicting perspectives on school size and programmatic
access. Some parents of upper-elementary students celebrate the prospect of moving from K–8 facilities
into larger, comprehensive middle schools where children can access broader electives, sports teams,
and specialized academic tracks. Conversely, other families mourn the potential loss of small school
environments, sharing that intimate campus settings foster strong multi-grade relationships, predictable
routines, and necessary social-emotional stability for vulnerable learners.

Discussions on building consolidations surface questions regarding workforce and equity. Collective
bargaining provisions with the Portland Association of Teachers (PAT) rely heavily on seniority for staff
reassignments during school closures. However, Black, Native, and Latino/a/e families raise concerns that
strict adherence to seniority rules will displace educators of color and bilingual teachers who serve as
mentors for historically underserved students. This would create a direct conflict between contractual
procedures and district equity commitments.

Finally, distinct regional narratives are emerging, illustrating competing definitions of equity and risk
across the district. Black, Native, and Latino/a/e families in North, Northeast, and Southeast Portland
emphasize that historically marginalized, lower-income communities bear a disproportionate share of
school closures, boundary shifts, and neighborhood disruptions compared to protected Westside
campuses. Meanwhile, Westside families argue that their region's steep topography, winding roads,
traffic congestion, and river bridge crossings create severe physical hazards for student transit.

Summary and Conclusion
This overview of emerging themes across all engagement types reveals questions, priorities, and
tensions that are emerging through the community engagement process. Next, we provide more
in-depth analysis of the feedback received from each track of the engagement process specifically.

First, we present findings and emerging themes from the feedback survey, which show specific insights
revealed through quantitative and qualitative analyses of survey data. After, we present emerging
themes from community engagement sessions, which provide more specific insights into culturally and
community specific engagement groups.

Alignment with the Guiding Principles

Well-Resourced, Sustainable Schools. Community members agree that every school needs full-time
teachers, art, music, physical education, counselors, and support staff. However, families express
skepticism that closing buildings will actually guarantee these resources in receiving schools. Parents
point out that past district budget cuts stripped away specialists and paraprofessionals, making them
worry that consolidated schools will simply become larger and more crowded without receiving extra
staff or better academic programs. To trust this principle, families need transparent, itemized staffing
plans and clear proof that central office administrative overhead is being reduced before classroom
resources are touched.

Safe & Healthy Learning Environments. Families emphasize physical building safety, emotional stability,
and facility quality. Community members stress that receiving schools must have modern infrastructure,
including working HVAC and air conditioning, seismic upgrades, up-to-date classroom technology, and
safe, accessible playgrounds. Beyond physical structures, parents highlight the need for mental health
support, full-time counselors, and dedicated transition specialists to help students process the emotional
stress of losing their familiar school environments. For neurodivergent students and children with
Individualized Education Programs (IEPs), safe environments also mean preserving quiet sensory spaces,
predictable routines, and physical accessibility like ramps and elevators.

Strong Neighborhood Schools. Respondents expressed civic and emotional attachment to their local
neighborhood schools, describing them as essential community hubs that tie neighborhoods together.
Parents worry that closing neighborhood schools damages local vitality, divides tight-knit communities
across multiple attendance boundaries, and eliminates walkability. Families emphasize that
neighborhood schools foster multi-grade relationships, volunteer networks, and local after-school
programs like SUN care. Many community members urge the district to view neighborhood schools as
irreplaceable assets worth protecting, suggesting boundary adjustments and community mergers rather
than outright school closures.

PPS Equity Lens. Feedback regarding equity reveals major concerns about an uneven burden falling on
low-income, Title I, and historically marginalized communities, particularly in North and Northeast
Portland. Families question why diverse schools face frequent boundary shifts, closures, and program
moves while wealthier Westside campuses appear protected. Additionally, respondents worry that
redrawing boundaries will strip receiving schools of their Title I status, taking away essential reading
specialists and student services. Parents of Special Education students, English language learners, and
immigrant families emphasize that true equity requires protecting vulnerable learners from repeated
system disruptions and providing dedicated support during any transition.

Emerging Hot Topics

Dual Language Immersion (DLI). Community feedback on Dual Language Immersion reflects a tension
between program sustainability and neighborhood access. While some families understand the district's
goal of consolidating DLI into full-building campus models to guarantee full-time bilingual staff and
resources, many parents express alarm over proposed program moves. Families worry that centralizing
DLI programs will isolate native language speakers, cause racial segregation, and force long travel
distances that working families cannot maintain. Parents of native Spanish and Mandarin speakers
demand clear safety plans, preserved heritage-language seats, and guaranteed transportation so that DLI
programs remain accessible to the communities they were created to serve.

K-8 Schools and Focus Options. Families connected to K–8 schools and focus options, such as Sunnyside
Environmental School, Creative Science, and the Metropolitan Learning Center, strongly oppose
removing middle grades or dismantling their unique models. Parents and students explain that K–8
continuity provides emotional stability, reduces disciplinary issues, and offers crucial sensory safety nets
for neurodivergent learners during difficult middle school transitions. Community members emphasize
that hands-on, alternative, and environmental learning environments cannot be replicated in large
comprehensive middle schools. Families urge the district to preserve these focus options and learn from
their successful practices rather than closing or shrinking them.

Transportation and Traveling to School. Transportation is one of the most urgent concerns raised across
all survey responses. Families express widespread alarm over losing walkable and bikeable neighborhood
routes, pointing out that proposed changes will force young children onto long bus rides or
multi-transfer public transit trips. Parents raise serious safety concerns regarding dangerous high-speed
street crossings, major traffic bottlenecks, and geographic hazards like mountain road closures and river
bridge crossings. Working parents emphasize that guaranteed district busing, crossing guards, and safe
bike routes are non-negotiable requirements if school boundaries are redrawn.

Building Capacity, Physical Accessibility, and Dedicated Spaces. Families raise serious concerns about
how receiving school capacity is calculated for special education. Parents and staff point out that special
education requires dedicated spaces for sensory regulation, speech therapy, behavioral de-escalation,
and pullout instruction. If these rooms are treated as standard classrooms in building capacity models, it
would misrepresent available space. Additionally, parents highlight physical accessibility barriers in older
receiving buildings, noting that missing elevators, ramps, and secure perimeters create safety and
mobility risks for students with physical disabilities or elopement concerns.

Deaf and Hard of Hearing (D/HH) & CRIS Program at Creston. A prominent theme in Southeast Portland
involves the Columbia Regional Inclusive Services (CRIS) Deaf and Hard of Hearing (D/HH) program
hosted at Creston Elementary. For over 20 years, Creston has built a deeply integrated, inclusive culture
where D/HH students learn alongside their hearing peers. Parents and staff express strong alarm over
scenario proposals that move the D/HH program away from Creston to align with middle school feeder
paths at Mt. Tabor. Families argue that separating D/HH students from their long-time hearing
classmates and experienced general education teachers breaks apart a supportive community fabric.
Community members demand that D/HH students remain with their Creston peers, and suggest offering
American Sign Language (ASL) classes to all students and staff at receiving sites.

Intensive Skills Classrooms (ISC) and Staffing Ratios. Families with students in Intensive Skills
Classrooms (ISC) and self-contained programs express fear over program relocations and staffing
                                                                                                      11
shortages. Parents point out that recent district budget cuts have already reduced educational assistants
and paraprofessionals. Families worry that moving ISC programs to new sites without guaranteed staffing
ratios will overwhelm teachers and leave high-needs students without adequate one-on-one support.

Emerging Themes Across All Stakeholder Groups
Community members across every region questioned the enrollment models used by the district,
pointing out past errors in small-area forecasting and census data. Parents worried that projections are
failing to account for new housing developments or recent enrollment stability in feeder schools.

Families also expressed concern over losing walkable and bikeable neighborhood schools. Parents
highlighted severe transit barriers, including dangerous street crossings, long bus rides, mountain road
closures, traffic backups on Germantown Road, and hazards across the St. Johns Bridge.
                                                                                                          13
Many community members emphasized that closing small schools or K–8 focus options removes sensory
accommodations, elevators, and predictable routines for students with Individualized Education
Programs (IEPs). Moving high-needs students into larger, busier middle schools threatens their emotional
and academic stability. However this belief was not universally held; some parents/guardians
communicated that they were hopeful that their 6-8 grade students would have additional learning
opportunities at a comprehensive middle school.

Families were concerned that shifting attendance boundaries could drop receiving schools below federal
Title I eligibility thresholds, stripping away reading specialists, support staff, and community resources.
Working parents also stressed that guaranteed after-school care (SUN programs and fee-based childcare)
is essential.

Community members expressed mixed views on centralizing DLI into full-building campus models. While
consolidated programs offer full-time bilingual staff and resources, families worry that pulling DLI out of
neighborhood schools creates racial and language isolation. Moreover, parents warned that moving DLI
programs long distances may force working families to leave the program.

Finally, participants consistently argued that the district must cut central office administrative overhead
and salaries before closing neighborhood schools or cutting classroom staff.

Feedback from Native Family Engagements
Native families who attended this engagement session strongly urged the district and the teacher's union
to ensure seniority rules do not push out impactful BIPOC teachers and underrepresented staff during
school mergers. Similarly, Native community members expressed frustration over executive pay raises
during a budget crisis, calling for cuts to central office salaries to support students.

Native families also raised concerns that digital surveys and other forms of communication
disproportionately represent white households and leave out community members without technology.

Feedback from Black/African American Family Engagements
Black families who attended this engagement session raised concerns that low-income and historically
marginalized schools in East and Northeast Portland face a disproportionate share of closures and
program moves compared to protected Westside campuses. Participants emphasized that school
consolidations must intentionally protect Black cultural hubs and community connections in North and
Northeast Portland. Additionally, Black parents and community members expressed concern regarding
staff of color during school closures and mergers. Specifically, many Black families worry that strict union
seniority rules will force out newer, highly effective, teachers of color who serve as vital mentors for
Black students.

Furthermore, Black leaders and parents expressed frustration with non-minority residents for misusing
terms like "redlining," "segregation," and "busing" to describe boundary changes. Similarly, Black
community members expressed frustration with the false narratives circulating that equate Black
student populations with Special Education programs, demanding public clarification from district
leadership. These families expressed that discourse about rightsizing is harming Black students and
families.
                                                                                                        14
Feedback from Latino/a/e Family Engagements
Much of the feedback from Latino/a/e families centered on the consolidation of Dual Language
Immersion (DLI) programs. Families expressed strong pride in Spanish DLI programs but worried that
relocating programs across town will isolate students or force parents to drop out of immersion
education. Working parents emphasized that long travel distances to reallocated programs would create
severe daily commute challenges for families without private transportation. Families noted that federal
immigration enforcement (ICE) activity in local neighborhoods has directly driven enrollment drops by
forcing immigrant families to relocate or stay home.

Additionally, Latino/a/e parents who attended this engagement session highlighted that many
Spanish-speaking families face significant language barriers, lack access to digital updates, or feel
uncomfortable attending large public meetings. They expressed frustration about the way they were
engaged broadly.

Summary and Conclusions
Community feedback shared in engagement sessions shows that families understand the district's
financial situation, but they want decisions that protect neighborhood schools and keep kids safe. Across
town halls and regional meetings, parents agree that school changes should not create dangerous
commutes, hurt special education services, or weaken dual language programs. People also expect
district leaders to cut central office spending and administrative salaries before closing any
neighborhood classrooms.

Conversations with Native, Black, and Latino/a/e families show that equity requires action. This includes
protecting Black and Native cultural hubs, improving engagement outreach for non-English speakers, and
working with labor partners to protect teachers of color from seniority layoffs. Black leaders also called
out residents who misuse terms like redlining and busing to protect their own school boundaries.

Section 2: Guiding Principles: Alignment and Trade-Offs
In May of 2026, the Portland Public Schools Board of Education adopted a resolution that outlined the
