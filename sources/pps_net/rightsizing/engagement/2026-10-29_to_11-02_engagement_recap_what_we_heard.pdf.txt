                                    October 29 - November 2, 2026

                                        Executive Summary
This memo is a summary of public feedback on the proposed rightsizing scenarios. It covers community
input gathered between October 29th and October November 2nd, 2026.

Between October 29th and October November 2nd, PPS hosted the following engagement sessions:
   ●​ Educator Town Hall (October 29th)

As of November 2nd, we have received 3,408 responses from parents/guardians, PPS staff, students, and
community members representing all of our school communities. About 93% of respondents
represented school communities affected by the rightsizing scenario proposals in some way, including
school closure, school consolidation, program changes, or boundary changes.

In this What We Heard memo, we present survey data collected to date, a summary of the educator
town hall meeting, and a deep dive into feedback about transitional supports discussed by community
members.

Introduction
Every Monday, from when the rightsizing plans were released on October 5th until Superintendent
Armstrong gives her recommendation to the board, the Data and Accountability team will gather and
summarize public feedback. The purpose of these reports is to be open and clear. We want to share
what community members have told us and explain how we will use that information. This memo shares
what we have heard from our community between October 28 - November 2, 2026.

Scenario Feedback Survey Findings to Date
As of November 2nd, we have received 3,408 responses from parents/guardians, PPS staff, students, and
community members representing all of our school communities! Responses have been collected in
English, Simplified Chinese, Spanish, Vietnamese, and Russian. The survey is available in Somali, but no
Somali-language responses have been collected. About 93% of respondents represented school
communities affected by the rightsizing scenario proposals in some way, including school closure, school
consolidation, program changes, or boundary changes. See Tables 1-3 for a breakdown of respondent

Open-Ended Response Analysis

The survey asked the following open-ended questions:
    ●​ What specific support (e.g., transportation to school, students staying with their classmates)
        would you need if school changes occur all at once instead of over several years?
    ●​ What specific resources or help should the district provide to support schools affected by
        rightsizing?
    ●​ Our goal is to create a model that is fair and equitable to all communities. Did we miss any
        important equity issues, community history, or student needs in creating these models? If yes,
        please tell us what we missed and how we can address it.
    ●​ What is one thing you would like us to know about your school community?

Below, we present emerging findings from all open-ended questions centering one key theme: supports
that students and staff affected by rightsizing will need as part of their transition. These themes are first
presented broadly, and then they are disaggregated by stakeholder groups.

District-Wide Support Requests
Over half of all respondents (52.7%) state that transportation support is the most important. Families
who are currently walking or biking to their neighborhood school expressed concern over walkable
routes and facing longer commutes across busy streets. Parents shared that district-provided yellow
busing, expanded crossing guard coverage, safe street crossing infrastructure, and coordinated arrival
times are necessary to prevent severe travel burdens, particularly for lower-income households without
private vehicles.

The second transitional support families discussed was social. Nearly one-third of respondents (32.1%)
highlight student cohort preservation as a critical support. Parents emphasize that keeping grade-level
cohorts, friend groups, and sibling sets together is essential for their students’ social and emotional
stability during building consolidations. Families also expressed concern that dividing established student
bodies across multiple receiving sites could create social isolation and anxiety. Respondents asked the
district to commit to intact cohort reassignments rather than splitting neighborhood cohorts across
different attendance boundaries.

Similarly, over 17% of respondents focus on retaining familiar teachers, administrators, and support staff.
Community members shared that familiar adults serve as stabilizing anchors for students undergoing
building transitions. Parents ask the district to reassign teaching teams alongside student cohorts to
receiving sites, ensuring that established relationships, school culture, and classroom routines continue
in new facilities.

Finally, respondents called for structured transition protocols, mental health resources, and preserved
childcare infrastructure. Families ask for spring orientation visits, joint field trips with receiving schools,
and shadow days to help students adjust to new environments before fall. Parents also emphasize the
need for guaranteed enrollment in SUN programs and fee-based aftercare, noting that losing local
after-school care would make attending a reassigned school unmanageable for working parents.
Next, we further disaggregate the support requested by stakeholder groups and student services.

Students Receiving Special Education Services
Families of Special Education students emphasize that transition supports must focus on service
continuity, IEP fidelity, and physical facility readiness. Parents of students with 504 plans or IEPs
                                                                                                            6
expressed concerns that moving to larger receiving schools will disrupt established accommodations,
sensory routines, and specialized behavioral supports. Respondents call for transition plans tailored to
individual student needs, including site visits, dedicated quiet sensory spaces in school buildings, and
physical accessibility retrofits like working elevators and secure perimeters.

Parents also highlight the necessity of retaining familiar support staff, such as paraprofessionals,
speech-language pathologists, and special education teachers, who understand their children's specific
learning profiles. A major focus among Southeast Portland families involves preserving specialized
programs, such as the Deaf and Hard of Hearing (D/HH) program. Parents emphasize that keeping
hearing and D/HH peers together alongside trained staff is essential to maintaining an inclusive learning
environment.

Multilingual Learner Services (MLL)
Multilingual families emphasize the need for clear communication, direct outreach, and accessible
transportation options. Parents report that navigating school changes requires district updates translated
accurately into home languages, as well as in-person informational sessions rather than digital-only
communication. Families also raise concerns about long commutes, noting that public transit transfers
create significant barriers for working parents who lack their own transportation.

MLL families express concern over losing essential community wraps and non-academic support
embedded in current neighborhood schools. Respondents point to services such as on-site food pantries,
clothing centers, and family resource coordinators, such as those at Woodmere Elementary. Parents
state that receiving schools must have equivalent community partner infrastructure and dedicated
bilingual staff to ensure multilingual students maintain academic and social support.

Dual Language Immersion (DLI)
DLI families focus heavily on commute logistics, program access, and student safety. Parents note that
relocating immersion programs across town forces families to choose between continuing dual-language
education and managing long travel times. Respondents state that district-provided transportation is
necessary for DLI families, particularly for those moving across regional corridors. Families also highlight
safety concerns regarding federal immigration enforcement (ICE), asking for explicit district safety
protocols and direct busing to protect families during daily travel.

DLI parents stress the importance of preserving heritage-language seats, keeping sibling groups in the
same building, and maintaining program stability. Families express concern that consolidating DLI into
single-site hubs will isolate immersion students from neighborhood peers or split multi-child households
between general education and immersion campuses. Respondents ask the district to guarantee stable
DLI pathways from elementary through high school without frequent boundary shifts.

Educators and Other Staff
Educators and school staff identified job security, clear contractual procedures, and adequate
preparation time as their primary support needs. Staff expressed frustration over unclear reassignment
rules, urging the district to provide transparent staffing models well ahead of implementation. Some
families and staff emphasized that labor partner seniority rules during mergers must be managed
carefully to protect newer teachers of color and bilingual educators, who provide critical mentorship to
historically underserved students.

Staff also raised operational concerns regarding facility readiness and the logistics of moving schools.
Educators point out that packing up classrooms, transferring specialized instructional materials, and
setting up new teaching spaces requires dedicated paid contract days and professional moving services
rather than uncompensated teacher labor. Furthermore, staff emphasize that receiving buildings must
have functional technology, adequate room layouts, and enforced class size limits before welcoming
merged student bodies.

                               Appendix B: Educator Town Hall Speak Back
                                            October 29, 2026

Location: Cleveland High School at Marshall Campus
Number of Attendees: 61

                                               What We Heard
A major focus of the Educator Town Hall was physical building limitations, classroom audits, and the
costs of moving entire schools. Educators shared that capacity numbers do not reflect daily operational
realities. For instance, teachers noted that receiving middle schools with a single gymnasium cannot
handle doubled student populations during winter weather. Staff also pointed to the need for fenced
outdoor play areas for Pre-K students, de-escalation spaces for special education, and the unbudgeted
time required to pack and move classrooms. District administrators responded that operations teams are
conducting site walks and "test fits" to ensure facility improvements happen before moves, but teachers
remained skeptical that older receiving buildings can absorb higher student density.

Staff also raised concerns about closing specialized school models and losing the teacher-student
relationships that keep students in school. Educators defended alternative programs like Sunnyside
Environmental School (SES) and the Metropolitan Learning Center (MLC), noting that these programs
serve large populations of neurodivergent and special education students who struggle in traditional
settings. Student services staff and teachers warned that moving students from small, predictable
environments into large middle and high schools removes the specific mentors and arts programs that
prevent truancy and dropouts. Educators urged the district to identify and expand successful practices
across the system rather than dismantling working models.

Educators and special education staff voiced frustration over the lack of explicit transition planning for
high-needs students. Teachers noted that initial scenario proposals lacked details on Individualized
Education Program (IEP) pullout spaces, physical accommodations, or staffing ratios. Staff also
highlighted that centralizing Dual Language Immersion (DLI) programs increases commute distances and
heightens exposure to federal immigration enforcement (ICE) for families.

Finally, educators expressed concern over the fast pace of the rightsizing timeline. Teachers and labor
partners asked district leaders to advocate for state education funding rather than rushing a local rollout.
Staff voiced distrust in the central office’s administrative capacity to execute a multi-school move,
pointing to payroll errors, furloughs, and bumpy facility transitions like Cleveland High School's move to
Marshall.
