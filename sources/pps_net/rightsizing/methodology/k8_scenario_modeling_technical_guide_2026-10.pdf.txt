2. What the scenario numbers are for
The scenario numbers compare proposals on a common basis: which schools reach their enrollment floors
in 2031-2032, which exceed their capacity in any year, how many students move and to where, and how
student groups and walking distances change.
Scenario numbers are not the enrollment forecast that staffing rests on. After the Board adopts boundaries,
grade configurations and program locations, PPS will give PRC the October 2026 enrollment together
with the adopted changes. PRC, using its own methods, will produce its own forecast, and the PRC result
becomes the one-year forecast for staffing in 2027-2028.


3. Names and conventions
  •​    Scenarios. The two scenarios compared throughout are Scenario A and Scenario B. Status Quo is
        PRC’s forecast with no scenario applied.
  •​    Years. A year written alone is the Fall of the school year. 2027 is the 2027-2028 school year. The
        first year of change in Scenarios A and B is 2027-2028.
  •​    Student groups. Students are grouped as PRC forecasts them: residents attending the
        neighborhood program, non-residents attending it (transfer and lottery students), each language
        immersion program, and special programs. Part 2, Section 2.1 defines them.
  •​    The 2027 boundaries. Every scenario is drawn against the 2027 boundary set: today’s attendance
        areas with the one change already adopted for 2027-2028, the end of a dual assignment at one
        high school.

1. Purpose and Scope
Each year, the Population Research Center (PRC) at Portland State University provides Portland Public
Schools (PPS) with independent, ten-year enrollment forecasts for every school, program, and grade in
the district. PPS's planning process then asks a further question: what would enrollment look like under a
proposed change — a school consolidation, a boundary adjustment, or a program merger?
To answer that question consistently and transparently, PPS built a modeling tool that takes a proposed
change (a "scenario") and mathematically combines it with PRC's forecast to produce a revised,
school-by-school and grade-by-grade projection. This part explains, in plain terms, exactly how that
combination is done — the rules the tool follows, and the mathematics behind them — so that PRC could
review and validate the approach, which it has done.
This part is limited to that combination step. It does not describe how PRC develops its underlying
forecasts — PRC's own methodology governs that — and it does not describe how PPS staff decide which
scenarios to propose in the first place, which is a separate planning and community engagement process.

In Brief: The Method in Eight Steps
   •​    Start from PRC's forecast exactly as provided. This baseline is never altered.
   •​    If a scenario closes a school outright, first adjust the historical residency table so that the
         closing school’s attendance boundary is carried forward to the school or schools that absorb
         it.
   •​    Once a proposed change takes effect in a given year, assume it continues in every later year
         of the ten-year forecast, unless a further change supersedes it.


                  K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 5
  •​     Decide which students are affected: either everyone in the specified grades right away, or a
         gradual, grade-by-grade phase-in that protects students who are already enrolled.
  •​     Determine how many students each rule moves. A percentage below 100 moves that share
         of PRC's original projection; a percentage of exactly 100 moves everyone present, including
         students an earlier rule has just moved in.
  •​     Move the affected students: either directly to one receiving school, or — when a school or
         program closes outright — split proportionally across the surrounding attendance
         boundaries, based on where those students have historically lived.
  •​     Convert every fractional count leaving a given school, program, grade and year into whole
         numbers together, in a single step, so that rounding never inflates or reduces the
         district-wide total.
  •​     Record the result as additions and subtractions layered on top of PRC's untouched baseline,
         then verify the finished scenario automatically before any figure is published.
The rest of this part walks through each of these steps in detail.

2. The Three Inputs to the Model
2.1 PRC's Enrollment Forecast (the Baseline)
For every school, enrollment category, grade, and year, PRC supplies a single projected headcount. This
complete set of numbers is the starting point — the "baseline" or "status quo" — against which every
scenario is compared, and it is never edited or overwritten anywhere in the process.
PRC organizes its forecast into a small number of enrollment categories that distinguish why a student is
enrolled where they are:
  •​     Residents Attending Neighborhood Program — students who live within a school's
         official attendance boundary and attend that school's standard program. This is the core
         population most directly driven by neighborhood demographic change, and generally the
         largest share of the district’s enrollment.
  •​     Non-residents Attending Neighborhood Program — students attending a standard
         program outside their own attendance boundary, typically because of a grandfathered
         boundary change, an approved hardship transfer, admission by lottery to a focus option
         housed at a neighborhood school, or a family that moved but chose to let a student finish out
         their remaining grades at their original school.
  •​     Special Programs — district-managed programs that are not tied to a residential boundary,
         including alternative and charter-style programs.
  •​     Language immersion programs (for example, Spanish, Mandarin, Russian, Japanese, and
         Vietnamese Immersion) — these draw from two sources at once: neighborhood residents
         who choose the immersion track at their local school, and transfer students who travel from
         elsewhere in the district specifically for that language pathway.
PPS's modeling tool uses these same categories throughout, without reinterpreting them. A group of
students keeps its original category before and after a scenario is applied, unless a scenario specifically
calls for recategorizing them — which happens, for example, when a school closes and its "non-resident"
population is reassigned into the standard resident category at their new home school.

2.2 Scenario Definitions
PPS planning staff describe each proposed change — a consolidation, a boundary shift, a program merger
— in a simple, structured way. For every rule within a scenario, they specify:
  •​    The originating school, program category, and grade range the change applies to;
  •​    The receiving school and program category — or, for changes that dissolve a program or
        close a school outright, an instruction to return the affected students to their home
        attendance boundaries rather than to one specific receiving school. This option is used for
        students whose enrollment is not tied to where they live — non-resident, language
        immersion and special-program students — for the share assumed not to follow when their
        school or program closes or moves, or when a school stops enrolling non-residents. Students
        who live within a closing school’s own boundary are instead sent to the named receiving
        school or schools, as in the worked example in Section 3.9;
  •​    The year the change takes effect;
  •​    What share of the projected group is affected, from a small fraction up to the entire group;
  •​    Whether the change should apply to everyone in the affected grades immediately, or phase
        in gradually (Section 3.4 explains the difference); and
  •​    A short written note describing what the rule is part of, in the planner’s own words. This
        note is carried through the calculation and appears alongside every figure the rule produces,
        so that a reader of the detailed output can see the planning intent behind a number without
        consulting a separate document.
A single scenario is typically made up of many such rules — one for each school, program, and grade
range it touches — which the modeling tool applies together, as described below. The percentage on each
rule is always applied to a specific number. A percentage below 100 is applied to the PRC’s original
projection for the group; a percentage of exactly 100 percent instead moves every student in the group,
including any moved there by rules the tool applies before it. Section 3.5 explains this distinction and the
order in which the rules are applied.

2.3 Historical Residency Proportions
When a scenario calls for returning students to their home attendance boundaries rather than sending them
to one specific receiving school — for example, non-resident, immersion or special-program students
whose school or program closes (Section 2.2) — the tool needs to know how a program’s enrolled
population has actually been distributed across the surrounding boundary areas. PPS maintains this as a
supporting table: for a given school, program, and grade band, the share of enrolled students who have, on
average over the three most recent school years, lived in each nearby attendance boundary.
For any given school, program, and grade band, these shares always add up to 100 percent across every
boundary area that program has historically drawn from. Some of that draw is legitimately from outside
the district; where that is the case it is carried as such, and students apportioned there are understood to be
leaving PPS rather than arriving at another PPS school.
This table describes history, and history does not know which schools a scenario is about to close. Section
3.3 describes the adjustment the tool makes before each scenario runs so that a closing school’s boundary

described in Section 3.6, and has no effect on scenarios that specify a direct, one-to-one destination
school.


3. How a Scenario Is Applied, Step by Step
3.1 Establishing the Baseline
Before any scenario is considered, the tool takes PRC's complete forecast and sets it aside, untouched, as
the reference point every scenario will be compared against. Every subsequent calculation adds to or
subtracts from this reference — it never changes the reference numbers themselves. PRC's original
forecast, unaltered, therefore remains available for inspection at every stage, alongside however many
alternative scenarios PPS chooses to evaluate.

3.2 A Change, Once Made, Carries Forward
A scenario rule takes effect starting in a specified year and, once in effect, is assumed to remain in place
for every later year of the ten-year forecast, unless a separate rule specifically supersedes it. In other
words, a scenario describes a new steady state going forward, not a one-time event confined to a single
year: a boundary change made effective in the fourth year of the model is assumed to still be in place in
the tenth year, consistent with how these decisions function in practice. This lets PPS see a full ten-year
trajectory for every school under a proposed change, not just a single snapshot year.

3.3 Preparing the Residency Table When a School Closes
The historical residency table described in Section 2.3 records where a program’s students have lived. If a
scenario closes a school, that school’s attendance boundary still appears in the table as a place students
live — and without an adjustment, a redistribution elsewhere in the district could assign students to a
school the same scenario has just closed.
The tool therefore prepares a corrected copy of the residency table before each scenario runs. The master
table is never edited; each scenario works from its own corrected copy.
Identifying a closure. A school is treated as closed in a given year when every group and grade it is
projected to hold that year is fully reassigned elsewhere. A single program leaving is not a closure: a
scenario can move an immersion program from School A to School B while School A continues to
operate its neighborhood program, and School A is correctly not treated as closed.
Identifying the successors. A closing school’s attendance boundary does not disappear — the families in
it are absorbed by whichever school or schools the scenario reassigns its resident students to. The tool
reads those destinations, and their relative shares, from the scenario’s own rules, so the boundary follows
the students rather than being assigned by a separate assumption.
Carrying the boundary forward. Every reference to the closing school as a place students live is
replaced by one entry per successor, each carrying the original share multiplied by that successor’s share
of the reassignment. Suppose School A closes, with its resident students divided evenly between School B
and School C, and suppose School A’s boundary historically accounted for 8 percent of the students in a
program at School D. In the corrected table that 8 percent is carried as 4 percent living in School B’s


                K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 9
boundary and 4 percent in School C’s. The families have not moved; the boundary they live in now
belongs to two different schools, and the total is unchanged.
Where a successor is itself closed by the same scenario, the tool follows the chain through to the schools
that remain open, composing the shares along the way. Where the successors differ by grade — for
example, elementary grades to one school and middle grades to another — the boundary is carried
forward separately for each grade range. Where a scenario closes a program that has no residential
boundary at all, there is nothing to carry forward and nothing is changed.
Every reassignment the tool makes is written to a "Closed School Successors" report alongside the results,
naming each closed school, the year it closes, each successor, and the share carried to it.

3.4 Determining Which Students Are Affected: Immediate vs. Phased-In Change
Not every change is meant to apply to every enrolled student the moment it takes effect. PPS planning
staff mark each rule as either immediate, or — by leaving this unspecified, which is the current default —
a gradual, phased-in change.
Immediate: every student in the specified grade range at the originating school and program is affected,
starting in the effective year and in every year after.
Phased (default): the change protects students already enrolled above the starting grade, applying instead
only to the incoming class at the youngest specified grade, and to every class that enters afterward. As
those students move up a grade each year, the change follows them — the same idea behind standard
cohort-survival forecasting, where a group of students is tracked not by name but by the size of the class
that entered together and ages up as a group. Practically, this means a change phases in one grade at a
time: in the first year, only the newly entering class at the starting grade is affected; the next year, that
same class — now one grade higher — is still affected, and a new entering class is added as well; and so
on, until the entire specified grade range is fully phased in and remains so for every year after.

3.5 What a Percentage Means: Share Rules and Sweep Rules
A scenario rule specifies what share of a group is affected. Because a single school, program and grade
can be touched by several rules at once — some students moving to one receiving school, some to
another, some returning to their home boundaries — the tool must be precise about what each percentage
is measured against. It distinguishes two cases.
A share rule is any rule below 100 percent. It moves that share of PRC’s original projection for the
school, program, grade and year — never a share of whatever an earlier rule happened to leave behind.
Every share rule drawing on the same group is therefore measured against the same number, so
complementary shares add up exactly as written.
This is what allows a scenario to divide a group cleanly. If a group of 100 projected students is split by
three rules — 50 percent returning to home boundaries, 25 percent to School B, and 25 percent to School
C — all three are measured against the same 100, they remove 50, 25 and 25, and the originating group
empties completely. Had each rule instead been applied to the students remaining after the previous one,
the same three rules would remove 50, then 12.5, then about 9.4, leaving more than a quarter of the
students enrolled at a school the scenario closes.
A sweep rule is a rule of exactly 100 percent. It moves every student present in that group at that point,
which includes any students an earlier rule in the same scenario has just moved in. This is deliberate: it is
what allows a closing school to land on exactly zero even when the scenario routes students through it. A
school projected to have 31 students that also receives 1 student from another rule will show a sweep of
32. Rules below 100 percent are applied first, then 100 percent rules that return students to their home
boundaries, then 100 percent rules to named schools; each moves the students present after the stages
before it.
The distinction concerns the denominator, not the destination. Both kinds of rule may send students
directly to a receiving school or return them to their home boundaries, as described in Section 3.6.
In practice this means a closure should be written either as a single 100 percent rule, or as a set of share
rules that add to 100 percent. One case deserves note: if a school’s closure is written entirely as share
rules totaling 100 percent, and that same school also receives students from another rule in the same
scenario, the share rules will not carry those arrivals away, because they are measured against PRC’s
projection rather than against the arrivals. For example, if School B is projected to have 40 students in a
grade and its closure is written as 60 percent to School C and 40 percent to School D, those two rules
move exactly 40 students. If another rule in the same scenario also sends 2 students from School A to
School B in that grade, those students are left behind at a school that has closed.
This case is narrow. It arises only when a scenario sends students into a school that the same scenario also
closes. One way students could arrive at a closing school — a closed program returning students who live
in its boundary — cannot cause it, because the adjustment in Section 3.3 sends those students to the
schools that absorb the boundary instead. What remains is a scenario in which one school’s students are
sent to a second school that itself is closing. The reliable way to avoid it is send the first school’s students
directly to the final destinations. Closing the second school with a single 100 percent rule also works
when the students arrive by a rule below 100 percent. In the scenarios PPS has evaluated to date, the
verification step has not found this case.



                K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 11
If it did occur, it does not pass unnoticed. The verification step described in Section 5 reports the students
left behind as an error, naming the school, program, grade and year, and they are resolved as described
there; the scenario’s rules are corrected and the scenario is run again, rather than the students being moved
by hand.

3.6 Two Kinds of Destinations: Direct Transfers and Boundary-Based Redistribution
Once the tool has determined how many students a rule moves, it sends them in one of two ways,
depending on how the rule is written.
Direct transfers are the more common case: the affected students in a specific school, program, and
grade range are reassigned to one specific receiving school and program. If a scenario specifies moving
100 percent of Kindergarten through Grade 5 from School A to School B starting in a given year, then
every projected student in that group — at School A that year, and in every following year’s incoming
Kindergarten class — is reassigned to School B for as long as the rule remains in effect.
Boundary-based redistribution applies when a scenario instead dissolves a program or closes a school
and returns its students to their home attendance boundaries, rather than sending them to one specific
receiving school. Because those students could live across several different surrounding boundary areas,
the tool cannot send them to just one place. Instead, it uses the historical residency proportions described
in Section 2.3 — as corrected for any school the scenario closes, per Section 3.3 — to split the departing
group across every boundary area it has historically drawn from, in the same shares history shows. For
example, if a program with a historical draw of 50 percent from Boundary Area 1, 31 percent from
Boundary Area 2, and 19 percent from Boundary Area 3 is discontinued, a departing group of students
from that program is split across the three areas in those same proportions, each added into that area’s
standard neighborhood program count.
In both cases, the number of students affected and the year the change takes effect come from the rule
itself; only the destination logic differs.
Grade-level detail within a redistribution
The residency shares described in Section 2.3 are quoted for a grade band — typically elementary and
middle grades — rather than for a single grade. The same share therefore drives every grade in the band,
and the model has no grade-level signal within it. This has one consequence worth stating plainly and one
worth guarding against.
The consequence worth stating is that the grade a returning student is assigned to should not be read as a
grade-level forecast. The model knows how many students each boundary area should receive across the
band, but not which grades they are in. For example, suppose a closing program sends 25 students home
from each grade, Kindergarten through Grade 5, and one boundary area has historically supplied 2 percent
of the program’s students. That area’s exact share is half a student in every grade, or 3 students across the
band. Because the model reports whole students, the area’s students appear in some grades and not others.
The band total is a meaningful projection; which grades show a student is not, because every grade has
the same claim of half a student and the data gives no reason to prefer one grade over another. Part 3
walks through a real case of this kind.
The same caution applies when comparing scenarios. Two scenarios that make the same change can place
a returning student in different grades, because each grade is settled together with every other boundary

               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 12
area in the band, and a closure elsewhere in one scenario changes those other shares. Compare scenarios
on band totals for returning students, not grade by grade.
Computing the shares grade by grade instead would not improve this. A program of roughly 25 students
per grade drawn from 40 or more boundary areas produces per-grade, per-boundary counts that are almost
entirely zeros and ones, and a three-year average of those figures is noise rather than signal. For example,
if one boundary area happened to send the program a first grader in each of the last three years but no
second graders, grade-level shares would credit it with 4 percent of first grade and none of second grade
— a difference created by which families happened to enroll in those particular years, not a pattern there
is any reason to expect to continue. The band-level average is the appropriate granularity for the data
available.
The risk worth guarding against is subtler. Because the same share applies to every grade in the band,
settling each grade’s split independently would repeat one near-identical rounding decision once per
grade, always in the same direction. A boundary whose exact claim is slightly more than half a student in
each grade would collect a whole student in every grade of the band; one whose claim is slightly less
would collect none in any of them. Over a six-grade band that turns an exact claim of, for example, 3.6
students into 6, or 2.4 into none — in the same direction in every forecast year, since the shares do not
change. The tool avoids this by settling the band as a whole, as described in Section 3.7, which works
through this example.

3.7 Keeping Every Student Whole: The Rounding Problem and How It's Solved
Applying a percentage to a whole-number forecast will usually produce a fraction. Thirty percent of
seventeen students is 5.1 — and while a forecast can describe a fraction of a student on paper, a modeled
outcome cannot. This is not a new problem; it is a version of a well-known issue in applied mathematics,
the same one that arises whenever a fixed total has to be divided among several groups in proportion to
unequal shares, such as apportioning a fixed number of legislative seats among states of different
populations.
Rounding each share independently — the obvious first approach — can quietly gain or lose whole
students. If ten departing students are split three ways in roughly equal shares (3.33, 3.33 and 3.33),
rounding each independently gives 3, 3 and 3 — only nine students, with a tenth vanishing from the
model even though ten clearly left the originating school.
Two decisions follow from this: which numbers must be rounded together, and how the rounding itself is
done.
One rounding decision per cell
All students leaving one school, program, grade and year are converted to whole numbers together, in a
single step — regardless of how many rules are moving them or whether those rules send students to
different receiving schools or back to home boundaries. The cell, not the individual rule, is the unit at
which rounding is settled.
Settling each rule separately would break the total. Suppose 27 students in one grade are divided evenly
between two receiving schools. Each rule’s exact share is 13.5. Rounded independently, both round the
same way, and the two together remove either 26 or 28 students from a group of 27 — leaving a student



               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 13
stranded at a school the scenario has closed, or removing one who was never there. Settled together, the
pair is required to total exactly 27, and one school receives 14 while the other receives 13.
The same principle governs a boundary-based redistribution, one grade band at a time. Because that step
builds on the rounding method described next, it is explained with a worked example, under “Setting a
grade band as a whole” at the end of this section.
How the whole numbers are chosen
The tool uses the Largest Remainder Method, also known as Hamilton’s method in the apportionment
literature. Each site first receives the whole-number part of its exact share. That will normally leave a
small number of whole students still to be assigned, and those go, one each, to the sites whose fractional
remainders are largest.
Stated formally: for a group of n sites sharing a departing or arriving student count, let vᵢ be the exact
fractional share calculated for site i. Write vᵢ as a whole-number part aᵢ plus a remainder rᵢ between 0 and
1, and let k be the number of whole students left over once every site has received its aᵢ:

k = round(v₁ + v₂ + ... + v ) − (a₁ + a₂ + ... + a )

Each site receives aᵢ. The k sites with the largest remainders each receive one additional student.


This is not an approximation. Ranking the remainders from largest to smallest as r₍₁₎ through r₍ ₎, the
largest gap between any site’s exact share and the whole number it receives is max(1 − r₍ₖ₎, r₍ₖ₊₁₎), and
no other assignment can do better: any alternative must round up a site with a remainder no larger than
r₍ₖ₎, producing a gap of at least 1 − r₍ₖ₎, and must round down a site with a remainder no smaller than
r₍ₖ₊₁₎, producing a gap of at least r₍ₖ₊₁₎. The same assignment also rounds up the largest total remainder
available, so it minimizes the sum of all the gaps as well as the largest one.
This reaches the same optimum described by the constrained-optimization formulation in the earlier draft
of this methodology — the smallest possible largest gap, subject to the whole numbers summing to the
correct total. It reaches it directly rather than by search, which means the tool no longer depends on a
third-party optimization solver, runs substantially faster, and produces identical results on any computer
and in any future run.
When two sites tie
Two sites can have identical remainders at the point where the leftover students run out. The clearest case
is an odd number of students split evenly two ways: each side’s exact share ends in one half, one side
must receive the extra student, and neither has a better claim to it. Every such combination scores
identically on both measures above, so the choice is genuinely free — but it should not always fall the
same way.
The tool settles these ties by a fixed rule that depends on the cell being calculated and on the names of the
sites involved. Two consequences follow. Within a grade range, the extra student alternates between the
receiving schools rather than accumulating at one of them: under the previous approach, a school could
receive the odd student in every grade of a K–5 span, ending several students ahead purely as a
by-product of how ties were resolved. And because the rule depends only on the inputs, it produces the
same answer on every computer and in every rerun.


                  K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 14
The tie-breaking rule is governed by a published setting recorded with the model. Re-running a scenario
under a different setting produces a different, equally valid resolution of the same ties, which allows PPS
to confirm directly that no conclusion depends on how ties happened to fall. In testing to date, changing
this setting has moved no school by more than three students in any year and has never changed a school’s
closure, a district total, or a utilization conclusion.
Direct transfers need no second rounding
For a direct transfer with a single receiving school, no second rounding decision is needed at all: whatever
whole number of students is confirmed as leaving the originating school is credited to the receiving
school as that same exact number. Direct transfers are therefore conserved exactly, down to the student.
The Largest Remainder Method is applied at two points only: when settling the departures from a cell,
and when splitting a confirmed whole-number departure across the boundary areas it returns to.
Settling a grade band as a whole
When students return to their home boundaries, every grade in a band is split using the same residency
shares, so settling each grade on its own would repeat the same rounding decision in every grade, always
in the same direction (Section 3.6). The tool therefore settles the band as a whole. It still works through
the band one grade at a time, and every grade still places exactly the students who left it. What changes is
the amount each boundary area is treated as entitled to in that grade: not its share of that one grade alone,
but what it is still owed across the band — its share of every student who has left the band so far, less the
students it has already received. Within each grade, the whole numbers are then chosen by the Largest
Remainder Method, exactly as described under “How the whole numbers are chosen” above.
An example makes this concrete. Suppose a program closes and sends 2 students home from each grade,
Kindergarten through Grade 5, and that its students have historically lived in three boundary areas: 50
percent in Area 1, 30 percent in Area 2 and 20 percent in Area 3. In each grade, the exact shares are 1.0,
0.6 and 0.4 students; across the band, they are 6.0, 3.6 and 2.4. The two approaches compare as follows:

                                       Area 1              Area 2             Area 3

Exact share, Kindergarten–Grade        6                   3.6                2.4

second student goes to Area 2, whose remainder of 0.6 is larger than Area 3’s 0.4. Repeated in all six
grades, that gives Area 2 six students and Area 3 none, even though one in five of the program’s students
has historically lived in Area 3.
Settled as a band, Area 1 still receives one student in every grade, since it is owed exactly one each time.

Area 2 receives no one in Grade 1, and an area that is further ahead receives no one until its share catches
up. The adjustment only ever works forward; once a grade has been settled, it is not revisited.
In practice the tool does not always begin at Kindergarten. Each year it starts the band at a different grade,
chosen by a fixed published setting, so that the rounding does not fall in the same grade in every year of
the forecast; Part 3 explains why that matters for how the output reads. In testing against a full ten-year
scenario, settling the band as a whole reduced the average error in a receiving school’s band total from
about 1.4 students to about 0.2, and eliminated every case where a school’s band total was wrong by two
or more students.

3.8 Recording the Result: An Audit Ledger, Not an Overwrite
Rather than editing PRC's baseline numbers directly, the tool records every scenario's effect as a running
ledger of paired entries, much like an accounting ledger: a decrease at the originating school, program,
grade, and year, and a matching increase at the receiving school, program, grade, and year — always
adding to exactly the whole-number amount determined in Section 3.7. The baseline itself is never edited.
This has several practical benefits. PRC's original forecast remains fully intact and inspectable at every
stage, as the "Status Quo" reference point. Every change traces directly back to the specific scenario rule
that produced it. Multiple scenarios can be modeled side by side against the same untouched baseline
without interfering with one another or with PRC's source data. And a school's fully modeled enrollment,
for a given scenario, year, and grade, is simply its baseline count plus every ledger entry that applies to
that same cell under that scenario.




               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 16
Each ledger entry also records the reasoning behind its own figure: which rule produced it, what the rule’s
percentage was applied to, the exact fractional result before rounding, whether the figure was rounded up
or down and why, and the planner’s written note for the rule. Section 6 describes how to read this record.

3.9 Worked Example (Illustrative Only)
The example below is entirely generic and does not describe any real school, program, or projected count;
it is meant only to show how the pieces above work together.
Suppose School A is proposed for closure starting in a given year. Its resident students are divided evenly
between School B and School C. Its non-resident students — those attending School A from outside its
boundary — are treated differently: half are assumed to return to the standard program at their own home
school, and the remaining half follow the residents, one quarter to School B and one quarter to School C.
The change is marked as immediate.
Follow one grade of the non-resident group, for which PRC projects 18 students in the first effective year.
  •​    Preparing the residency table. Because School A closes entirely, its attendance boundary
        is carried forward before anything else happens (Section 3.3). School A’s residents split
        evenly between School B and School C, so every place the residency table records a student
        as living in School A’s boundary is replaced by two entries at half the share, one for School
        B and one for School C.
  •​    Determining the amounts. All three rules affecting this group are share rules, so all three
        are measured against PRC’s projection of 18 (Section 3.5). They remove 9.00, 4.50 and 4.50
        students respectively — exactly 18 in total, so the group empties completely.
  •​    Converting to whole students. The three fractional departures are settled together (Section
        3.7). They must total 18. The first is already whole at 9. The other two are tied at 4.50, and
        one of them must give up the extra student; the tie-breaking rule assigns it, and the
        departures are recorded as 9, 5 and 4. In the next grade up, where the projected count differs,
        the tie may well fall the other way.
  •​    Distributing the boundary return. The 9 students returning to their home boundaries are
        split using the corrected residency table (Section 3.6). If that table shows shares of 50, 31
        and 19 percent across three boundary areas, the exact shares are 4.50, 2.79 and 1.71. Each
        area first receives its whole-number part — 4, 2 and 1, totaling 7 — which leaves 2 students
        still to assign, and those go to the two largest remainders, 0.79 and 0.71. The tool therefore
        assigns 4, 3 and 2, totaling exactly the 9 who left. Note that the first area is not rounded up
        even though its exact share ends in one half: rounding each figure independently to the
        nearest whole number would give 5, 3 and 2 — ten students from a departure of nine.
        Settling the three together instead holds the total at nine and keeps the largest single gap to
        0.50 of a student, which is the smallest it can be.
  •​    Recording the result. The ledger records three decreases against School A for this group,
        grade and year — 9, 5 and 4 — and matching increases: 5 at School B, 4 at School C, and 4,
        3 and 2 at the three boundary areas’ standard neighborhood programs. PRC’s original
        forecast for School A is left completely untouched.

4. From Modeled Enrollment to Building Utilization
Once a scenario's fully modeled enrollment is assembled for every school, grade, and year, PPS compares
it against each building's functional capacity – the number of students a building can reasonably serve – to
calculate a utilization rate (modeled enrollment divided by functional capacity) and the number of seats
available or short at each site.. This comparison is produced twice for every school and year: once using
PRC's forecast before any scenario changes, and again using the fully modeled result after a scenario's
changes are applied, so that planners can see directly how a proposed change would affect each building's
utilization.


5. Built-In Verification and Reconciliation
Every scenario is checked automatically after it is calculated and before any figure is published. The
checks are deliberately mechanical: each one states a property the finished numbers must have if the
method has been applied correctly, and reports every instance where they do not.
  •​    Every school, program and grade that a scenario reassigns in full ends at exactly zero. A
        residual of even one student is reported as an error, identified down to the grade and year.
        The students are not moved by hand; instead, the scenario’s rules are corrected and the
        scenario is run again, so that every student who moves does so through a rule recorded in
        the ledger (Section 3.8), Section 3.5 describes one way a residual can arise and how it is
        avoided.
  •​    No school’s modeled enrollment is negative in any group, grade or year.
  •​    District-wide totals are unchanged year by year: the fully modeled result must reconcile
        exactly to PRC's baseline total, since a scenario moves students between schools but never
        creates or removes them. Students apportioned to an out-of-district boundary (Section 2.3)
        are carried on their own line, so this reconciliation still holds; the school-by-school tables
        leave that line out, so their sum is below PRC’s total by exactly those students.
  •​    No students are routed into a school the scenario closes, in the closure year or any year after.
  •​    No set of rules claims more than 100 percent of the same group, which would otherwise
        drive a school’s enrollment below zero.
  •​    Every receiving boundary is a school that exists in PRC’s forecast. Out-of-district
        destinations are recognized as legitimate and reported separately from genuine errors.
Findings are written to a "Guard Findings" report alongside the results, giving the scenario, school, group,
grade, year, the check that failed, and a plain description of the discrepancy. Findings are graded: an error
means the numbers should not be published as they stand, while a warning marks something that may be



               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 18
defensible but deserves a human decision. The tool can be set to halt the run outright if any error is
present, so that a scenario with a known defect cannot reach a published utilization table.
Conditions that could previously have passed unnoticed now stop and report. A redistribution with no
matching residency record, a boundary area left with no successors after a closure, and a group of students
that cannot be placed are each named explicitly rather than being silently dropped.
Alongside the findings, the tool publishes the "Closed School Successors" report described in Section 3.3,
so that every adjustment made to the residency table is visible next to the results it produced.

7. Summary
Throughout this process, PPS's modeling tool is built around five guarantees. PRC's original forecast is
preserved exactly and is never overwritten. Every scenario's effects are layered on top of that forecast as a
fully traceable set of additions and subtractions, each carrying the reasoning behind its own figure.
Percentage-based moves are measured against PRC's projection and converted to whole numbers a cell at
a time, so that complementary rules add up as written and rounding never gains or loses students in
aggregate. Changes are modeled in a way that reflects how they actually unfold in practice — carrying
forward year over year, protecting students already partway through an affected grade range by default,
and carrying a closing school's attendance boundary forward to the schools that absorb it. And every
finished scenario is verified automatically against a set of stated properties before any figure is published.

This methodology has been reviewed by the PRC. PPS welcomes questions about the approach described
above.

Part 3. Reading Grade-Level Detail in Redistribution Results
Why students returning to a neighborhood school do not appear to move up a grade
A companion to Part 2 · August 2026, revised October 2026

About this part: This is a supplement to Part 2, written because one feature of the model’s output
reliably surprises people who look at it closely — including the staff who built it. It concerns how to
read grade-by-grade figures when a school or program closes and its students return to their home
attendance boundaries. Nothing here describes a defect. It describes a limit on what the underlying
data can support, and the choice PPS made about how to present a result honestly within that limit.

A note on the figures: The numbers below are taken from an actual run of an earlier exploratory
scenario, not from the scenarios currently proposed; school names have been replaced with generic
labels so that this part can be shared independently of any particular proposal. "Program P" stands for
a special program proposed for closure; "School R" stands for one neighborhood school whose
attendance boundary supplied a small share of that program’s students.



1. The observation
When a special program closes and its students are returned to their home attendance boundaries, a reader
looking at a single receiving school’s results year by year may notice something odd. The students appear
in the same grade in every year of the ten-year forecast.
In the case that prompted this part, School R gained two students in second grade in the first year of the
closure — and two students in second grade in every year afterwards, for nine consecutive years, with
nothing in any other grade until late in the forecast.
That looks wrong in a specific way. If a second grader returns to School R this year, common sense says
they should be a third grader there next year. A stack of students sitting permanently in one grade looks
like the model has failed to age anyone.
It has not. The explanation involves three separate things that are easy to run together, so this part takes
them one at a time.




                  K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 23
2. The short answer
The model does not contain students. It contains counts.

Nothing inside the model has an identity that could get a year older, so nothing can move from one
grade to the next. Each year’s figure is computed from scratch.

Cohort progression has already happened before the model runs.

PRC’s forecast is built by tracking cohorts as they age. The model applies a percentage to numbers
that already carry that work.

The repeated grade was a rounding artifact, not a statement about students.

The model’s honest answer for School R is about half a student in every grade in every year. Whole
students have to be printed somewhere, and they were all landing in the same row.



3. What the model is made of
The model works on counts held in boxes. A box is one school, one enrollment category, one grade, one
year. PRC supplies a number for every box; a scenario adds to some boxes and subtracts from others.
When the tool "moves students," it subtracts a number from one box and adds the same number to another
box in the same grade and the same year. There is no student record, no roster, no identifier attached to
anyone. Nothing in the model is the kind of thing that could have a birthday.
So the question "why don’t these students move up a grade next year?" has an unexpected answer: there is
nothing there to move. The figure in School R’s third-grade box for next year was not produced by aging
this year’s second-grade figure. It was calculated independently, from PRC’s forecast for that school, that
grade, and that year.


4. Where cohort progression actually happens
Cohort progression is central to this work — it simply happens earlier in the chain than people expect.
PRC builds its forecast using cohort survival: it tracks a group of students as they move up one grade each
year and estimates how many remain. That work is finished before PPS receives the file. In the run behind
this part, PRC projects that Program P has 26 students in second grade in one year and 27 in third grade
the year after. Those are largely the same children, one year older. The progression is already inside those
two numbers.
The model then does something much simpler. School R’s attendance boundary historically supplied a
small, fixed share of Program P’s enrollment, so the tool multiplies that share by each year’s projected
count:

Year 1, grade 2: 26 students × School R’s share = 0.44 students returning to School R

Year 2, grade 3: 27 students × the same share = roughly the same figure again (the table in Section 5
has every grade and year)




                 K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 24
The progression lives in 26 becoming 27. That is PRC’s work, and the multiplication rides on top of it.
A useful way to picture it is a pipe with a small tap. PRC decides how much water flows through the pipe
each year at each grade — all the demographic and cohort reasoning happens there. The tap diverts a
fixed fraction into School R. The tap does not need to know anything about cohorts, because the pipe
already does. Building cohort logic into the tap as well would do the same work twice.


5. What the model actually knows about a receiving school

Real children do of course progress. The students School R gains in the first year are largely the same
people it has in the second year, one grade higher. But that continuity is invisible in the totals, because it
is exactly offset by the cohorts entering and leaving. What remains is a plateau.


6. Why whole numbers force an arbitrary choice
A forecast can describe 0.44 of a student. A modeled result cannot. Every figure PPS publishes has to be a
whole number of children.
School R’s exact claim comes to roughly 2.6 students a year across the six elementary grades. The model
must show a whole number, so it shows 2 in most years and 3 in others. That number then has to be

placed in one or two of the six grade rows — and the table in Section 5 gives no basis whatsoever for
choosing which. Every row has an equal claim of about half a student.
Whichever rows are chosen, the result is wrong by roughly the same amount in every box: the model
prints 1 where the truth is 0.44, and 0 where the truth is 0.44. This is unavoidable. It is not a shortcoming
of the method but a consequence of reporting a fractional expectation as whole children.
What is avoidable is the shape of that error.

9. How to read the output
Three practical points follow from all of this.
    •​   Trust the school and year totals. A receiving school’s total arrivals in a given year are
         accurate. Across the current scenario runs, a receiving boundary’s total from one closing
         program across the grade band lands within an average of 0.3 students of its exact share and
         never more than a student away; a boundary that receives no one had an exact share of less
         than one student.
    •​   Do not read grade-level redistribution figures as a grade-level forecast. Which grade a
         returning student is assigned to is decided by whole-number arithmetic, not by evidence.
         This applies only to students arriving through a boundary-based redistribution; every other
         figure in the model, including PRC’s baseline and all direct school-to-school transfers, is
         grade-specific in the ordinary sense.
    •​   Expect the grade to vary between years, and do not read meaning into the variation
         either. A school that gains two students may see them in different grades in successive



                K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 28
        years. That variation is not a projection of movement between grades. It is the visible form
        of a choice the data cannot make.
For anyone wanting to check a particular figure, the detailed output records the reasoning behind every
number — the rule that produced it, the historical share applied, the exact fractional result, and whether it
was rounded up or down. Part 2, Section 6 explains how to read it.


10. Why not simply use grade-level residency data?
The natural response to all of this is to ask for better data: if the residency shares were calculated for each
grade separately rather than for a grade band, the model would know which grades the students belong in.
It would not, and the attempt would make matters worse. A program of roughly 25 students per grade
draws from more than 40 boundary areas. Computing shares grade by grade means dividing 25 students
among 40 areas, which produces counts that are almost entirely zeros and ones. Averaging three years of
such figures does not produce a stable rate; it produces noise with the appearance of precision, and the
model would then distribute students according to which boundary happened to have a child in a
particular grade in a particular past year.
The grade band is the level at which this data is statistically meaningful. The honest course is to use it at
that level and to be clear, as this part is, about what it can and cannot support.


11. Summary
When a program closes and its students return to their neighborhood schools, the model applies a
historical residency rate to a forecast that has already accounted for cohort progression. It holds counts
rather than students, so nothing within it ages from one grade to the next; the aging has already been done
by PRC and is carried in the figures the model starts from.
For a school whose boundary supplied only a small share of a closing program, the resulting expectation
is a fraction of a student in each grade in each year. Reporting that as whole children requires choosing
grades for which there is no evidence. The tool varies that choice across the forecast rather than
concentrating it in one grade, so that an arbitrary decision looks arbitrary instead of looking like a finding.
Totals by school and year remain accurate and can be relied on. Grade-level detail arising from a
boundary redistribution should be read as an allocation, not a forecast.

How proposed attendance boundaries become the counts in the scenario rules
A companion to Part 2 · October 2026

 and 45 percent to another, say. This part explains where those percentages come from. It describes

 the demographic and walking-distance measures. It is written for readers who want to verify that the

 A note on names and figures: As in Part 2, the examples use generic names (School A, Area 1) and

1. The question this component answers
Two of the three kinds of values in a scenario come from enrollment history. PRC’s forecast, and the
residency table, are used when a closed school or program returns students to their home areas. The third
type of value comes from the map. When a scenario changes an attendance boundary, the share of a
school’s resident students who move with it is not a planning judgment. It is a count of the students who
live in the blocks being reassigned, under the current boundary and under the boundary being proposed,
divided by the students who live in the area today. Part 4 follows that count from the map to the rule table.


2. The data
2.1 Blocks
The unit of all boundary work is the 2010 census block. Every block in the district carries its census
identifier (GEOID10), the planning area it falls in, and a school program assignment for each grade from
kindergarten through 12th grade. A school’s boundary in this system is not a drawn line; it is the set of
blocks assigned to a school for a grade. Drawing a proposed boundary means reassigning blocks.
The block geography is old. The district’s boundaries were drawn against these blocks when they were
first created, and one boundary change since then, between Lincoln and Ida B. Wells-Barnett in 2016, cut
across a block. The work-around for that divided block is described under residency below. PPS states the
age of the geography plainly: While the block geography is dated, it is sufficient for modeling by school
and year which is the same way scenarios are being compared. The block is the unit in which boundaries
are drawn and counted. It does not describe individual homes or residential complexes.

2.2 Students
Each year’s roster (STU_Profiles_2913) holds one row per student per school year, going back to
2007-2008, and includes the geocoded home location (state plane, SRID 2913), the census block it falls in

student resides at each grade band. This is the chain that produces it:
  •​    Addresses come from the student information system, are processed into the enrollment profile

        can be geocoded.
  •​    Residency is derived from the geocoded address point, not from the school of enrollment. A point
        inside the district’s boundary polygons receives a resident school for each level; a point outside
        them is Out of District; a student with no point is Undetermined. The resident high school is taken
        from the school’s boundary polygon rather than from the census block, because one high school
        boundary divides a block.
  •​    A load procedure joins each point to its census block and rebuilds the spatial roster for every year.
        The roster also carries a second set of resident-school columns, ending in “_living_2027”,
        updated in a one-time update from two GIS polygon layers: the K–8 boundaries adopted for
        2023-2024, and the high school boundaries, which have not changed since 2021-2022. The one

        on them as well.
  •​    PRC_group, the enrollment category PRC forecasts (including: residents attending the
        neighborhood program, non-residents attending it, each immersion program, special programs), is
        a computed column in the roster, derived from the resident school and the school of enrollment.
        The groups the forecast is organized by, and the groups the GIS counts, are therefore one
        definition, not two.

4.2 Residents in a selection
For any list of blocks, a table-valued function (SDSS_Count_comparison_grade_group_csv) returns by
grade group and by the pair of comparison school and scenario school: the number of students living in
those blocks, the number of them attending their neighborhood program, and each as a proportion of the
comparison school’s total.

4.3 Residents by pair, across eleven years
The summary view (vw_SDSS_Assignments_AND_comparison_summary_2913) does the same for every pair
of comparison school and scenario school, with no selection, and for every school year from 2015-2016

pair covers, and the share of the comparison school’s residents they represent in that year. A three-year
aggregate (2022-2023 through 2024-2025) is carried alongside. The eleven-year series is the stability
check. A split that reads 55 percent today and ranged between 52 and 57 percent over the decade is a
different kind of number from one that swung between 35 and 70, and the series shows which kind a
proposal rests on.

4.4 From the count to the rule
The percentage on a resident rule in the scenario table is this share: the fraction of the comparison
school’s residents attending the neighborhood program who live in the blocks the scenario sends to the
receiving school, read from the current year and checked against the series. The percentages are the
output of an iteration between a planner and an analyst. The planner proposes a split, the analyst draws it
and counts, and either the line or the intent is revised until the count serves the area’s goals. They are not
starting assumptions.

grade, and that is the school a resident is shown at when a boundary changes. The procedure requires that
every scenario in the rule table have block assignments under exactly the same name in the GIS registry; a
scenario with none is reported as an error, not returned as a quiet result. Part 5 describes the placement
rules in full. Student data in the table can be aggregated then by demographics to describe the
compositions of schools under a scenario.

5.4 Walking distance
A table (SDSS_Walking_Distance_Time) holds the walking distance in feet and the time in minutes from
each census block to each school site: an origin–destination list generated in ArcGIS around 2016. The
one-mile threshold for K–5 and the one-and-a-half-mile threshold for 6–8 are the district’s transportation
thresholds, and two views (sdss_walking_K5_GT_1 and sdss_walking_68_GT_1_5) dissolve the blocks
beyond each threshold into one polygon per school for the map (Figure 2b).
The published measure comes from a second view
(sdss_student_data_w_scenario_assignments_chg_walking). It joins the student-level scenario
assignments of Section 5.3 to the distance table twice: once for the distance from the student’s block to
the school attended today, and once for the distance from the same block to the school the scenario
assigns. It covers students who attend their neighborhood program and have a block. For each receiving
school under each scenario, and for the district as a whole, it reports the number and the share of those
students more than a mile and more than a mile and a half from the assigned school. It also shows the
median, mean, and quartiles of the change in walking distance. A Status Quo row carries the same
measures against the school attended today, so every scenario is read against one baseline. Two
consequences follow. The measure counts residents attending the neighborhood program only, so a
scenario that moves only transfer or immersion students does not change it. And because it reads the
student-level assignments, it carries that model’s whole-group placement rules rather than the enrollment
model’s shares.

  •​   A block is the smallest unit. A boundary that divides a block cannot be represented at the block
       level. In the one instance where a block is divided, the polygon layer governs the student’s
       residency and the block layer governs the scenario, and the two are reconciled by hand.
  •​   Students without a usable point get no block and no scenario assignment. They are carried as
       “Undetermined”. They are few, and their number is checked at each load.
  •​   The two all-immersion elementary areas. Rigler and Lent are only Spanish Immersion schools,
       and their attendance areas work as dual-assignment areas. A family living in either is guaranteed
       an immersion place or otherwise attends a neighboring school’s English program, Scott’s for the
       Rigler area and Marysville’s for the Lent area. For residents and non-residents alike, PRC
       forecasts incoming students with its own method, and the forecast PPS receives is by school,
       enrollment group, grade, and year; it does not include a forecast of the students living in each
       attendance area. PRC counts the two areas as Scott’s and Marysville’s and forecasts Rigler and
       Lent as immersion only. The model reassigns each neighborhood school’s residents by the
       scenario’s boundaries and, in Scenarios A and B, moves immersion students to the immersion
       hubs. In the district’s GIS, however, the two areas are still drawn as Rigler’s and Lent’s own

       the published scenarios. The return-home step reads the residency table built on those layers, so a
       few returning students who live in the two areas are placed in a neighborhood program at Rigler
       or Lent that PRC does not forecast: 5 in 2027-2028 in each of Scenarios A and B, and no more
       than 13 in any year. And the student-level demographic and walking figures at Scott and
       Marysville treat those schools’ students from the two areas as living in Rigler’s or Lent’s area. A
       baseline that maps the two areas to Scott and Marysville has been built for the next iteration.

       same caution.
  •​   Very small reassignments are real. A handful of students per grade who live much closer to a

1. Purpose
Rightsizing scenarios are evaluated in two complementary ways. The PRC enrollment model forecasts
how many students would attend each school under each scenario. The model described here answers a
different question: which of today's students would be affected, and how. It takes every currently enrolled
student, determines where that student would be assigned under each scenario, and compares the result
with where they would be under the status quo. Because it works with real students rather than projected
counts, its results can be summarized by demographic group and by geography.
This model reports composition, not enrollment. Its counts are today's students redistributed under a
scenario. They are not a forecast, they do not include next year's kindergarteners or any in-migration, and
they will not match the PRC enrollment model. The two models are built for different questions and
should be read side by side, not reconciled.
The model produces two location assignments for every student and every scenario:
  •​     This year's assignment. Where the student would be assigned at their current grade if the
         scenario were fully implemented. This is the original demographic-impact output.
  •​     Next year's assignment (the roll-forward). Where the student would be one grade older,
         under the status quo and under the scenario. This captures the transitions that drive most of
         the real movement: 5th graders entering middle school, 8th graders entering high school,
         and immersion strands moving between buildings.

4. Modeling principles
A handful of rules govern every decision in the model. They are stated once here because the same
choices recur in both potential outcomes.

A student cannot be split
The enrollment model can send 60% of a program to one school and 40% to another. A student cannot be
divided, so where a workbook row moves part of a group, the whole group follows the plurality
destination. Ties are broken deterministically (by share, then by name) so repeated runs on the same



                 K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 44
inputs produce identical results. A partial reassignment, below the 99% threshold that defines a full move,
leaves students in place.

Two sources of truth
Residents of a neighborhood program follow their census block in the GIS layer, whatever the modeling
tool workbook says about that school; the workbook's share for residents is informational. Students in
immersion strands and special programs follow the modeling tool workbook, because the boundary layer
says nothing about programs. This split is what lets a scenario redraw a boundary and move a strand
independently.

First match wins, and every placement is explained
Rules run in a fixed order and each fills only students not yet placed. Every rule writes a note naming
itself, so any count in a dashboard can be traced back to the rule and the inputs that produced it. The
distribution of students across rules is recorded on every run and compared with the previous run, which
is the primary signal that a change to the workbook or the procedure has moved something.

The status quo and the scenario are built the same way
For next year, the status-quo columns and the scenario columns are produced by the same rules from the
same inputs, differing only in which boundary set and which program locations they read from. The
output is a matched pair, the status quo and the scenario, built to be compared. It is not a forecast of either.
For transfer students that the rules cannot see (Benson, da Vinci, special education placements, hardship
transfers, lottery outcomes), the model places them in the same place on both status quo and scenario and
contributes nothing to the difference, which is the intended treatment of anything unmodeled. The one
thing that would bias the comparison is a rule applied to the status quo or the scenario but not both so
every next-year rule is written as a pair.

6.1 One set of placement rules for both status quo and scenarios

  •​    Status quo: NY_Campus_Enrolled and NY_PRC_Group, derived from where the student is

                  K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 46
Every rule below has an "a" form that sets the status-quo columns from the enrolled campus and PRC
group, and a "b" form that sets the scenario columns from the scenario school and group, joined to the
scenario. Each side is first-match-wins on its own null test, so the two can diverge for the same student,
which is the point. A note on each side records the rule that placed the student.

6.2 Grade progression
Next year's grade is this year's grade plus one for every student in grades K-11. It does not depend on
which school the student attends, so it is set once before any placement rule runs.

6.3 Moving up in place
A student moves up a grade at the same school when next year's grade falls within the span of grades that
school serves for the student's group. The gradespan is the minimum to maximum grade enrolled (status
quo) or assigned under the scenario (scenario side), not "someone is enrolled one grade up", so a sparse
group with an empty grade in the middle still carries forward. The student's own grade is irrelevant: a 5th
grader at a K-8 stays, a 5th grader at a K-5 does not, and K-8 schools need no special treatment.
Three groups inherit a wider gradespan than their own enrollment would show:
  •​    Non-residents inherit the residents' gradespan at their school. Under board policy a
        non-resident may stay through the school's top grade unless the school closes; their own
        gradespan is narrow only because there are few of them.
  •​    Special programs inherit the school's full gradespan. A charter or focus option is its own
        campus, so nothing changes; a co-located program continues at its host through the host's
        top grade.
  •​    Immersion strands inherit the school's full gradespan, so a program still growing into its
        final grade continues to 12, such as McDaniel's Vietnamese strand, grades 9-11 today . To
        keep a K-5 strand at a K-8 building from being carried to 6th grade in place, a student
        whose school, group, and grade have an immersion feeder row (6.4) is excluded from this
        rule and leaves instead.
On the scenario side the gradespan is read from scenario assignments. So for a K-8 where the scenario
converts to K-5, the school has no one assigned above grade 5, and does not carry its 5th graders forward.
In a scenario where the K-5 converts to K-8, the 5th grader does carry forward.

6.4 Immersion feeder patterns
Immersion strands move between buildings in fixed patterns that the boundary layer does not describe. A
K-5 strand feeds one middle school and a middle school strand feeds one high school. These patterns are
held in a single table (Appendix C) and applied in five steps, each reached only if the step before did not
place the student:
  1.​   Status quo. For a student whose enrolled campus, group, and grade match a row, they are
        assigned to that row's destination, in the same group.
  2.​   Scenario. The same immersion feeder pattern lookup on the scenario school and group,
        with one additional condition: the destination must still have the strand one grade up in that



               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 47
        scenario. If the scenario moved or closed the program at the destination, the student is not
        assigned there.
  3.​   Program moved by the scenario. If the destination's strand was fully moved by the
        workbook at next year's grade, the student follows it to the workbook's named destination,
        in the workbook's group.
  4.​   Strand relocated by the scenario. If the scenario closed or moved the student's own
        school, so that the scenario school matches no feeder row, the student still follows the feeder
        pattern of the school attended today, provided they remain in the strand and the destination
        still has that strand one grade up. A strand dissolved into the neighborhood program is
        excluded and goes through the neighborhood rules instead.
  5.​   Strand at the student's own school moved at the next grade. A K-8 strand whose 6-8
        portion is relocated in the scenario is covered by none of the above. The school's scenario
        gradespan stops at grade 5, a K-8 has no feeder row, and any feeder destination that a
        merged-in strand brought with it may also have moved. If the workbook fully moves the
        strand at the student's own scenario school at next year's grade, the student follows it to the
        workbook's destination. This is step 3 applied to the school itself rather than to a feeder
        destination.
The feeder table is checked against the roster on every run source row that matches no students, or a
destination with nobody in that group one grade up today, is reported. Several corrections to the table, and
the fifth step above, came from those checks and from the comparison scripts in section 8.

6.5 If a Spanish Immersion program has no one-to-one feeder HS
At grade 8 a student goes to the Spanish Immersion program serving their high school attendance area,
which a crosswalk maps from the HS boundary school to the HS that houses the program (Appendix C).
The crosswalk was read off the roster as the most common high school attended by current 10th-grade
Spanish Immersion students in each attendance area. The status-quo side keys on the student's HS living
school under the 2027-2028 set; the scenario side keys on the HS boundary school of the student's block
under the scenario, with the same "program still there" condition as the feeders.

6.6 Neighborhood transitions
Everyone at a transition grade who is not in an immersion strand goes to the school serving their address
at the next level, as a resident: 5th graders to the middle school, 8th graders to the high school. The
status-quo model reads the roster's living school at that level under the 2027-2028 set; the scenario model
reads the block's school at that level under the scenario, taken from the GIS layer at a fixed anchor grade
(grade 7 for middle school, grade 10 for high school) rather than the student's own grade.
This rule includes non-residents because a transfer does not carry to the next level, as well as
special-program students whose program ends at the transition grade. A student with no living school
(out-of-district or undetermined address) has nowhere to fall back to and is left unplaced on that model.

6.7 The change flag
For every K-11 student, NY_change is 1 when the scenario's next-year school differs from the status
quo's, and 0 when they are the same. A student whose next-year school is unknown under both the status


               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 48
quo and the scenario is not a move and is marked 0. A student placed on one side and not the other is 1, so
that anything the rules could not resolve stays visible as a difference rather than disappearing into the
unchanged. The flag compares schools only; a student who stays in the same building in a different group
is 0.


7. What the model does not do
  •​    Forecast. It does not show cohort-survival ratios, in-migration, attrition, or new
        kindergarteners. Every count is a subset of today's students.
  •​    Split students. A 50/50 workbook split is modeled as 100% to the plurality destination. For
        residents this rarely matters because they follow the GIS block; for programs it means a split
        strand is shown entirely at one school.
  •​    Predict individual transfers. Lottery programs, hardship transfers, special education
        placements and similar are not predicted. They are treated identically on both status quo and
        scenario and so do not affect the comparison.
  •​    Move current high school students. Grades 9-12 are held at their current school by policy,
        so scenarios that redraw high school boundaries show movement only through next year's
        9th graders.
  •​    Place students with no address. Out-of-district and undetermined-address students at a
        transition grade have no living school to fall back to and are left unplaced on both sides,
        which the change flag treats as no change.
  •​    Place a dissolved strand next year. Where a scenario dissolves an immersion strand into
        neighborhood schools, its 5th and 8th graders go through the neighborhood rules as
        residents rather than following a program.

        PRC-modified forecasts, as a full outer join. The two are not expected to agree, since the
        forecast applies cohort survival and in-migration. What the comparison catches is a school
        or strand present on one side only, which is almost always a scenario defined differently in
        the workbook and the forecast sheet, or a school spelled differently. This is the check that
        surfaced a partial "neighborhood" row emptying a program, and a feeder row pointing at a
        middle school that no longer takes that strand's 6th graders.
  •​    This year against next year. Counts by school, group and grade, this year's assignment
        against the roll-forward, on both sides. In a steady-state school the two agree grade for
        grade; the entry grade of each level is where feeder and crosswalk problems show. A second
        result set lists every immersion student not simply carried forward in place, with the rule
        that placed them on each side.




               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 50
  •​    Drill-down. For a school and scenario: a name audit across all schools, the workbook and
        successor rows that mention the school, where its current students went and by which rule,
        and who lands there next year.
  •​    Feeder patterns by scenario. For every immersion strand, source school and transition
        grade: where the students go next year under the status quo and under each scenario, with
        counts, and a column naming the scenarios whose pattern differs from the status quo. Read
        from the published table rather than from the feeder definition, so it shows what the rules
        did, including an unplaced strand. This is the view that surfaced the fifth feeder step.

8.3 Regression check
Before and after any change to the procedure, the published table is copied and compared with an
EXCEPT in both directions on student, scenario, school, and group. A change that should only affect one
rule, one scenario, or one feeder row is expected to show exactly that, and nothing else.

10. Summarizing the roll-forward
The roll-forward is incomplete. Next year's lottery results, special education placements, and hardship
placements are not known when it runs, so the moves they will make are not in it (section 7). Within that
limit it is faithful to the data PPS has: every placement follows a recorded rule, and the status quo and the
scenarios are built from the same students in the same way.
It measures who is affected, not school composition. The demographic composition of schools under a
scenario, as shown on the demographic pages, comes from this year's assignment (section 5), which
places every current student as though the scenario took effect all at once. The roll-forward is for counting
the students a scenario affects next year, overall, and by demographic group. It should not be read as any
school's composition, or school by school. Transfers it cannot predict will move students in and out of
individual schools.
The next-year results are reported as one measure, the share of continuing students the scenario moves:
the sum of NY_change over the number of K-11 students, within whatever group the table is cut by. It is a
share of students whose next-year school differs between the scenario and the status quo, built the same
way on both model outputs (section 4). Students unplaced on both model outputs count as unmoved;
students placed on one model output only count as moved. The procedure returns three summaries as its
last three result sets, each a grid with the scenarios as columns, built from the same student rows it has
just published, so the summaries always describe the run that produced them.

By high school region
Schools are grouped into four regions named for their high school pairs (Cleveland/Franklin, Ida B.
Wells-Barnett/Lincoln, Grant/McDaniel, Jefferson/Roosevelt) plus Other. Each school is placed in the
region where the plurality of the students it serves as a boundary school live, counted across the
elementary, middle, and high school boundary columns of the roster under the 2027 set. Focus options
and other non-boundary schools (da Vinci, Odyssey, Winterhaven, Richmond, MLC, ACCESS) are
placed by hand. A student's region is the region of their status-quo next-year campus, so a 5th grader is
counted in the region of the middle school they would attend next year under the status quo, not of the
elementary school they are leaving.

By demographic group
Four metrics, each a partition of all students: direct certification for free meals (certified or not), special
education (IEP or not), limited English proficiency (LEP or not), and race and ethnicity as recorded by the
district, with a missing value reported as Unknown. An “All students” row carries the district rate, which
is the baseline each group is read against. The question these tables answer is disproportionality: whether
a scenario moves one group at a higher rate than it moves students overall.




               K–8 Rightsizing & Consolidation Scenario Modeling: Technical Documentation | Page 52
By demographic group within region
The two summaries combined, with an All regions block and an All students row in each region, so any
cell can be read against its region's overall rate and against the district's rate for that group. This is the
table that distinguishes a scenario that moves one region heavily from one that moves one group heavily
within a region.

Conventions shared by all three
  •​    Scenario columns are built from the scenarios in the run; nothing in the procedure names
        one. The @SummaryScenarios parameter selects which appear, in what order, and under
        what presentation name, such as Scenario A.
  •​    Rates for groups smaller than the @SummaryMinCell parameter (default ten) are
        suppressed, with the group size still shown so the reader can see the group exists and why
        the rate is blank.
  •​    Each set describes one school year and leads with it; a two-year run returns a set per year
        rather than a blend. The workbook that carries them records the year in its title.
  •​    The region definitions, the high school pairs and the hand-placed non-boundary schools, are
        tables inside the procedure, so a change to the regions is made in one place and applies to
        every summary.
  •​    Alongside the three rate tables, the feeder-pattern grid (section 8.2) is the companion view
        for immersion: for each strand and transition, the school the students go to next year under
        the status quo and under each scenario.


11. Running the model
  •​    Load the scenario workbook and the Closed School Successors tab (Python loaders), and
        draw or update the GIS boundary scenario with the Enrollment Balancing Toolbox. The
        scenario name must match exactly between the workbook and the GIS layer.
  •​    Run the procedure. By default it models the latest roster year; it can be asked to model the
        preceding year in the same call. The first run after a change is made with validation set to
        report rather than block, so the result sets can be inspected.
  •​    Read result set 1 (problems) and result set 2 (rule distribution and change since the last run)
        before using the output. A rule that suddenly places thousands more students, or drops to
        zero, is the signal that something moved.
  •​    Result sets 3 to 5 are the change summaries. Pass @SummaryScenarios to choose and label
        the scenarios they show, and copy them into the summary workbook for that year.
  •​    When the feeder table or crosswalk changes, rerun the this-year-against-next-year
        comparison for the affected strand.

Part 6. Floors, Capacity and Utilization
Each scenario is tested school by school against two limits, (1) an enrollment floor, tested against
predicted 2031-2032 scenario enrollment, (2) the building’s functional capacity, tested using each year's
predicted scenario enrollment.


1. The thresholds
School type                  Floor (minimum enrollment)          Ceiling

K–5                          360                                 Functional capacity

K–8                          500                                 Functional capacity

Middle school (6–8)          500                                 Functional capacity

High school (9–12)           1,100                               Functional capacity

Floors are minimum student enrollments, not targets. A school may sit below its floor or above its
capacity only temporarily, or where a scenario deliberately limits how many schools close. In scenarios
with fewer consolidations, not every school reaches an enrollment above its floor. Functional capacity is
the number of students a building can reasonably serve, based on the district’s school utilization model.


2. How the tests are applied
  •​    School type comes from the modeled grades. For each scenario, school, and year, the school
        type used is identified based on grade levels served in the scenario. A K–8 that a scenario
        converts to K–5 is tested as a K–5 from the conversion year on.
  •​    The floor is tested in 2031-2032. Enrollment in other years is reported against the floor for
        information. The goal is to get to sustainable school sizes in five years.
  •​    Capacity is tested in each forecast year, 2027-2028 through 2035-2036. A school with no
        functional capacity on file receives the floor test only. Testing each year helps ensure there is not
        overcrowding as PPS transitions to sustainable school sizes.
  •​    Anything that cannot be tested is reported as an error, not passed, such as a grade span that
        the test does not recognize, or a group of students still enrolled at a school the scenario closes.
  •​    Utilization is modeled enrollment divided by functional capacity. This is computed for PRC’s
        baseline and for each scenario (Part 2, Section 4).

3. Outputs
The Floor Ceiling Detail table lists every school, scenario and year with its school type, enrollment, floor,
functional capacity, utilization, seats available, and status. The Floor Ceiling Summary gives one row per
school and scenario. Each row shows the school’s type and enrollment in 2031-2032, whether scenario
enrollment falls between its floor and its capacity, the result of the floor test, whether enrollment stays
within capacity every year, the school’s peak enrollment and the year of the peak, and the year the school
closes if the scenario closes it.

2. Limits of the data
  •​    Census blocks are based on the 2010 geography. The walking distances are an ArcGIS
        origin–destination list generated in 2016. Both are adequate for comparing scenarios school by
        school and year by year. Neither is a parcel-level description (Part 4, Sections 2 and 6).
  •​    The residency table describes where students live today, over the three school years since the
        2023-2024 K–8 boundary change. It does not include housing that is planned but not yet built.
        PRC's forecast does account for new housing in each school's total, but when a scenario divides a
        school's forecast among receiving schools, it uses the shares of where students live today.
        Students expected from new housing are split as if they lived where current students do.
  •​    Students without a usable address are carried as Undetermined. These students have no
        block, so boundary counts and the student-level placement processes cannot assign them to a
        school. There are not many students without a usable address and their number is checked each
        year when we make October Enrollment reports.
  •​    Grade-level detail in a redistribution is an allocation, not a forecast. Grade band totals are
        reliable, but the grade a returning student is shown in is not (Part 3).
  •​    The two models answer different questions. The enrollment model applies percentage shares to
        PRC's forecast counts, so it can move part of a group. The demographic impact model places each
        of today's students at a single school and does not split groups. Their counts are not meant to
        match (Part 5, Section 1).
