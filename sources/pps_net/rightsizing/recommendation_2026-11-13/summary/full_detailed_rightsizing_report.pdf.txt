While this has been a challenging month, what has been clear is how connected our students and families are
to their schools. This recommendation is an important first step to stabilizing our schools across Portland Public
Schools and providing students with rich learning environments regardless of where they live. We will need to
regularly review how our resources are aligned with student needs. When we spread fewer resources across
fewer schools we make a larger impact. We will develop an annual process to review enrollment across the
system and determine if adjustments are needed. As part of the rightsizing process we established the
Superintendent’s Enrollment Advisory Committee made up of building principals and our union partners. This
committee will become a standing committee to continue this process and as we move to the implementation
phase. We will also monitor the following:

    ●​ Implementation of the changes approved by the board.
    ●​ Initiation of a systemwide review of middle schools in 2029-30.
    ●​ Initiation of a regional boundary review process, starting on the west side.

As part of my recommendation, I propose convening community advisory groups in each area where a school
building is recommended for closure. Beginning as early as January, these groups will explore future
building-use options and develop recommendations that reflect community priorities and needs.

Each group will forward me its recommendation, which I will bring to the Board at a future regular meeting for
consideration.

My goal is to ensure that the future of these buildings is shaped by the communities they have served.
Executive Summary
Portland Public Schools faces declining enrollment and a $65 million budget deficit. Enrollment fell 12%
between 2018-19 and 2025-26, with 89% of that decline in the elementary grades, and further losses are
forecast to move into the middle grades next. Fewer students spread across the same number of buildings
means more under-enrolled schools, thinner staffing and uneven access to programs. With no changes, fewer
than 20% of PPS schools would have sustainable enrollment by 2031-32.

The Superintendent recommends closing and consolidating 10 elementary schools, phased over two years
beginning in Fall 2027. The recommendation also:
    ●​ Converts seven K-8 schools to K-5, with grades 6-8 moving to nearby middle schools and one K-12
        school to a K-8 with students moving to another alternative high school.
    ●​ Consolidates nine Spanish Dual Language Immersion (DLI) elementary pathways into four regional hubs
        and Mandarin DLI from three pathways to two.
    ●​ Changes school boundaries and feeder patterns to reflect the new configurations.

The recommendation substantially increases the number of students, and especially focal students, attending
schools with enough enrollment to sustain stable, well-resourced programs.

How the Recommendation Was Shaped
The recommendation is guided by four principles adopted by the Board in Resolution 7307: well-resourced
schools, safe and healthy learning environments, educational options, and the PPS Equity Lens. It draws on
enrollment forecasts from Portland State University’s Population Research Center, facility capacity data and
historical enrollment patterns. It also reflects more than 3,400 survey responses, engagement sessions with
more than 1,000 participants across communities, and 35 Superintendent site visits.

Scenario A proposed 14 closures and Scenario B proposed 11. The final recommendation closes 10 schools,
while still bringing half of the elementary schools to sustainable enrollment. Changes that reflect concerns
raised by students, families, staff and other PPS communities, and further analysis include:

  ●​ Sellwood Middle School, James John, Maplewood and Stephenson will remain open.
  ●​ Sunnyside remains an environmental focus school, but converts to K-5.
  ●​ Skyline students will continue on to Lincoln High School rather than Roosevelt.
  ●​ The Odyssey K-8 choice program moves to co-locate with Forest Park instead of Metropolitan Learning
     Center.
  ●​ The Mandarin DLI program at Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. remains at its current location.
  ●​ Lewis, Marysville, Woodmere and Rose City Park will close in Fall 2028 instead of Fall 2027.


Financial Impact and Reinvestment
Consolidation captures an estimated 75 FTE in structural efficiencies that are not available if PPS keeps
operating the same number of buildings, while keeping class size ratios unchanged. Net recurring savings are
projected at about $10.2 million across both years. After one-time transition costs, about $7.1 million remains
to reinvest in receiving schools, transportation and transition supports, including 15-20 FTE reserved for schools
welcoming new students.


Implementation
The changes will be phased in over two years. PPS will carry out the Board's rightsizing decisions through
supported transitions and clear steps that deliver on the district's commitments to students, families and staff.
Implementation is centered on student wellbeing, belonging and learning. It supports school communities
through transition while the district expands student access to sustainable, well-resourced schools.

Implementation planning is already underway and will adjust to the Board’s final decision. Once the Board votes
in December, PPS will begin working with school communities on student supports and programming,
enrollment and transfers, staffing, and facilities and operations. Quarterly public reports will track progress,
surface lessons learned, and flag equity gaps so the district can adjust as the work continues. Annual
summative reports to the board will provide additional comprehensive learning.



                                                                                                                    2
Ongoing Review and Next Steps
PPS will review enrollment annually. The Superintendent's Enrollment Advisory Committee will become a
standing committee overseeing implementation, followed by a systemwide middle school review in 2029-30
and regional boundary reviews beginning on the west side. Starting as early as January 2027, community
advisory groups will recommend future uses for each closed building.

The Superintendent presents the recommendation to the Board on November 17. Listening sessions to follow at
Franklin High School (November 19) and Grant High School (December 3). In addition, a community
information session will be held at McDaniel High School (November 21) with the final Board vote on December
15, 2026.

Change is hard for school communities, and PPS does not take these decisions lightly. But these decisions have
been delayed long enough, and acting now puts the district on a more stable footing for every student.

equitable access to a robust and high-quality educational experience throughout their careers regardless of
where they live in our city. For the better part of a decade, declining enrollment has increased the number of
small schools that cannot provide the level of programming and supports students deserve within available
resources. The Portland State University Population Research Center (PRC) forecasts additional enrollment loss
over the next decade.

Superintendent to initiate a districtwide rightsizing process to consider school and program consolidations,
boundary adjustments, special program changes, and grade reconfigurations. Following a series of listening
sessions, the Board endorsed a set of guiding principles, timeline and deliverable requirements for the project
through Resolution 7307. The Rightsizing effort is aligned with the PPS Student Assignment to Neighborhood
Schools (4.10.045-P), Educational Options (6.10.022-P) and School Initiation and Closure (6.10.030-P) policies.

Rightsizing is a necessary mechanism for creating more stable and predictable school enrollment, which is a
critical component in assuring equitable access to the robust programming and individualized supports students
need to thrive. We know this work carries real impact. The enrollment declines through 2025-26, while
currently concentrated in the elementary grades, will continue impacting schools in every part of our city.
Eighty-nine percent of the enrollment decline since 2018-19 has been felt in the elementary schools. The
leading edge of this decline will enter the middle grades in the next six years accounting for 43% decrease.
Because middle schools only have three grades, the impacts will sweep through swiftly and dramatically,
leaving the vast majority of middle schools with low enrollment. For K-8s which support students in both grade
bands the declines are persistent and leave schools with low enrollment in both the elementary and middle
grade bands. High school enrollment stays fairly stable through 2031-32, so the scenarios are designed to
stabilize elementary, K-8 and middle schools.



Approach
PPS developed two scenarios and the final recommendation guided by a set of criteria that reflect what the
district is trying to achieve: schools large enough to offer stable, adequately resourced programming, buildings
that are used well, and school communities that stay together through change as much as possible. In addition,
throughout the development process PPS looked at the changes through our equity lens which considers who is
impacted by the proposals attending particularly to students who are most underserved in our system.

PPS modeled multiple options and used the KPIs to examine whether each configuration would create stronger,
comparable or more challenging conditions for students. The analysis considered:
    ●​ Building utilization rates and functional capacity;
    ●​ Existing conditions at both sending and receiving schools; including how they affect safe and reliable
       operations;
    ●​ The number of students affected by changes in those conditions;
    ●​ Trade-offs across indicators rather than relying on a single indicator or data point;
    ●​ Improvements or mitigation needed for a receiving school to successfully serve additional students;
    ●​ Equity impacts across all indicators, including whether particular student groups experience
       disproportionate benefits or burdens.


Community Voice
Community voice shaped Rightsizing from the start. It informed the Guiding Principles and the KPIs used to
evaluate every option, and it directly shaped the Superintendent’s Recommendation.

After Scenarios A and B were released, PPS gathered input in two complementary ways.

Community feedback survey: Offered in all PPS-supported languages, the survey received more than 3,400
responses from parents and guardians, staff, students and community members across all school communities.
Respondents rated the scenarios against the Guiding Principles, weighed tradeoffs and described the support
their school communities would need.

Community engagement sessions: From October 6-29, PPS heard from more than 1,000 people in smaller

    ●​ Village Table: A districtwide family and community advisory group.
    ●​ Community Voice conversations: Dedicated sessions for Native, Black/African American and Latino/a/e

    ●​ School Impact Sessions: Regional sessions in North, Southeast and West Portland for the school

    ●​ Board Community Connections: Open sessions, in-person and virtual, where community members

    ●​ Educator Town Halls: Dedicated sessions for PPS educators.
    ●​ Student voice: Input from student leadership groups, including the District Student Council, Student

Key Themes & Overall Learnings from Community Engagement
Across all engagement formats, participants expressed a shared recognition that the district faces real structural
challenges and that status quo operations are unsustainable. However, community members consistently
emphasized that how the district approaches these changes is just as critical as the changes themselves. Eight
themes emerged from the sessions:

    ●​ Neighborhood preservation with closures as a last resort: Families and educators view neighborhood
       schools as community anchors. While acknowledging the need for action, participants urged the district
       to exhaust other operational and boundary adjustments before considering school closures.
    ●​ Need for operational clarity and implementation capacity: Communities requested clear, concrete
       details regarding the day-to-day student experience, school staffing models, and logistics. Many
       expressed concern about the district's administrative capacity to execute large-scale, simultaneous
       transitions effectively.
    ●​ Protection of Special Education and transition supports: Families and staff emphasized that students
       receiving special education services require robust, individualized transition plans and guaranteed
       continuity of support throughout any structural changes.
    ●​ Equitable access to Spanish Dual Language Immersion (DLI): Significant concern was raised regarding
       the potential consolidation of Spanish DLI programs and the risk of reducing access for historically
       underserved students.
    ●​ Transportation and geographic realities: Participants highlighted transportation as a major barrier to
       consolidation, specifically pointing to the unique topography, traffic bottlenecks, and limited transit

                                                                                                                   13
       infrastructure on Portland’s west side. Additionally, many east side families were concerned about
       losing walkable and bikeable commutes to school.
    ●​ Divergent views on grade configuration (K–8 vs. middle school): Feedback reflected mixed community
       priorities regarding school models; while many families deeply value the stability and tight-knit
       environment of K–8 schools, others welcome the expanded academic, elective, and extracurricular
       opportunities offered by comprehensive middle schools.
    ●​ Skepticism toward data used by the district: Several participants questioned the accuracy,
       transparency, and framing of the enrollment and capacity data used to justify proposed changes, calling
       for clearer methodologies to rebuild trust.

Options
The guiding principles criteria often pull in different directions. Keeping a school community together may
require a larger boundary change. Consolidating a small immersion program may lengthen some students' trips.
For that reason, the criteria were weighed against one another rather than applied as a fixed ranking, and the
balance struck differs from one part of the city to another, because conditions differ. The scenarios and final
recommendation varied mostly on which schools would close and phasing the implementation over two years.

A comparison of current and projected enrollment to the minimum enrollment thresholds led to a focus on K-5,
K-8 and middle schools, where enrollment decline has been most pervasive and the majority of schools do not
meet the standard.

High school changes in both options were limited to feeder pattern and boundary shifts that created stronger
K-12 continuity. While declining enrollment is expected to impact high schools in the coming years, almost all
high schools are forecast to remain about the 1,100 student standard through 2031-32.


Scenarios A and B
The guiding question for scenario development: How can we make sure more students attend schools with
enough enrollment to sustain stable, well-resourced programs, while also using our buildings efficiently?

On October 6, 2026, two options were presented for consideration. Both scenarios sought to stabilize
enrollment, strengthen academic program pathways, and align the district’s use of school facilities with student
needs. They combined school consolidations, grade reconfigurations, program moves and boundary changes to
achieve these outcomes. While school consolidations represented the most intense level of change, analysis
showed that higher levels of enrollment stability, particularly for focal student groups, could not be achieved
through boundary change and program/grade level reconfigurations alone.


                                                                                                                 14
Scenario A proposed 14 school closures, while Scenario B proposed 11. The primary differences concerned
Stephenson, Rose City Park, and Lewis, which would have closed under Scenario A and remained open under
Scenario B. Scenario B would have affected fewer families and changed fewer boundaries, while leaving 43% of
schools below the enrollment needed for stable, sustainable programming. Scenario A would have involved
more transitions and boundary changes while bringing 67% of schools to sustainable enrollment levels.

District leaders and planning staff developed the two proposed scenarios using criteria focused on enrollment
sufficient to sustain stable, adequately resourced programming, effective use of buildings, and keeping school
communities together as much as possible.

Recommendation
After gathering feedback from 44 engagement sessions and 35 superintendent school/community site visits
during the fall of 2026, further analysis, and site reviews, PPS developed the Superintendent’s recommendation.
The set of proposed changes were measured against the overarching goal of improving the educational
experience for students across our system. This requires stabilizing staffing and resources at as many schools as
possible while also being realistic about our capacity to successfully navigate the changes. These proposed
changes will impact students, educators, and communities across our system - shaping who they connect and
build relationships with, how they travel through our city, and the opportunities for students to grow and thrive.
These changes bring us closer to realizing our vision of schools that develop compassionate critical thinkers able
to collaborate and solve problems, and prepared to lead a more socially just world.

The recommendation that follows incorporates the fundamental structure found in both scenarios A and B
while also being responsive to the feedback we heard from communities. As we considered adjustments, we
returned to the guiding principles and the key performance indicators for comparison to the previous scenarios.
We balanced this with considerations about the magnitude of change which drives how soon we will have to
review enrollment processes or make adjustments in the future, how many school communities are affected,
how many students and educators will be asked to move to a different building where they will forge new
relationships. The complexity of the changes where we assess what supports, resources and structures are at
each site students will be leaving as well as the community that will receive the students, educators and
families. Finally, we also looked at the intensity of the changes that a site will navigate. These considerations
helped us determine readiness for the change as well as plan for the intentional and multi-year supports that
will be needed to help students and families through this transition. While we have a sense of urgency as we
see schools struggling to maintain and sustain robust educational experiences for our students, we also need to
handle these decisions with care.

While the goal was to keep as many students together as possible through these moves, we have not been able
to keep whole school communities together in all cases. PPS moved whole attendance areas where possible.
We heard this concern from families and the call to acknowledge that school communities are not defined by
attendance areas, they are built over time and embody the variety of experiences at each school site. They
expressed concern about school programs being split from their neighborhood peers when dual language
immersion programs or special education focus classrooms moved to different schools than their peers.

The changes outlined in this recommendation are phased over a two year period, beginning in the fall of 2027.
The changes in the first year are contingent on each other and also reflect the significant enrollment decline.
The recommendation includes changes primarily from Scenario A while applying the magnitude of Scenario B.




                                                                                                               22
The recommendation consolidates resources through school closures, program consolidations, and grade
reconfigurations. PPS has schools that are underenrolled at both the elementary and middle grades and this
recommendation focuses primarily on those grade levels. School closures occur only at the elementary level
and are designed to stabilize the enrollment at the receiving schools as well as within the region. This step is
necessary because balanced school enrollment could not be achieved through boundary and program moves
alone.

Dual Language Immersion program consolidations are necessary because the resources are spread across too
many elementary schools. PPS values dual language immersion and takes pride in providing programs in five
different languages. PPS is committed to supporting students in becoming bilingual and learning in rich
bicultural settings. Co-located dual language immersion programs are difficult to sustain when they are in
elementaries that have less than two strands at each grade. This leads to an imbalance between the dual
language immersion and English scholar programs. In recent years we have seen an increase in the number of
grade level blends in one program or the other. We are also seeing the impact of difficult decisions that
principals of co-located programs are forced to make about how to split limited building resources (e.g. ELD
support, instructional coaching, academic intervention, professional learning time, etc) across two instructional
programs.

Japanese, Russian and Vietnamese dual language immersion programs will remain open across the district and
are each available at one elementary school. Mandarin Chinese Dual Language Immersion has three available
elementary pathways which will be consolidated over two years to two, one each in north and southeast
Portland. For Spanish Dual Language Immersion the recommendation moves from nine elementaries to four -
one in each region. In north Portland students will access Spanish Dual Language immersion at César Chávez
K-5, in northeast at Rigler K-5, in southeast at Lent K-5 and in the west at Ainsworth K-5. This will allow us to
maintain the research-based 90:10 language allocation model, and will move us closer to the recommended
program composition of one third native language speakers, one third heritage language speakers, and one
third native English speakers in the program. The total number of seats available in each Dual Language
program will remain unchanged.

We heard concerns from families about transportation to the whole school dual language immersion sites not
only for students, but also for caregivers. To mitigate transportation concerns PPS is developing hub bus routes
that will be available for students who are in the program. While in our current model, DLI transportation is
available only to Native and Heritage speakers, the hub bus model will allow us to offer transportation to all
students enrolled in DLI programs. See the Sample Transportation Routes document in the appendix for sample
travel times for the routes.

In addition, bilingual community agents will work with each school to support families in the transition and to
support outreach to families with kindergarten age students to raise awareness of the DLI opportunity. Native
speakers will be able to go directly to their program to register, removing the lottery application barrier for
families. We heard concerns that we assumed too many students would follow the program to the new site. In
response, we lowered our assumption and provided a range of students who may move. PPS will continue to
monitor the enrollment in each dual language elementary school and the program from kindergarten through

                                                                                                                   23
graduation so that students can flourish in language and cultural rich environments that thrive for years to
come.

The recommendation also converts seven K-8 schools to elementaries. Middle grade students from these
schools will enroll in adjacent middle schools. These changes provide students with access to more stable and
broader academic programming including multiple year elective pathways in the arts or STEM and pre-ninth
grade credit in a world language and in Algebra 1-2. These changes also increase enrollment at the middle
schools, buffering them to some degree from the enrollment decline that is working its way through the system.
These changes also decrease the need to subsidize middle grades programs at K-8s. The K-8s that remain open
are in choice programs, geographically isolated, or are in a region that needs additional middle grades seats
because of a limited number of middle schools in the area.

Families of students with disabilities provided important input during engagement sessions. They emphasize the
critical need to protect dedicated support staff, specialized sensory spaces, and access to smaller learning
environments. They request transparent, equitable planning that guarantees day-one service continuity and
prevents disproportionate harm to students with intersectionality in addition to disability during any potential
school transition.

With the proposed recommendation, there is no decrease in specialized programming, Learning Center
programming, or related services for students with disabilities. Currently, the average Learning Center teacher
caseload is 26 students per FTE. Projections for 2027-28 show an average Learning Center teacher caseload of
21. Focus programming shows a similar trend. The average Social Emotional Skills (SES) case manager caseload
is currently 10 per FTE. Communication Behavior (CB) caseload is 10.5, with Intensive Skills (ISC) at nine.
Projections indicate the average teacher caseload per allocated FTE in SES to be nine, CB to be eight and ISC to
be 7.5. Current data indicates we will maintain, and in some cases slightly improve, caseload ratios in the
2027-28 school year. Student to staff ratios for related service providers, such as school psychologists and
speech and language therapists will be unchanged in the Rightsizing process. All of these supports will continue
to meet the service requirements of each IEP.

It is important to note that Special Education data represents actual students and staff and is in constant flux as
students move in and out of Special Education or PPS. Every year over 1,000 students who receive special
education services transfer in or out of PPS, or are determined by their IEP team to require special education
services in a specialized classroom located outside of their neighborhood school. These transitions are required
to ensure a continuum of services.

We have heard concerns about the possibility of disproportionate impact on students with disabilities as well as
other focal student groups. We acknowledge that additional transitions and changes in programming can have a
higher impact on students with disabilities. However, because every student with a disability first and foremost
is a general education student, they also participate in the benefits provided by Rightsizing. Currently in the K-8
grade band, there are 56 Specialized Program classrooms. Of those, 36 will experience no direct impact from
Rightsizing. Of the 20 impacted, nine of them will move to a new school. The remaining 11 will receive students


                                                                                                                 24
from another school or program. That means 16% of our Specialized Programs in K-8 will transition to a new
location. While that impact is not to be minimalized, it is proportionate to the overall impact in our system.

PPS strives to keep students in specialized programming with their general education peers and within a school
closest to their neighborhood. Each of the schools impacted by Rightsizing has been assessed for adequate
space. When considering needed classroom spaces, counts included designated spaces for sensory/regulation,
classroom spaces, specialized program spaces, and office spaces. Each site in the recommendation has a
verified amount of space for special education programming.

In response to concerns raised about middle school programming, PPS will add two CB classes in fall 2027. One
site for CB programming will be Hosford Middle School, which also receives middle school students from
Sunnyside. While student IEPs do not include school size as an accommodation, because school size is part of
the general education program rather than a special education service, we acknowledge some students may
need a different model within their special education services in support of their neurodivergence.

Our parent community has asked important questions about data, impact, and future programming.
Additionally, there is a strong focus on making thoughtful transition decisions for each student who will make a
transition. The processes that are typically used to transition students with disabilities between school sites,
whether due to grade changes or programming changes, must also be carefully applied here. Teams will be
expected to make individualized transition plans for students and meet with receiving school colleagues and
families to develop thoughtful plans. Students who require additional service or support for a transition will
have that designated through the IEP process.

Equity Impact Statement
Making no changes leaves less than 20% of PPS schools above a sustainable threshold. (See Schools Above the
Threshold table above). The final recommendation more than doubles that for every focal group in elementary
and middle schools (see Focal Students Above the Threshold table above). Of the three scenarios, the final
recommendation provides significant gains for focal students while closing the fewest schools, no changes does
far less for them than the final recommendation while closing none.

While there are benefits for students with this change, there is also the burden of managing change and the
grief that comes with disrupting communities. Multilingual learners carry more of this change than any other
group (see Focal Students Who Move Due to Changes table above) while also moving to more stable school
sites (see Focal Students in Schools Above the Threshold table abovec). While multilingual learners are
overrepresented in the percentage of students who move, they ultimately end up as the highest percentage of
students in sustainable schools overall. As enrollment grows at receiving schools, the total number of students
receiving special education services may rise—but in most cases, their overall share of the student body
remains steady.

The Ida B. Wells/Lincoln region contributes no closures and has the fewest schools at sustainable enrollment
levels.

The families least represented in the community feedback are the families most affected by the largest program
change in the package. Multilingual families are about one in eight students and about one in 50 survey
respondents.

The recommendation advances educational equity relative to the status quo for every focal student group, by a
substantial margin. It does not distribute the burden of change evenly. As we move into future years and
implementation, if this recommendation is adopted, then we will need to continue to look back to ask if the
disruption has provided sufficient benefits for the populations impacted.

We recognize that this disruption is significant for all students. We commit to adding a field in Synergy for
students who are moved because of this recommendation and providing future analysis of academic and

attendance impacts with this disaggregation. The analysis supports moving forward on the condition that the
commitments are funded, owned and monitored rather than described.

Without those commitments, this is an enrollment plan with an equity result. With them, it is an equity plan.


Financial Impact Rationale
The primary financial benefit of consolidating shrinking schools and programs is to align physical facility capacity
and annual operating costs with projected student enrollment, allowing the district to concentrate limited
operational resources in fewer, fully resourced campuses. Combining school communities eliminates duplicative
school-level administrative overhead, improves overall district building utilization, and stabilizes
enrollment-based staffing models.

While initial one-time transition expenses—including equipment relocation, receiving school facility
adjustments, and ongoing minimum building security/utility maintenance—will be incurred during
implementation, the consolidation produces substantial net recurring savings. These net annual savings are
directly reinvested into receiving campuses, transportation costs, and transition supports.

Methodology & Important Caveats
The school-level projections in this section are preliminary planning estimates and will be updated following
the release of Portland State University’s revised enrollment forecasts in January. These figures are intended
strictly for comparative modeling and should not be interpreted as final school staffing allocations.

To provide an apples-to-apples comparison, both scenarios model the impact of applying identical baseline
reduction criteria across the district, reflecting a gross systemwide reduction of 277.5 FTE under the status quo
and 308.5 FTE under the final recommendation before add-backs and reinvestments are applied. The stated
target is 223 FTE to address our projected $65 million budget deficit. Both items are over reduced to allow for
the opportunity for some reinvestment. Both scenarios require an additional 20 - 30 FTE for program balancing
for unforeseen issues that arise in the spring and fall.

How Systemwide Reductions Are Modeled
The table below breaks down how the district reaches its required budget savings across the status quo and the
final recommendation. Rather than representing arbitrary cuts, the model illustrates how consolidating

                                                                                                                  50
under-enrolled sites unlocks structural efficiencies, allowing the district to protect core classroom instruction
and reinvest directly into student programs. In both models, class size ratios in all grades remain the same,
including blended classrooms in grades 1 and 2 as well as grades 4 and 5. In order to achieve the necessary
savings significant reductions are necessary and they fall into three categories:

    ●​ Enrollment & Recommendation Reductions
           ○​ Enrollment (-40.4 FTE): Reflects standard staffing adjustments tied to projected districtwide
                enrollment decline across both models.
           ○​ Scenario Consolidation (~-75 FTE): Under the status quo, the district captures 0 FTE in structural
                savings because it continues operating the same number of school buildings. By consolidating
                under-enrolled sites, the final recommendation captures about 75 FTE in programmatic
                efficiencies and shifts 10.5 FTE in Principal and Administrative Assistant positions into direct
                building operational savings as well as 8.5 FTE back to Title 1 K-5 receiving schools to work
                towards less blends in those schools
    ●​ Class Size Ratios
           ○​ Class Size ratios were not adjusted in either Status Quo nor in the recommendation.
           ○​ Blends were not adjusted in either the Status Quo nor in the recommendation, except
                potentially in the case of middle grades students in K-8s and neighborhood/DLI blends in
                co-located programs.

Receiving School Reinvestment (15–20 FTE): Out of the 22 FTE in programmatic reinvestment unlocked by the
Recommendation, 15 to 20 FTE is reserved specifically to reinvest in schools welcoming students from
consolidated sites, supporting smooth student transitions, manageable class sizes, and expanded opportunities.

Transportation Impacts
PPS supports multiple ways for students to travel to school, including walking, biking, parent or family
transportation, and PPS-provided school bus service for eligible students. Elementary students who live more
than one mile from their neighborhood school and middle school students who live more than 1.5 miles from
their neighborhood school are provided bus service. Other students may qualify based on Oregon Department
of Education guidelines or individual needs. Bus stops and routes are planned around student proximity, safety,
ride time, and anticipated ridership. Although PPS is not generally required to provide transportation to
focus-option programs, including Dual Language Immersion (DLI), the district has prioritized access through a
hub transportation model. This model connects students through designated regional pickup locations,
supporting access for families across a broader geographic area, including the many native-speaking students
these programs serve.

DLI students would have increased access to bus service through strategically designed regional hub routes,
with estimated one-way route times averaging under 30 minutes for all languages except Japanese, on the east
side of the river. Routes on the east side range from 18 minutes for regional routes to 43 minutes for west to
east routes. Westside and Japanese routes average around 35 minutes with the longest route running 58
minutes. Overall, PPS-provided service is estimated to increase to 37 elementary school DLI routes and 29
middle school DLI routes. Efficiencies gained through consolidating and coordinating routes would offset some
additional service needs, resulting in an estimated net annual transportation cost increase of $3 million. See
attachment for more information.

Bus routes from this recommendation increase from 122 current routes to an estimated 153 routes. Currently
there are more middle school routes than elementary, in the recommendation middle school routes exceed
elementary routes. The cost for the additional 31 routes is estimated to be approximately $2.7 million.

Implementation Framework
The Rightsizing Implementation Framework guides how Portland Public Schools carries out the Board's
Rightsizing decisions. It connects the district's commitments to students, families, and staff with the supported
transitions and clear steps required to deliver on them.

The framework is centered on student wellbeing, belonging, and learning. It supports school communities
through transition while the district expands student access to sustainable, well-resourced schools.


Purpose
The framework makes sure Rightsizing decisions are implemented with care, coherence, and accountability.
Specifically, it:
    ●​ Translates Board decisions into action, defining what the district will do to support and transition
         impacted school communities for a successful start.
    ●​ Guides project planning, priorities, and governance, setting the structure needed to deliver the work.
While the framework informs a multiyear implementation plan, the initial scope focuses on the first year of
implementation. The plan will continue to be built out, informed by lessons learned, to support schools
transitioning in future years.


Structure
The framework is organized into five components, the essential elements of implementation. Together, they
shape the plan to transition schools and establish the district supports needed to carry out the Board's final
Rightsizing decisions:
    ●​ Successful Transitions
    ●​ Student Supports & Programming
    ●​ Enrollment & Transfer
    ●​ Staffing & Staff Supports
    ●​ Facilities & Operations

The framework is delivered through three layers of information, each designed for a different audience:
    ●​ Rightsizing Transition Guide – The primary public resource for staff, families, and the community,
        providing information to navigate a complex process and be ready for school and learning.
    ●​ School-specific and functional guides – More detailed information tailored to individual school
        communities and to specific areas of the work.
    ●​ Internal project management – The detailed technical information district teams need to carry out the
        work.




                                                                                                                 56
Each component has a designated workstream, with clear governance and accountability structures that set
how the work is led, coordinated, and monitored.


Framework Components
Successful Transitions
Schools hold memories, friendships, traditions, and a sense of community. For many students, families, and
staff, these changes will bring a sense of loss and uncertainty alongside hope for what comes next. Successful
transitions start from that understanding. It is how PPS will support people through the transition, honor school
communities, and help students feel safe, known, and connected in their new school.

This includes maintaining social emotional supports and trusted relationships for students, honoring each
school’s legacy and traditions, and creating opportunities for families, students, and staff to connect as a new
community. Families, students, and staff will help shape this work, with school and district leads providing
opportunities and supports for families to connect. District resources will include mental health supports, family
information and events, joint gatherings such as school tours and PTA meetings, and preparation for school
teams, all aimed at building belonging over time.

Student Supports and Programming
When schools merge or change, families want to know that learning, and the services their students rely on, will
carry forward. In every PPS classroom, we aim to provide rigorous, high-quality learning experiences that are
inclusive and joyful, and to disrupt racial inequities so every student has the opportunity to demonstrate
excellence. Student Supports & Programming focuses on how PPS intends to maintain and strengthen that
commitment through the transition.

This work spans the school day and beyond: general academics, social emotional learning, multilingual
programs including Dual Language Immersion, Special Education, middle school transitions, early learning,
curricular resources and libraries, and partnerships and programs such as SUN, childcare, and athletics. Initial
plans for each area focus on continuity for students and clear information for families, and will be refined as
school-level planning moves forward.

Staffing and Staff Supports
Staffing and Staff Supports addresses how PPS will support employees through school closures, program moves,
and grade reconfigurations. It focuses on mental health and employee supports, labor agreements and staffing
processes, communication plans, and estimated timelines.

PPS recognizes that familiar educators, support staff, and administrators help students feel known, seen, and
ready to learn. In collaboration with all labor partners, PPS will honor contractual obligations and applicable
bargaining requirements as staffing transitions are planned and carried out, and will look for opportunities to
keep familiar adults with students from each impacted community within those agreements.

Facilities and Operations
Facilities and Operations addresses how PPS will prepare receiving schools so students and staff are ready for
learning. It coordinates moving and readiness across facility and operational areas, including technology,

transportation, security, nutrition services, and academic and shared spaces. School readiness plans guide space
planning, facility improvements, and a timeline of what to expect.

It also includes a building closure and repurposing plan for schools that are closing. Support will continue after
schools open to help with final adjustments.



Implementation Evaluation Plan

The Rightsizing Implementation Evaluation Plan will monitor, evaluate, and learn from PPS's Rightsizing and
school transition process. A Culturally Responsive Evaluation (CRE) framework will actively involve impacted
communities in contextualizing/interpreting outcomes and shaping recommendations.

The evaluation will be both formative and summative. Formative evaluation will be reported on a quarterly
basis and will monitor progress in real time, showing the relationship between what we said vs. what has been
done to date. These quarterly feedback loops will identify implementation bottlenecks, equity gaps, and
operational friction early enough for course corrections.

A comprehensive end-of-year report will aggregate quarterly analyses to assess overall impact, measure
progress against district commitments, and uphold public accountability to the Board, families, and community.


Evaluation Methods
The evaluation will utilize a mixed-methods design combining quantitative and qualitative data and analysis.
Potential quantitative data under consideration includes attendance stability, enrollment retention, building
walk-through data, and survey scales. Potential qualitative data sources include listening sessions and student
focus group data.

All data will be disaggregated to ensure that rightsizing does not exacerbate systemic inequities.
